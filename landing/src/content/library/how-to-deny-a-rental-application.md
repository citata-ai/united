## Which reasons are legal to deny a rental application?

A legal denial reason is a business reason from your written criteria, applied the same way to every applicant. An illegal reason is any reason based on a protected class.

The federal Fair Housing Act, 42 U.S.C. 3604, makes it unlawful to refuse to rent because of race, color, religion, sex, familial status or national origin. Section 3604(f) adds disability. Many states and cities add more classes, such as source of income. Check yours.

| Usually legal (if in your written criteria) | Not legal |
|---|---|
| Income below your stated ratio | Race, color, national origin |
| Income you cannot verify | Religion |
| Unpaid rent owed to a past landlord | Sex |
| Eviction judgment within your lookback | Familial status (children, pregnancy) |
| False or incomplete application | Disability |
| Too many occupants under a local code | Any class your state or city protects |

Watch for: a neutral rule can still cause trouble if you apply it to some applicants and not others. Write your criteria before you list the unit. Give the same criteria to every applicant.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## Must I say why I denied the application?

You must give a specific notice when a consumer report played any part in the decision. This comes from the Fair Credit Reporting Act. The FTC's guide for landlords calls it an adverse action notice.

The FTC says adverse action includes:

- denying the application,
- requiring a co-signer,
- requiring a deposit that another applicant would not pay,
- charging higher rent than another applicant would pay.

The FTC lists what the notice must contain:

1. The name, address and phone number of the screening company that supplied the report.
2. A statement that the screening company did not make the decision and cannot give the reasons for it.
3. The applicant's right to dispute the accuracy or completeness of the report.
4. The applicant's right to a free report from the screening company, if asked within 60 days.

The FTC says the notice may be written, electronic or oral, and that written notice is the best practice. The notice is required even if the report played only a small part.

Some states ask for more. Colorado's HB19-1106 requires a landlord who denies an application to give written notice stating the reasons for the denial.

Here is a short, polite denial message you can adapt. Add the FCRA items above if you used a report.

> Thank you for applying for [address]. We are not able to approve your application. The decision is based on our written rental criteria, which we apply to every applicant. [Add the reason if your state requires it.] [Add the FCRA notice if a consumer report was used.] We wish you success in your search.

## Do I refund the application fee?

The answer depends on your state. Some states cap the fee, and some require a refund of any unused part. Here are 3 examples.

| State | Rule | Source |
|---|---|---|
| California | Fee capped at $30 per applicant, adjusted for inflation since 1998, and no more than actual costs. Return any unused part if no reference check or credit report was obtained. Give an itemized receipt, and a copy of the credit report within 7 days of receiving it. | Cal. Civ. Code 1950.6 |
| Colorado | Fee must cover only actual processing costs and be the same for all applicants. Make a good-faith refund of any unused part within 20 days. | HB19-1106 |
| New York | Background and credit check fees: no more than the actual cost or $20, whichever is less. Waive the fee if the applicant gives a check from the past 30 days. | N.Y. Real Prop. Law 238-a |

Here is the math for a California-style refund. The numbers are an example.

| Line | Example |
|---|---|
| Fee collected | $50 |
| Credit report cost | $0 (not ordered; you chose another applicant first) |
| Time spent checking references | $0 (not done) |
| Refund due | $50 |

If you collect fees from many applicants for one unit, process them in order. Do not take fees from more people than you will screen.

## How do I keep records of a denial?

Keep a full record of every denial for at least 2 years. A federal Fair Housing lawsuit can be filed up to 2 years after the act (42 U.S.C. 3613). A HUD complaint can be filed up to 1 year after (42 U.S.C. 3610). Your file is your proof that you followed your criteria.

Keep these items in each denial file:

- [ ] The written screening criteria that applied on that date
- [ ] The application, with date and time received
- [ ] Income and reference checks, with notes
- [ ] The screening report, stored securely
- [ ] The decision and the reason, tied to a line in your criteria
- [ ] A copy of the denial and adverse action notice, with the date sent
- [ ] Fee receipt and refund record

The FTC says you must securely dispose of a consumer report when you are done with it. Shred paper. Delete files fully. Keep your own notes and the notice, which do not hold the full report.

## A step list for each denial

Use the same steps every time:

1. **Check the criteria.** Find the exact line the applicant did not meet.
2. **Check for a protected class issue.** Make sure the reason has nothing to do with one.
3. **Check accommodation requests.** If the applicant asked for a disability accommodation, handle that request first.
4. **Write the notice.** Add the FCRA items if you used a report, and reasons if your state requires them.
5. **Refund any fee due.** Follow your state's deadline.
6. **File the record.** Save it for at least 2 years.

Clear, written steps protect you and treat each applicant with respect. If you want a professional to run screening for you, see how owners [compare property managers](/property-management).
