## Which line items do owners check first on a monthly statement?

Owners check the payout amount first, then rent collected, then any expense they did not expect. Put those three where the eye lands. A clear first block cuts the calls you get each month.

Some states make the statement a duty, not a courtesy. Texas rule 22 TAC 535.146(c)(6) says a broker must provide an accounting to each beneficiary of trust money at least monthly if the account had activity. New Mexico rule 16.61.24.12(A) says the qualifying broker must give the owner a report of receipts and disbursements monthly, or as the agreement requires. It must show the previous balance, funds deposited by category, funds disbursed by category and the ending balance. Other states have their own rules. This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

Use this checklist for each property, each month.

**Summary block**

- [ ] Property address and statement period
- [ ] Opening balance
- [ ] Total income
- [ ] Total expenses
- [ ] Owner payout, date and method
- [ ] Closing balance, including the reserve

**Income block**

- [ ] Rent collected, per unit and per tenant
- [ ] Late fees and other charges collected, if the owner gets them
- [ ] Rent due but not paid, with the amount and days late
- [ ] Prepaid rent held for next month

**Expense block**

- [ ] Each repair: date, vendor, short description, amount
- [ ] A link or copy of each invoice
- [ ] Management fee, with the rate and the rent it is based on
- [ ] Leasing, renewal or other fees, each on its own line
- [ ] Utilities, HOA dues, insurance or tax paid for the owner

**Balance block**

- [ ] Reserve held and the target amount in the agreement
- [ ] Security deposits held for this property (not owner money)
- [ ] A closing balance that matches your trust ledger

**Note block**

- [ ] Two to four sentences on vacancy, repairs and next steps

## What does a good monthly owner statement look like?

A good statement reads top to bottom and adds up without a calculator. Here is a worked example for one single-family home. The fee rate is an example, not a market figure.

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Opening balance (reserve) | $500.00 |
| Rent collected, June | $2,100.00 |
| Management fee, 8% of $2,100 | −$168.00 |
| Plumbing repair, invoice #4471 | −$285.00 |
| Funds available | $2,147.00 |
| Reserve target held back | −$500.00 |
| Owner payout, ACH, July 5 | $1,647.00 |
| Closing balance (reserve) | $500.00 |

Check the math. $500 + $2,100 − $168 − $285 = $2,147. Hold back $500, and $1,647 goes to the owner. The closing balance equals the reserve target.

Keep security deposits out of this math. A deposit is tenant money held in trust. Show it as a separate line so the owner knows the amount, but never add it to the payout.

## How do I show repairs and markups on the owner statement?

Show the vendor's cost and your own charge as two lines. One blended number hides your fee and invites a dispute.

| Method | How it looks | Owner trust |
|---|---|---|
| Blended | "Plumbing repair $330" | Low: owner cannot see your share |
| Split | "Plumber $300" and "Coordination fee 10%, $30" | High: both numbers visible |
| Disclosed in agreement only | "Plumbing repair $330" with a markup clause | Medium: legal, but owners forget the clause |

Attach the vendor invoice to each repair line. Owners who see the invoice ask fewer questions. Buildium's guide on automated owner reports lists maintenance requests and management fee calculations as standard report items.

## Should I add a short note to the owner statement?

Yes. A note of 2 to 4 sentences answers the question an owner would call about. Write it in plain words, with numbers.

Good note examples:

- **Vacancy.** "Unit 2 is vacant since June 12. We showed it 6 times. We have 2 applications in review."
- **Repair.** "The water heater is 14 years old. We expect to replace it within 12 months. A quote is attached."
- **Late rent.** "Unit 1 paid $900 of $1,400. We sent the notice on June 6. The rest is due June 20."

Keep the note factual. Do not write about tenants' personal lives. Describe the lease, the payment and the property.

## How do I check the statement against the trust account?

You check it by matching the sum of all owner and tenant balances to the reconciled bank balance. If the two do not agree, stop and find the gap before you send statements.

Florida sets a clear standard for brokers. Rule 61J2-14.012 says a broker must, once a month, compare total trust liability with the reconciled bank balance of all trust accounts. The broker must review, sign and date that reconciliation. If the numbers do not agree, the broker must explain the difference and the corrective action.

Even where no rule like this applies, use the same 4-step check:

1. Reconcile the trust bank statement to your ledger.
2. Total every owner balance, tenant deposit and prepaid rent.
3. Compare that total to the reconciled bank balance.
4. Fix each difference, then run owner statements.

## What year-end reports do owners need?

Owners need a 1099-MISC, a 12-month income and expense report, and a year-end balance of reserves and deposits. Send them in January, before owners meet their tax preparer.

| Report | What it shows | Deadline or timing |
|---|---|---|
| Form 1099-MISC | Rent you paid over to the owner | Give to owner by January 31 (IRS) |
| 12-month income and expense report | Totals by category, per property | January, with the 1099 |
| Year-end balance report | Reserve held, deposits held, prepaid rent | January |
| Copies of large invoices | Repairs and replacements | On request, or in the owner portal |

The IRS instructions for Forms 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC say a renter who pays through a property manager does not report that rent. The property manager must use Form 1099-MISC to report rent paid over to the owner. The same page says to give payee statements by January 31 and file by February 28, or March 31 if you file electronically. For 2026 payments, the box 1 rent threshold on that page is $2,000. Ask a tax professional how this applies to your company.

This is general information, not tax advice. Check the current rules or ask a tax professional.
