## What Do Property Managers Do With Late Fees?

Property managers can use different late-fee allocations, so put the chosen arrangement in writing. Do not present your own policy as a national rule.

Rentec Direct describes arrangements where managers keep all, part or none of late fees. That describes possible business terms. It does not give permission to charge a resident or prove which arrangement is most common.

Separate the documents. The resident lease addresses the resident charge. The management agreement addresses your authority and compensation. Your accounting process records the collection and allocation. A mismatch between those records can create a dispute even when everyone recalls a different verbal promise.

Ask what work the late fee is meant to fund. The owner faces delayed cash. The manager may spend time on reminders, ledger review and follow-up. A clear allocation explains how those interests fit together.

If the agreement is silent, do not assume that possession of the funds makes them yours. Ask the supervising broker and local counsel to review the terms. Resolve the allocation before transferring disputed money into operating income.

## Is Splitting Late Fees Fair?

A split can be reasonable when both parties understand the work and the accounting. No single percentage is fair for every property or service plan.

Use this decision table when discussing options. It is a planning aid, not a legal fee schedule.

| Allocation choice | Business reason to discuss | Question to resolve |
|---|---|---|
| Owner receives the fee | Owner bears delayed income | How does normal management pay cover follow-up? |
| Manager receives the fee | Manager handles added collection work | How do you avoid a reward for poor payment handling? |
| Owner and manager share | Both bear added cost | Which collection event starts the split? |
| Separate fixed service charge | Work cost does not match receipts | Is the charge approved and lawful? |

Explain whether the split applies to assessed fees or cash actually received. A ledger charge that no one pays is not the same as available cash. State how refunds and waivers affect the allocation.

Keep the owner statement readable. Show the resident fee collected, the manager portion and the owner portion as separate entries. Do not bury the transfer inside a general adjustment. The owner should be able to trace the result without asking staff to rebuild it.

## Does Keeping Late Fees Create a Conflict of Interest?

Keeping late fees can create an incentive concern if staff earn more when residents pay late. Address the concern through process and clear supervision.

Budget normal operations without assuming late payments. Use rent collection quality and accurate records as performance goals. Do not reward staff for the total penalties assessed. A higher penalty balance may show a payment problem rather than stronger performance.

Make the payment process clear at move-in. Show residents where to pay, how to report an error and who handles a failed transaction. Resolve posting mistakes before discussing penalties. A charge caused by your own process failure needs review.

Give someone authority to check disputed late fees. Record the reason for waivers and refunds. Use consistent, lawful criteria, and escalate requests that involve accommodation or other rights to trained staff.

Tell the owner how late-payment decisions are made. The owner should know when staff can waive a charge and when approval is needed. Staff should never delay posting rent to create fee income. Compare receipts with posting records when investigating a complaint.

## How Do State Late-Fee Caps Affect the Allocation?

State and local rules can limit the resident charge before any owner-manager split occurs. An allocation clause cannot expand the amount the law permits.

North Carolina offers a concrete example. Its General Assembly states in G.S. 42-46(a) that a late fee requires an agreement and rent at least 5 calendar days late. For monthly rent, the cap is the greater of $15 or 5% of monthly rent. G.S. 42-46(b) limits the charge to once for each late payment.

The same North Carolina statute addresses subsidized rent. Under G.S. 42-46(h)(5), covered fees use the tenant's rent share, excluding the subsidy. These are North Carolina rules. Do not apply them as national terms.

Have local counsel check your actual properties and leases. Ask about the cap, waiting period, permitted base and payment allocation. Check local rules and housing program terms too. If you manage across markets, keep separate approved settings rather than a shared default.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## What Should the Late-Fee Policy Checklist Cover?

A useful policy connects lawful assessment, payment handling and manager compensation. Use the following checklist before changing the allocation.

- Confirm the current lease language and property jurisdiction.
- Verify the charge, timing and base with a local legal review.
- Record the owner-manager allocation in the management terms.
- Define the treatment of unpaid fees, waivers and reversals.
- Check that software uses the correct property settings.
- Give residents a clear route to dispute posting errors.
- Reconcile collected fees with owner and manager entries.

Review exceptions with accounting and the supervising broker. If a resident payment covers several charges, use the lawful payment order. Do not let a software default decide a legal question.
