California's DRE says commingling is grounds to revoke a real estate license. The money in the account is not yours. The state treats you as a trustee, and it audits you like one.

Rules differ by state. This guide uses three state rule books as examples: California, Texas and Florida. Use it to know which questions to ask, then read your own state's rules.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## Trust account rules at a glance

The table compares the main trust account rules in three states. Each cell comes from the state source listed under it.

| Rule | California | Texas | Florida |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where client money goes | Trust account in the broker's name as trustee, at a bank in California (B&P Code 10145, Reg. 2832) | Designated trust account, or an authorized Texas escrow agent (TREC Rule 535.146(b)(1)) | Trust or escrow account kept by the broker (F.S. 475.25(1)(k)) |
| Deposit deadline | Not later than 3 business days after receipt (Reg. 2832) | Close of business on the 2nd working day after receipt, unless principals agree in writing (535.146(b)(3)) | "Immediately" upon receipt (F.S. 475.25(1)(k)) |
| Broker's own money allowed | Up to $200 for bank charges; earned fees may stay up to 25 days (Reg. 2835) | A reasonable amount for bank service fees (535.146(c)(4)) | Up to $5,000 in a property management escrow account (F.S. 475.25(1)(k)) |
| Monthly duty | Reconcile monthly, bank record to bank statement and to each beneficiary record (RE 13) | Monthly accounting to each beneficiary if the account had activity (535.146(c)(6)) | Monthly written statement, signed and dated by the broker (Rule 61J2-14.012) |
| Record retention | 3 years (B&P Code 10148) | 4 years (535.146(e)) | Records kept available to the regulator (61J2-14.012) |

Sources: California DRE guide RE 13; 22 Tex. Admin. Code 535.146 on Cornell LII; Florida Statutes 475.25; Fla. Admin. Code 61J2-14.012 on Cornell LII.

## What money must go in a trust account?

Any money you hold for someone else must go in a trust account. For a property management company, that usually means:

- Rent collected for owners, until you pay it out.
- Tenant security deposits, if your state lets the manager hold them.
- Owner reserves held for repairs.
- Prepaid rent and application deposits.

The California DRE guide (RE 13) says trust funds that do not go to the principal or a neutral escrow depository must go into the broker's trust account. It also says the account must be named as a trust account, with the broker as trustee.

Your management fee is not trust money once you earn it. But it starts life inside the trust account, because it comes out of the rent. The rule then is to move it out on time. California lets earned commissions and fees stay in the trust account for no more than 25 days.

## Can I mix security deposits and rents in one account?

Many states let you hold rents and deposits in one trust account, as long as your records show what belongs to whom. Texas Rule 535.146(c)(2) says a broker may keep separate trust accounts for each type of trust money, such as security deposits for managed rentals, but does not have to.

Security deposits can carry extra rules. Some states and cities set where a deposit must sit and whether it earns interest for the tenant. Check your landlord-tenant law as well as your license law.

What you may never mix is your own money with client money. That is commingling. The California DRE guide calls commingling grounds to revoke or suspend a license under B&P Code 10176(e). Texas Rule 535.146(b)(5) says placing trust money in a broker's personal or operating account is prima facie commingling.

## Who may sign on the trust account?

The licensed broker signs on the trust account, and some states let the broker add others in writing. California Commissioner's Regulation 2834, as quoted in RE 13, allows withdrawals only on the signature of:

1. The broker in whose name the account is held.
2. The designated broker-officer, if the account belongs to a corporate broker.
3. A salesperson licensed to the broker, if the broker authorizes it in writing.
4. An unlicensed employee, if the broker authorizes it in writing and the employee has a fidelity bond at least equal to the most trust money the employee can reach at any time.

The guide adds that the broker stays responsible even when others sign. Adding a bookkeeper as a signer does not move the liability.

If you use remote staff or a virtual assistant for accounting, give them entry rights in the software, not signing rights at the bank. Keep approval of payments with a licensed person.

## What do state audits check?

State audits check that the trust account balance equals what you owe all owners and tenants. California calls that total the "aggregate trust fund liability".

- **Shortage:** the bank balance is less than what you owe. The DRE guide says this breaks Regulation 2832.1, unless every owner of the funds agreed in writing before the payment that caused it.
- **Overage:** the bank balance is more than what you owe. That often means your own money is in the account.

Auditors also look for:

- [ ] Deposits made inside the state deadline.
- [ ] A journal of every receipt and payment, in date order.
- [ ] A ledger for each owner or beneficiary with a running balance.
- [ ] Monthly reconciliations, signed where the state requires it.
- [ ] No negative owner balances paid with other owners' money.
- [ ] No personal or operating expenses paid from the trust account.
- [ ] Records kept for the full retention period.

Florida Rule 61J2-14.012 lists what the monthly statement must show: the date, the bank and account numbers, balances, deposits in transit, outstanding checks, and an itemized list of trust liabilities. When the numbers do not agree, the statement must explain the difference and the corrective action. The broker must review, sign and date it.

## Which trust account practices keep you safe?

These five habits follow from the rules above. They are our practice advice, not state law.

1. **Pay owners only from collected funds.** Do not pay an owner's repair from the account when that owner's ledger is at zero.
2. **Move your fees out on a schedule.** A fixed monthly transfer keeps you inside limits like California's 25 days.
3. **Keep a small, set bank-fee cushion.** Stay under your state's cap, for example $200 in California.
4. **Reconcile every month, before the 10th.** Find a mismatch while you still remember the month.
5. **Separate duties.** The person who records payments should not be the only person who reconciles.

For the monthly check itself, the method is a three-way reconciliation: bank, book and owner ledgers. Your software matters here too. See how two common systems compare in our [AppFolio vs Buildium guide](/guides/appfolio-vs-buildium).

## Where do I find my state's rules?

Your state's real estate commission or licensing board publishes its trust account rules. Search its site for "trust account" or "escrow account". Read three things:

- The license law section on handling client money.
- The board rule on trust or escrow accounts.
- Any trust account guide or audit checklist the board publishes.

License rules for property managers differ by state. If you manage in more than one state, keep a one-page table like the one above for each.
