New landlords rarely fail on big things. They fail on small steps they did not know about. A missed form, an old insurance policy, a deposit in the wrong account. Each one is cheap to fix before the first tenant and costly after.

This checklist groups the most common mistakes in four areas. Each item says what goes wrong and what to do instead.

This is general information, not legal or tax advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney or a tax professional.

## What Do New Landlords Forget About Insurance?

The biggest miss is keeping a homeowners policy on a rented home. A homeowners policy is written for a home you live in. The North Carolina Department of Insurance (NCDOI) says a dwelling fire policy is typically used for rentals.

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Keeping the homeowners policy | A claim may be denied | Switch to a landlord or dwelling policy before move-in |
| No liability coverage | NCDOI says dwelling policies typically do not include liability | Add liability or an umbrella |
| No loss-of-rent coverage | Rent stops while a covered loss is repaired | Ask about fair rental value coverage |
| Ignoring vacancy rules | The Texas Department of Insurance says most companies stop coverage after 60 days vacant | Tell the insurer when the home will be empty |
| No renters insurance clause | The tenant's things are not on your policy | Require renters insurance in the lease, where allowed |
| Assuming floods are covered | TDI says most home policies don't cover floods | Price flood coverage separately |

## Why Do New Landlords Underprice or Overprice Rent?

New landlords often set rent from their mortgage payment or from a guess. The market does not care about your mortgage. Tenants compare your home with others nearby.

Two mistakes, two costs:

- **Too high.** The home sits empty. One empty month costs about 8% of a year's rent.
- **Too low.** You lose money every month of the lease. In some states, raising rent later takes long notice. Washington requires at least 90 days' written notice of a rent increase under RCW 59.18.140.

Here is a worked example with invented numbers. Market rent is $2,000. Owner A asks $2,200 and waits 2 months for a tenant. Owner B asks $2,000 and rents in 2 weeks.

| Owner | Monthly rent | Months vacant | First-year rent |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | $2,200 | 2 | $22,000 |
| B | $2,000 | 0.5 | $23,000 |

Owner B collects $1,000 more in year 1, at the lower rent. Price from at least 3 similar rentals nearby, and review after 2 weeks of few calls.

## Which Tax Records Do New Landlords Skip?

New landlords skip records they did not know they needed. Start the file on day one.

- **Depreciation.** IRS Publication 527 says residential rentals are depreciated over 27.5 years. IRS Publication 544 uses depreciation "allowed or allowable", so skipping it does not protect you at sale. You lose the deduction and still face the tax.
- **Conversion value.** If the rental was your home, Publication 527 says the basis for depreciation is the lesser of fair market value or adjusted basis on the conversion date. Record the value on that date.
- **Every expense.** Publication 527 lists advertising, cleaning and maintenance, insurance, interest, legal fees, management fees, repairs, taxes and utilities.
- **Repairs vs. improvements.** Publication 527 says improvements that better, restore or adapt the property must be capitalized. Keep receipts with a note on what each job was.
- **Security deposits.** Publication 527 says a deposit you plan to return is not income. A deposit you keep, or one used as last month's rent, is income.
- **Travel.** Publication 527 allows travel costs when the main purpose is to collect rent or manage, conserve or maintain the rental. Keep a log.

Use a separate bank account for the rental. Your records and your Schedule E then match.

## What Legal Steps Do New Landlords Miss?

New landlords miss federal steps that apply in every state, and state steps that differ by state.

**Federal steps:**

- **Fair housing.** The Fair Housing Act, 42 U.S.C. 3604, bans discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status or national origin. It also bans ads that show a preference based on these. Write screening criteria and use them for every applicant.
- **Lead paint.** The EPA says the lead disclosure rule applies to most housing built before 1978. Give the disclosure before the tenant signs.
- **Screening reports.** The FTC says landlords who use consumer reports must follow the FCRA. If you deny an applicant, require a co-signer or ask for a higher deposit based partly on a report, give an adverse action notice.

**State steps (examples):**

| Topic | Example rule | Law |
|---|---|---|
| Deposit account | Florida: hold deposits in a separate Florida bank account, without mixing funds, or post a surety bond | Statutes 83.49(1) |
| Deposit claims | Florida: notify the tenant within 30 days after move-out of a claim on the deposit | Statutes 83.49(3) |
| Entry notice | Arizona: at least 2 days' notice to enter, except emergencies | ARS 33-1343(D) |
| Rent increase notice | Washington: at least 90 days' written notice | RCW 59.18.140(3)(a) |

Your state's rules may be very different. Read your state's landlord-tenant law before you write the lease.

## What Is the Before-Move-In Checklist?

- ☐ Landlord policy with liability in place
- ☐ Rent set from 3 nearby comps
- ☐ Written screening criteria, same for all applicants
- ☐ Lead disclosure ready, if built before 1978
- ☐ Adverse action notice template ready
- ☐ Lease that follows state law
- ☐ Deposit account set up under state law
- ☐ Move-in photos and checklist
- ☐ Separate bank account and a rental ledger
- ☐ Depreciation start date and basis recorded
