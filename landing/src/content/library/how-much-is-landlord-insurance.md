Most cost guides for landlord insurance quote a single number from a quote engine. We wanted a number from a regulator. The best public source we found is the NAIC home insurance report. The NAIC is the National Association of Insurance Commissioners, the group of state insurance regulators.

Its newest edition covers 2023 data. Here is what it says, and what it does not say.

## How Much Does Landlord Insurance Cost on Average?

The countrywide average dwelling fire premium was $1,019 a year in 2023, per the NAIC report. Many landlords buy dwelling fire policies. The North Carolina Department of Insurance says these policies are typically used for homes that are not the owner's primary residence, such as rentals.

| Policy type, 2023 countrywide (NAIC Table 4) | Average yearly premium |
|---|---|
| Dwelling fire (all forms) | $1,019 |
| HO-3 homeowners | $1,737 |
| All dwelling fire and owner-occupied forms | $1,731 |

The dwelling fire average is lower, but it buys less. The NAIC says dwelling fire premiums are generally lower because dwelling policies offer more limited coverage than homeowners packages. NCDOI says dwelling policies typically do not include liability. Many landlords add liability, so their real total is higher than $1,019.

Prices also climbed. The NAIC report on 2022 data said the average premium for dwelling fire and owner-occupied homeowners policies rose 10.5% from 2021 to 2022.

## How Does Coverage Amount Change the Premium?

Larger coverage amounts cost more. NAIC Table 4 groups premiums by the amount of insurance. Here are selected countrywide ranges for 2023:

| Amount of insurance | Dwelling fire average | HO-3 average |
|---|---|---|
| Under $150,000 | $583 | $954 |
| $150,000 to $199,999 | $955 | $1,192 |
| $400,000 to $449,999 | $1,607 | $1,679 |
| $500,000 to $599,999 | $1,957 | $1,973 |
| $700,000 to $999,999 | $2,969 | $2,916 |
| $1,000,000 and over | $4,735 | $4,314 |

The dwelling fire numbers jump around between some middle ranges. Dwelling fire is a small market, about 2.9% of the exposures in this table, so small groups move the averages. Use the table as a guide, not a quote.

## What Drives the Premium?

The home and its location drive most of the price. The Texas Department of Insurance (TDI) lists the factors most companies use:

- **Age and condition.** Companies can charge more for older homes.
- **Replacement cost.** A higher cost to rebuild means a higher premium.
- **Construction.** TDI says all-wood houses cost more to insure than brick or stone.
- **Location.** Areas with more storms or crime cost more.
- **Fire protection.** Homes close to fire stations cost less.
- **Claims history.** Past claims on you or the house can raise the price. TDI says companies check the CLUE database, which holds 7 years of claims.
- **Credit score.** Some companies use it. TDI says a company can't turn you down based only on credit.

The NAIC report adds catastrophes. It says insured losses from U.S. catastrophes from 2016 to 2025 were more than $940.4 billion, in 2025 dollars. Higher losses feed into higher prices.

## Which States Cost the Most?

The NAIC publishes average premiums for each state in Table 4. It also warns that differences in state rules, coverage and risk make direct state-by-state comparisons difficult. Two homes with the same rent can carry very different premiums in two states, or in two ZIP codes of one state.

The safest approach: get 3 quotes for your own address, with the same coverage on each. Compare the form (DP-1, DP-2 or DP-3), the dwelling limit, the deductible and liability.

## How Can I Lower the Premium?

You can cut the premium without cutting the coverage you need. Use this checklist:

- **Ask about discounts.** TDI lists discounts some companies give for monitored burglar or fire alarms, sprinklers, impact-resistant roofs, and newer homes or homes in good condition.
- **Price a higher deductible.** Ask for quotes at two deductibles. If you pick the higher one, keep that cash in your repair reserve.
- **Fix the roof and systems.** Condition affects price and renewals.
- **Insure to the right value.** TDI says most companies require at least 80% of replacement cost. Too little coverage can hurt a claim. Too much wastes money.
- **Avoid small claims.** TDI says a company may raise rates after two or more non-weather claims.
- **Shop at renewal.** TDI says different companies charge different rates for the same risk.
- **Deduct it.** IRS Publication 527 lists insurance as a common rental expense.

Here is a simple worked example with invented numbers. A $1,400 premium with a $1,000 deductible drops to $1,200 with a $2,500 deductible. You save $200 a year but take on $1,500 more risk per claim. If you file one claim in 7.5 years, you break even.

## Why Are Some Carriers Not Renewing?

Some carriers limit how much they sell in an area. TDI lists the reasons a company may nonrenew a policy:

| Reason (TDI) | What you can do |
|---|---|
| The house is in bad condition | Repair before renewal; the company may require it |
| Two or more non-weather claims | Pay small losses yourself when you can |
| The house is vacant for 60 days or more | Tell the insurer; ask about vacancy coverage |
| The company limits coverage in your area | Start shopping as soon as you get the notice |

Vacancy matters for landlords. TDI says most companies stop coverage when a house is vacant for 60 days or more. Rentals sit empty between tenants, so ask your insurer how it handles that.

TDI says a company must give 60 days' notice of nonrenewal for Texas policies bought or renewed in 2024. If no standard company will insure you, TDI points to the Texas FAIR Plan and surplus lines companies, which cost more. Other states have their own rules and plans. Ask your state insurance department.
