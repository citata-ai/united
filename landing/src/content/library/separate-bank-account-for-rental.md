## Why does mixing rental and personal money create problems?

Mixed transactions make it harder to show what belongs to the rental. A separate operating account creates a clearer starting point. You still need receipts, a ledger, and regular reconciliation.

A bank statement shows money movement, not tax treatment. A deposit may be rent, a refundable tenant deposit, an owner contribution, or loan funds. A withdrawal may be an expense, an improvement, or money paid to yourself.

IRS Publication 527 distinguishes these transactions. Refundable security deposits generally are not income when received if you plan to return them. Advance rent generally follows the income rule for advance rent, even if a lease calls it a deposit.

For an LLC, keep the entity's activity distinct from personal spending. The SBA describes business banking as a way to separate funds. A separate account alone does not settle every liability or legal issue.

If you paid a rental bill personally, record the payment and reimbursement clearly. Do not count both as expenses. Save the original invoice and note which property received the work.

## Which accounts do landlords use?

Operating funds, tenant deposits, and owner reserves have different jobs. Your legal account duties depend on location and ownership. The structure below is a planning model, not a national account requirement.

| Cash purpose | What it holds | Record to keep |
|---|---|---|
| Operating account | Rent receipts and paid property bills | Income and expense ledger |
| Tenant security account | Deposit money held under local law | Individual tenant deposit balances |
| Owner reserve account | Owner funds for future costs | Reserve plan and transfer history |

Your bank may offer several account products. Compare fees, access, payment limits, and whether the product meets local trust or deposit rules. An account called savings is not automatically a compliant tenant trust account.

Keep a ledger for each property even when the same owner uses a shared operating account. Keep each tenant deposit separately identifiable under the applicable rules. Do not assume a combined balance shows who owns the money.

A manager may hold funds for you. Ask where the money sits, who controls it, what records you receive, and how balances transfer when the agreement ends. The management contract should make those duties clear.

## Do states require separate deposit accounts?

Some states require specific handling of tenant security money. Read the rule for the property's location before taking a deposit. Local terms can govern the bank, interest, notice, and records.

Massachusetts guidance says a landlord must keep a security deposit in a separate interest-bearing bank account in Massachusetts. It also says the funds belong to the tenant unless the landlord may lawfully use them. The page describes required notice of bank details.

New York General Obligations Law Section 7-103 treats covered security money as trust money. It says the funds must not mix with the recipient's personal money or become the recipient's asset. The statute also addresses banking and notice duties.

These examples do not establish a uniform rule elsewhere. Ask a local attorney which requirements apply to your property type. Confirm the correct handling before choosing an account based only on convenience.

Do not treat payment-platform labels as legal instructions. A platform may receive a deposit without creating the correct custody arrangement. Verify the full route from tenant payment to the final holding account.

## How do you set up the accounts?

Start with the legal owner, then choose a bank product that fits the use. Ask the bank what identity, formation, ownership, and tax documents it needs. The SBA lists these as common business banking preparation items.

Give the bank a clear description when the account will hold tenant money. Ask whether the account title and terms support the local requirement. Obtain written guidance from your legal adviser when trust handling is involved.

For operating cash, move recurring rent and bill payments in a planned order. Confirm that existing automatic payments still clear. Keep enough money in the old account until the change is complete.

Record opening balances by purpose. A lump sum may contain owner cash and tenant money. Identify each part before using the balance as an operating reserve.

- **Verify ownership.** Match the account setup with the owner and management arrangement.
- **Check deposit law.** Confirm custody, interest, notice, and record rules locally.
- **Create the ledgers.** Separate properties, tenants, owner transfers, and reserves.
- **Test payments.** Check the route and reference attached to each receipt.
- **Limit access.** Give each helper only the authority needed for their task.
- **Save documents.** Keep the account agreement and required tenant notices.

## How should you reconcile rental cash?

Reconcile the bank balance against the ledger and each held-money balance. A positive account balance does not prove you can distribute all the cash. Open bills and tenant deposits may already have claims on it.

In a hypothetical example, an account shows $8,000. Assume $3,000 is tenant security money, $2,000 covers unpaid bills, and $1,000 is reserved for work. Only $2,000 remains before any further cash needs.

Those figures are invented planning inputs. If local law requires the security money elsewhere, the example is also a reason to correct custody. A spreadsheet category cannot cure an unlawful account setup.
