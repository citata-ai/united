## What work does eviction coordination include?

Eviction coordination can include organizing records, communicating with counsel and updating the owner. Write that scope before choosing a fee. Keep administrative work distinct from legal advice, document preparation that requires legal authority, and court representation.

Map the file your team needs to assemble. It may contain the lease, account history, owner instructions, communications and condition records. Have counsel specify the case evidence and the proper process for sending it securely.

A coordination fee should show what the manager does. It should not imply that the company controls court timing or can guarantee possession. Avoid selling an administrative package as an all-inclusive legal service.

Use this proposed scope table:

| Work or cost | Responsible party to identify | Billing question |
|---|---|---|
| Gather property records | Management team | Included in coordination fee? |
| Assess legal options | Authorized legal adviser | Separate engagement and bill? |
| File and serve documents | Properly authorized provider | Actual cost or included item? |
| Give owner updates | Named manager | Frequency and scope stated? |
| Attend a scheduled event | Authorized attendee | Flat fee or approved hourly work? |
| Secure lawful returned possession | Approved field team | Separate work order and cost? |

This is a planning table, not authority to perform any legal task. This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## Should court time use a flat fee or hourly rate?

A flat fee can work for predictable administrative steps, while hourly billing can fit variable attendance and follow-up. Either structure needs a clear boundary. Define what happens when the case takes more work than expected.

For a flat fee, list the included events and the point when the fee is earned. State whether a dismissed or settled case changes the charge. Keep the owner from paying twice for the same record preparation.

For hourly billing, define billable tasks and how staff record time. Give the owner a review process and an approval threshold chosen for the agreement. Do not present your threshold as a legal or industry standard.

Build the price from recorded staff time and the company's full service cost. If you lack records, label the first estimate as a trial assumption. Compare it with actual work before using it across the portfolio.

Do not price unauthorized work as though a higher fee makes it lawful. Ask counsel who can attend, speak or sign in the relevant court. Property type and owner entity can affect that review.

Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to compare broader management income. Add case-specific work separately so a rare event does not hide inside the base percentage.

## Should an eviction guarantee replace the fee?

An eviction guarantee can replace some stated charges, but only within its written terms. Compare the work the promise covers with the cost your company could face. Do not sell certainty about a legal outcome.

Write the eligibility rules in plain language. Explain whether the program depends on screening, payment history, continued management or timely owner cooperation. State the exclusions and how a claim is reviewed.

Separate unpaid rent, legal bills, administrative work and property damage. A promise covering coordination does not necessarily cover those other losses. Show the owner the categories rather than using a broad label.

Ask an attorney and insurance adviser to review the program before launch. They can assess the legal form and any coverage questions. Do not call a company-funded service promise insurance unless that description is proper.

Test the cash effect on your own business. Review a difficult case alongside the ordinary one. A guarantee funded from hoped-for future growth can strain the company when several claims arrive together.

Keep screening and collection decisions independent of the guarantee. Staff should follow lawful, consistent procedures. Do not let the cost of a claim pressure staff to skip steps or discourage a resident from raising a valid concern.

## How do I write the fee in the agreement?

Write the fee beside a defined scope, billing trigger and approval process. State what the owner pays your company and which outside costs remain separate. Have counsel align the clause with local rules.

Oregon's Real Estate Agency says management agreements must describe fees and other compensation, including when they are earned and paid. Its guidance also calls for written, signed amendments when fees or duties change. That is an Oregon example, not a national rule.

Use a drafting checklist rather than copying a fee clause from another state. Identify the service, excluded work, outside providers, owner approvals, time records and refund treatment. Ask counsel to turn the agreed business terms into valid language.

Do not assume the owner's fee can appear on the resident ledger. Resident charges need their own legal and lease review. Keep the accounting treatment separate until that review is complete.

California Courts gives a useful process example. Its eviction guide says landlords cannot force a move-out by locking tenants out, shutting off utilities or removing belongings. They must use the court process. Do not extend that example into a complete national eviction guide.

## What should I check before quoting the fee?

Check whether your staff can describe the service and its limits without promising a result. Give them a sample owner question about costs after a case settles. Make sure the answer matches the agreement.

Review the attorney engagement and the manager's authorization together. The owner should know who provides legal advice and where to send a disputed legal question. Staff should know when to stop explaining and refer the issue.

Create a case-cost record that separates your earned fee from money paid to others. Attach approvals and provider invoices. Give the owner a clear final statement when the coordination work ends.

After a case closes, review the time spent and the handoffs that failed. Improve records and training before raising the price. Browse the [guide library](/guides) for related fee work, then price the exact lawful service your company can deliver.
