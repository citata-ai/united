## Does your agreement move to the new company?

The answer depends on the transaction, the agreement and applicable law. A new brand name does not tell you whether the contracting entity changed. Ask the manager to identify the entity that signed your agreement and the entity now responsible for service.

Request a written explanation of the sale structure. Did the buyer acquire ownership of the existing company, or buy assets and contracts? Have a local attorney review what that structure means for your agreement. Do not infer consent rights from a general announcement.

California DRE's Property Management chapter recommends a written agreement that states duties, fees, term and authority. Use those headings to compare the current agreement with any proposed replacement. A welcome letter is not a full comparison.

Use this transition checklist before signing new terms:

| Item | Ask the new operator | Record to keep |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contracting entity | Who owes the management duties? | Legal entity name and agreement |
| Assignment | Which clause supports the transfer? | Clause and any consent form |
| Service scope | What duties change? | Written comparison |
| Fees | Which charges change and when? | Fee schedule and effective date |
| Funds | Where does each balance transfer? | Closing and opening ledger |
| Open work | Who owns the next action? | Repair and notice log |

Ask for enough time to read the proposed documents. Keep a copy of the old agreement and all sale notices. Do not rely on a portal copy that may later be replaced.

## Can you cancel after the sale?

Cancellation rights need a contract and local-law review; a sale alone does not answer the question. Read the termination, notice, renewal and assignment clauses together. Ask counsel whether a change-of-control clause also matters.

Identify the exact notice method before sending a cancellation. An email to your former contact may not satisfy the agreement. Ask which entity and address should receive notice. Keep proof of what you sent and when.

Request a written calculation of any claimed exit fee. Compare the amount and reason with the agreement. Ask whether leasing fees, final bills or other charges remain open. Do not accept a lump sum with no explanation.

If you want to stay, ask whether you must sign a new contract. Compare the whole document, including repair authority, vendor charges and dispute terms. A familiar percentage fee does not mean the rest of the agreement stayed the same.

If you plan to leave, prepare the next management arrangement before the cutoff. Name who receives tenant requests, holds keys and tracks rent during the transition. Avoid a gap where each manager believes the other handles urgent work.

Keep the service decision separate from the transfer of client funds. Ask for the fund records even if you have not chosen whether to stay. You need a clear balance to assess either option.

## What should you check about deposits and reserves?

Verify every fund category through dated closing and opening records. A single transfer total cannot show whether each tenant deposit and owner reserve is correct. Ask for the detail that explains your property.

The North Carolina Real Estate Commission's takeover guidance calls for review of reconciled bank, journal and ledger balances. It also directs brokers to identify the nature of held funds, including rents, deposits and reserves. These are useful review questions for an owner, without treating them as every state's rules.

Ask the managers to agree on a common cutoff. Request your property's ending ledger, transfer amount and new opening ledger. List outstanding checks, receipts not yet cleared, unpaid invoices and owner payments in transit. Obtain a written explanation for any difference.

Keep tenant deposit records separate from your available operating funds. Ask which party holds each deposit and which records support the amount. Have counsel check the local transfer and notice duties. A business sale does not remove the need to identify the holder.

Also review access and payment changes. Confirm new bank or portal instructions through a phone number you already trust. Do not verify a change solely through the contact details in the change message. Ask for a coordinated tenant notice that avoids conflicting rent instructions.

Export your statements and invoices before the old portal closes. Keep readable copies of the lease, deposit record and open maintenance files. Confirm that the new operator can access the same records without asking tenants to rebuild the history.

## How should you judge the new owner?

Judge the new operator by named staff, clear records and performance against the agreement. A purchase announcement can explain intent, but it cannot prove service quality. Ask who will manage your property and who handles an unresolved complaint.

Request a written transition plan. It should cover tenant contact, emergencies, vendors, accounting and owner reporting. Ask which staff will stay and which processes will change. Confirm the first reporting cutoff and expected distribution process.

Review vendor relationships and fees. Ask whether maintenance uses an affiliated company and how charges are disclosed. California DRE's reference chapter describes an agent's disclosure and accounting duties. Ask the new operator to explain its own process in concrete terms.

Set an owner review after the first complete reporting cycle. Compare collected rent, bills, reserve changes and fees with supporting records. Review open repair cases at the same time. Do not accept a correct payout as proof that every pending duty transferred correctly.

Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to compare the stated costs of staying and moving. Add any verified transition charges to your own analysis. Make the service decision from the written terms and records, not only the new company's sales pitch.

Send the operator a short list of unanswered questions. Request a named person and next action for each item. Keep that list until the balances, service duties and records are fully accounted for.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.
