You find a good tenant for your condo. Then the association sends a letter: the building has reached its rental cap, and your unit is on a waiting list. That letter can cost months of rent.

The rules that cause it live in two places. Your HOA's documents, and your state's law on what those documents may say. This guide covers both, with four state examples.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## What Is a Rental Cap?

A rental cap limits how many units in a community can be rented at one time. When the cap is full, new rentals wait for a slot. The cap is usually written in the declaration, the main governing document of the HOA or condo association.

State laws treat caps very differently. Here are four examples:

| State | Rule on rental limits | Law |
|---|---|---|
| California | An HOA can't prohibit or unreasonably restrict rentals. It can't cap rentals below 25% of units. | Civil Code 4741(a)-(b) |
| Florida (condos) | An amendment that bans rentals, limits the term or limits how often you rent applies only to owners who consent and to later buyers. | Statutes 718.110(13) |
| Arizona (planned communities) | A member may rent unless the declaration prohibits it, and must follow its rental time limits. | ARS 33-1806.01(A) |
| Texas (property owners' associations) | The law does not bar a dedicatory instrument from restricting occupancy or leasing. | Property Code 209.016(d) |

California adds a detail. Civil Code 4741 says a unit occupied by its owner, including its accessory dwelling unit, does not count as a rented unit. It also says HOAs had to amend non-compliant documents by July 1, 2022.

Many other states have no specific rule. In those states, the declaration and general contract law decide most questions.

## What Are Minimum Lease Terms and Short-Term Bans?

A minimum lease term sets the shortest lease you may sign, such as 6 or 12 months. A short-term rental ban stops nightly or weekly stays. Both are common in condo and HOA documents.

State law often allows short-term bans even where it limits other rental rules. California Civil Code 4741(c) lets a common interest development ban rentals of 30 days or less. Arizona's law says a member must follow the declaration's rental time period restrictions.

Florida protects current owners from new limits. Under 718.110(13), an amendment that changes the length of rental terms applies only to owners who agree and to owners who take title after the amendment. If you bought before the change and did not vote for it, the old rule may still apply to you.

Insurance matters here too. The Texas Department of Insurance says most home policies won't pay for damages or injuries during short-term rentals. Check your policy before you list nightly.

## Can the HOA Approve or Screen My Tenant?

It depends on the state. Some states let associations ask only for basic details. Others allow more.

| State | What the association may ask | What it may not do |
|---|---|---|
| Texas | Names, mailing address, phone and email of each adult tenant; lease start date and term | Require tenant approval by the association; require a credit report or the rental application (209.016(b), (e)) |
| Arizona | Names and contact details of adult occupants, lease dates, vehicle description and plates | Ask for other tenant information; charge more than $25 per new tenancy for the disclosure; charge rental units differently from owner-occupied units, with stated exceptions (33-1806.01(C)-(D)) |
| California | Rules depend on the documents | Prohibit or unreasonably restrict rentals (4741(a)) |
| Florida | Rules depend on the declaration | New rental limits can't bind owners who did not consent (718.110(13)) |

Arizona also lets you name an agent, such as a property manager, to deal with the association for the rental. The agent can't vote or serve on the board for you.

Whoever screens the tenant must follow fair housing law. Apply the same written criteria to every applicant.

## What Happens If I Break the Rules?

The association can fine you, limit common area use, and in some cases go to court. The steps and limits depend on your state and your documents.

Florida gives a clear example for condos. Statutes 718.303(3) lets the association levy reasonable fines against an owner or tenant who breaks the rules. It caps fines at $100 per violation, or $1,000 in the aggregate. A fine may not become a lien on the unit. The association may also suspend use of common areas for a reasonable time, with exceptions such as access to the unit.

Rules also bind associations. California Civil Code 4741(g) says an association that willfully violates the rental section is liable for actual damages and a civil penalty of up to $1,000.

## How Do You Check the Rules Before You Rent?

Use this checklist before you list your unit:

- **Get the documents.** Ask for the declaration (CC&Rs), bylaws, rules and all amendments.
- **Find the rental section.** Look for caps, waiting lists, minimum terms and short-term rules.
- **Check the dates.** Note when each rental amendment passed and when you took title.
- **Ask the manager** how many units are rented now and if a waiting list exists.
- **List the forms.** Find what the association needs about the tenant and any fee.
- **Read your state law.** Use the table above as a starting point if you are in one of these four states.
- **Check insurance.** Confirm your condo policy allows renting, and ask tenants to carry renters insurance.
- **Put HOA rules in the lease.** Give the tenant a copy and require them to follow it.
