## Massachusetts rent and notice rules at a glance

Massachusetts sets no number for how much you may raise the rent. It does set how you give notice, when a late fee may start, and when a raise looks like retaliation. We read each statute below on the Massachusetts Legislature site (text as of 2026).

| Rule | Limit or deadline | Statute |
|---|---|---|
| Statewide rent cap | None | No cap in the statutes we read |
| Local rent control | Banned, except a voluntary, compensated form | c. 40P §4 |
| End a tenancy at will, rent paid monthly | Rent interval or 30 days, whichever is longer | c. 186 §12 |
| End a tenancy at will, rent paid every 3 months or less often | 3 months | c. 186 §12 |
| Offer of new terms in the notice | Allowed, does not void the notice | c. 186 §12 |
| Rent in advance after move-in | No more than the current month | c. 186 §15B(1)(d) |
| Late fee or interest on rent | Not until 30 days after the due date | c. 186 §15B(1)(c) |
| Reprisal presumption | Rent increase within 6 months of a protected act | c. 186 §18 |

## Does Massachusetts have a cap on rent increases or rent control?

Massachusetts has no statewide cap on rent increases, and it bars local rent control. M.G.L. c. 40P §4 says no city or town may "enact, maintain or enforce rent control of any kind". There is one exception. A town that accepts the chapter may adopt rent control only if all of these hold:

- **Owner compliance turns voluntary** 6 months after the town first adopts the rules.
- **No control of evictions, occupancy, services or condo conversion.**
- **No coverage** of owners with fewer than 10 rental units, or of units with a fair market rent above $400.
- **The town pays owners** the gap between fair market rent and the controlled rent, from its general funds.

Rent control here means any rule that requires below-market rents (c. 40P §3). The ban does not cover public housing, publicly subsidized housing, federally assisted housing or mobile homes. Those units can have their own rent rules, such as a HAP contract.

## How many days notice does a Massachusetts landlord need to raise the rent?

For a tenant at will, the rent increase rides on a notice to end the tenancy. M.G.L. c. 186 §12 sets the length:

1. **Base rule:** 3 months' written notice.
2. **Rent paid more often than every 3 months:** the notice is enough if it equals the interval between rent days or 30 days, whichever is longer.

So a tenant who pays on the 1st of each month gets at least 30 days, or one full rent interval if that is longer. §12 also says your written notice "may include an offer to establish a new tenancy" on different terms. The offer does not make the notice invalid. That is how a landlord states a new rent in Massachusetts.

Worked example: rent is $1,800, paid on the 1st. You want $1,900. You give written notice that ends the current tenancy after at least one full rent interval. In the same notice, you offer a new tenancy at $1,900. If the tenant accepts, the new tenancy starts at $1,900. If not, the tenancy ends on the notice date.

Watch for: if the tenant stays without agreeing, the dispute goes to court, not to a lockout. §13 says no eviction case may start until the rent interval or 30 days, whichever is longer, passes after the tenant gets written notice. During that period the tenant pays rent at the old rate.

## Can a Massachusetts landlord raise the rent during a fixed-term lease?

The Massachusetts statutes we read give a landlord no right to raise rent during a fixed-term lease. The written lease sets the rent for its term. Read your lease for any clause on rent changes, and have a lawyer check it.

Two statutes limit what you can collect during any tenancy:

- **Advance rent:** after the tenancy starts, you may not demand rent in advance beyond the current month's rent (c. 186 §15B(1)(d)).
- **Late fees:** no lease may impose interest or a penalty for late rent until 30 days after the due date (c. 186 §15B(1)(c)).

At the end of the term, you can offer a renewal at a new rent.

## How much notice is needed to end a month-to-month tenancy in Massachusetts?

A Massachusetts month-to-month tenancy (a tenancy at will) ends with written notice equal to the rent interval or 30 days, whichever is longer. Either party can give it (c. 186 §12). The rules differ when the reason is unpaid rent:

| Reason | Notice | Statute |
|---|---|---|
| No fault, rent paid monthly | Rent interval or 30 days, whichever is longer | c. 186 §12, §13 |
| No fault, rooming house, rent weekly or daily | 7 days before a court case | c. 186 §13 |
| Unpaid rent, tenant at will | 14 days | c. 186 §12 |
| Unpaid rent, written lease | 14 days | c. 186 §11 |

A sale or foreclosure does not end a tenancy at will by itself (c. 186 §13). A new owner who wants a change must give notice like any landlord.

## Can a rent increase be a reprisal in Massachusetts?

Yes. M.G.L. c. 186 §18 creates a presumption of reprisal for a rent increase notice that arrives within 6 months after the tenant:

- [ ] reports a health or building code violation to a board of health or similar board;
- [ ] reports or complains of a violation to you in writing;
- [ ] starts or wins a legal action to enforce housing law;
- [ ] organizes or joins a tenants' union.

You can rebut the presumption only by clear and convincing evidence of a separate reason. You must also show you would have raised the rent the same way at the same time anyway. Damages run from 1 to 3 months' rent, or actual damages if higher, plus costs and attorney's fees. A lease waiver of §18 is void.

What to do: set a fixed renewal calendar for all units, write down the market reason for each increase, and pause raises while a code complaint is open.

## Do cities in Massachusetts add their own rent or notice rules?

Cities can add some rules, but not rent control outside c. 40P. We did not verify any city ordinance for this page, so check your city's website before you send notice.
