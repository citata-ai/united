## The 8 steps at a glance

Buying a first rental comes down to 8 steps. Each one has a test you can pass or fail before you spend more money.

| Step | What you do | Pass test |
|------|-------------|-----------|
| 1 | Set your cash budget | Down payment + closing + reserve in the bank |
| 2 | Get pre-approved for an investment loan | Written pre-approval with rate and terms |
| 3 | Pick a market | You can reach it, and renters want to live there |
| 4 | Pick a property type | It fits your budget and the local renter demand |
| 5 | Analyze the deal | Positive cash flow with all costs |
| 6 | Make an offer with contingencies | Inspection and financing contingencies in the contract |
| 7 | Do due diligence | Inspection, rent check, insurance, taxes, rules |
| 8 | Close and set up management | Lease, accounts and a manager or a plan |

## How much money do I need to start?

You need a down payment, closing costs and a cash reserve. Of the three, owners most often skip the reserve, and it causes the most trouble.

Down payment limits come from the loan. Fannie Mae's eligibility matrix, dated August 5, 2026, sets the maximum loan-to-value (LTV, the loan as a share of the price) for investment property purchases:

| Property | Max LTV (Fannie Mae, investment purchase) | Minimum down payment |
|----------|-------------------------------------------|----------------------|
| 1 unit | 85% | 15% |
| 2 to 4 units | 75% | 25% |

Fannie Mae's Selling Guide defines an investment property as one the borrower owns but does not occupy. It also notes that loan-level price adjustments apply to all investment property loans. So the rate is often higher than on a home loan.

Worked cash budget for a $250,000 house, with made-up closing costs and reserve:

| Line | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Down payment (15%) | $37,500 |
| Closing costs (example: 3%) | $7,500 |
| Make-ready repairs | $3,000 |
| Cash reserve (6 months of costs) | $12,000 |
| Total cash needed | $60,000 |

Ask your lender for its own reserve rule. It can be stricter than your own.

## How do I pick a market and a property type?

Pick a market you can reach or manage well from far away, where renters want to live. Then pick the home type those renters rent.

Market checks:

- **Distance.** Can you or a manager get there in under an hour?
- **Rent level.** Compare local rents with the HUD Fair Market Rent on our [Section 8 rent limits](/guides/section-8-rent-limits) page.
- **Vacancy.** The US rental vacancy rate was 7.3% in the second quarter of 2026 (Census Bureau). Ask a local manager how fast homes like yours fill.
- **Rules.** Rental license, inspection and short-term rental limits.
- **Property tax.** Rates differ a lot by county.

Property type checks:

| Type | Upside | Watch for |
|------|--------|-----------|
| Single-family house | Easy to buy, sell and finance; families stay longer | One vacancy means zero income |
| Condo or townhome | Lower price, outside upkeep by HOA | HOA fees and leasing limits |
| Duplex to fourplex | Several rents cover the loan | 25% minimum down as an investment loan (Fannie Mae) |

## How do I analyze a deal before I offer?

You analyze a deal by building a monthly cash flow with every cost, including the ones you do not pay every month. If the cash flow is positive only with zero vacancy, pass.

Worked example for the $250,000 house, with made-up costs:

| Line | Per month |
|------|-----------|
| Market rent (from comps) | $2,100 |
| Vacancy (8%) | −$168 |
| Principal and interest ($212,500 loan, example 7%, 30 years) | −$1,410 |
| Property tax | −$250 |
| Insurance | −$110 |
| Repairs (5%) | −$105 |
| Capital items: roof, HVAC, water heater (5%) | −$105 |
| Management (8.49%, the 2026 iPropertyManagement average) | −$178 |
| Cash flow | −$226 |

This deal loses money each month. That is a common result, and it is useful. Try a lower price, a higher down payment or a different house. Do not cut the vacancy, repair or management lines to make it work. Our [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) gives the management line for your rent.

Include management even if you will self-manage. Your time has a cost, and a later buyer or lender will count it.

## What due diligence comes before closing?

Due diligence comes between the accepted offer and closing. It confirms the numbers you used.

Checklist:

- **Inspection.** Roof, HVAC, water heater, plumbing, electric, foundation. Get repair quotes for big items.
- **Rent check.** 3 to 5 current comps for this exact home.
- **Insurance quotes.** A rental needs a dwelling policy. The North Carolina Department of Insurance describes DP-1, DP-2 and DP-3 forms, and notes they typically do not include liability coverage the way some homeowners forms do. Ask for liability.
- **Property tax.** Check the current bill and whether the tax rises after the sale.
- **HOA documents.** Leasing limits, minimum lease terms, fees and reserves.
- **City rules.** Rental license, registration or inspection.
- **Existing tenant.** If a tenant stays, read the lease and get a signed estoppel (a statement of rent, deposit and terms).

## Who should be on my team?

Your team is the set of people who catch mistakes before they cost you. Build it before you make an offer.

1. **Lender** who makes investment loans and explains reserves.
2. **Agent** who has bought or sold rentals.
3. **Inspector** who writes clear, photo-based reports.
4. **Insurance agent** who writes dwelling policies.
5. **Tax professional** for depreciation and Schedule E.
6. **Property manager**, or your written plan to self-manage. Our [property management](/property-management) page explains how owners compare managers.
7. **Handyman and plumber** you can call on a Saturday.

This is general information, not legal, tax or financial advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney, a lender or a tax professional.
