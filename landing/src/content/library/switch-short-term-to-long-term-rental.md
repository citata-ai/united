## When does a switch to long-term rental make sense?

A switch makes sense when a realistic long-term budget serves your cash needs and time limits better than guest bookings.

Use collected income rather than the best month on your listing dashboard. Include empty periods and canceled stays. Separate lodging taxes from owner revenue. Remove cleaning income only after you account for the cleaning costs that disappear with it.

Compare the same property across both models. Keep taxes, debt, and repair needs consistent. Then change only the costs tied to the rental type. A longer lease may reduce cleaning work while changing which utilities you pay.

| Budget line | Short-term review | Long-term review |
|---|---|---|
| Income | Completed stays and refunds | Supported rent and likely vacancy |
| Utilities | Bills paid by owner | Lease allocation and setup costs |
| Furnishings | Replacement and damage | Retain, remove, or offer separately |
| Management | Booking and local services | Leasing, monthly work, and renewals |
| Transition | Canceled dates and storage | Turnover work and lease preparation |

Stress-test the decision with weaker income and an unplanned repair. Use assumptions you can fund, not an occupancy claim from a sales call. The [management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) can help compare service costs.

Write the reason for the change before you start. That note helps when a strong holiday booking tempts you to delay. Set a target date only after you review existing commitments.

## What should I do with vacation rental furniture?

Keep furniture only when the target tenant wants it and the extra rent can support its costs.

Ask local leasing agents about demand for furnished and unfurnished homes. Use examples that match your home's location, size, condition, and lease length. A furnished corporate stay is not a direct comparison for an unfurnished household lease.

Make a full inventory before anything moves. Photograph the condition and record which items belong to the property. Separate fixtures from removable goods. Ask the insurer how storage and a change in use affect the covered property.

Price each option using actual quotes. Selling has collection and disposal work. Storage has recurring charges and transport costs. Leaving furniture can create later repair and replacement tasks. Do not assume furniture adds its original purchase cost to the rental's value.

If the home stays furnished, attach an inventory and care terms to the lease after local review. State who repairs owner appliances and how to report damage. Avoid vague promises that all goods will stay in perfect condition.

Keep purchase records even after removing items. IRS Publication 527 distinguishes rental expenses from depreciation and improvements. Ask your preparer how disposal or a change in use affects each asset. Do not erase a furnishing from the tax file simply because it leaves the house.

## How do I price and prepare a long-term lease?

Set long-term rent from comparable local leases and prepare the home for the tenant's actual use.

Start with homes a tenant could rent instead of yours. Match bedrooms, condition, parking, utility terms, and furniture. Separate asking rents from completed leases where you can obtain that evidence. Note when each comparison was available.

Ask for a written rent estimate from a manager who leases similar homes. Request the supporting properties and any proposed price changes. A high suggested rent without evidence can cost time if the home sits empty.

Check remaining guest reservations before advertising a firm move-in date. Review platform and management agreements for cancellation duties and exit terms. Resolve each booking through the relevant process. Keep the guest communication and final payment records.

Prepare the home for continuous occupancy. Review locks, safety items, appliance condition, mail access, parking, and waste service. Give the tenant clear instructions without leaving guest-only accounts or access codes active.

A long-term lease needs local review. Deposits, required disclosures, entry rights, screening, and notice rules vary by location. Do not extend a guest booking form and assume it works as a residential lease. Set written, lawful screening standards and apply them consistently.

Use a handover checklist with photos. Record keys, meter readings, supplied goods, and open repairs. Agree on a contact for urgent issues before the tenant takes possession.

## How do taxes and insurance change after the switch?

A change in rental use requires a new review of services, participation, personal use, and insurance terms.

IRS Publication 527 explains residential rental income and expense reporting. Publication 925 explains passive activity rules and the short-stay exception. A longer average stay can change that analysis. It does not reset your tax basis or erase past records.

Give your preparer the exact date guest use ended and the date long-term availability began. Include booking records, personal stays, and any period used for repairs. Preserve depreciation schedules for the building and furnishings. Ask how the new facts affect loss limits and reporting forms.

Tell your insurer about the new lease before occupancy starts. Travelers describes landlord coverage as protection for rental properties and covered rental income loss. Your policy terms must match the actual use. Ask for written confirmation of the change and any vacancy conditions during the transition.

Review permits and tax accounts with each relevant local office. Do not assume a platform closes an owner registration when a listing disappears. Keep confirmation of final returns or account changes where required.

Build a transition file with the last guest statement, lease, condition photos, insurance confirmation, and vendor handover. Review the first long-term owner statement against that file. Fix mismatched charges while the source records remain easy to find.
