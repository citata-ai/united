## How does the work per unit differ?

Work per unit differs when travel, shared systems and owner needs change. A single-family home may require a separate trip for each field task. A small building may combine visits while adding shared-area responsibility.

Do not assume every small building saves money. Inspect the actual scope. A shared entry, roof, heating system or parking area can create work that no unit's rent line captures.

Use this cost comparison before setting a price:

| Cost driver | Single-family question | Small multifamily question |
|---|---|---|
| Field travel | How far apart are homes? | Can tasks share a visit? |
| Access | Who controls entry to each home? | Who controls common and unit access? |
| Repairs | Which systems serve the home? | Which costs serve several units? |
| Reporting | What does the owner need per property? | What detail is needed by building and unit? |
| Leasing | What work follows each vacancy? | Can showings share a lawful process? |
| Owner decisions | How much approval work occurs? | Who approves building-wide work? |

The table describes questions to test, not guaranteed savings. Use staff records and vendor scope to answer them.

SBA's finance guidance supports tracking costs and business results. Apply that discipline to each service group. Keep property expenses separate from your company's own cost of delivering management.

## Should I charge per unit or per building?

Per-unit pricing suits recurring work tied to each household, while a building fee can cover shared management work. You can combine them if the agreement states the full calculation clearly. Test the bill during vacancy and unit-count changes.

A building base can pay for reporting, vendor oversight and common-area coordination. A unit component can cover resident accounts and leasing support. Avoid adding a building charge to a percentage without explaining what new work it buys.

Percentage pricing also needs a defined income base. State whether the calculation uses rent billed, rent collected or another agreed amount. Explain the treatment of arrears, concessions and non-rent receipts.

Compare total owner cost across plausible operating states. Include full occupancy, a vacant unit and a major repair period. Label those states as planning scenarios, not market forecasts.

Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to compare the percentage and fixed-fee portions. Add building-specific work that the calculator does not model. Keep the same scope when comparing proposals.

Choose the structure staff can invoice reliably. If every statement requires manual exceptions, simplify the offer before expanding it. A precise price is useful only when your billing process can reproduce it.

## How should maintenance fees differ?

Maintenance fees should follow the management work performed, not the property label alone. Identify scheduling, approval, site review and project oversight separately. Decide which work the base fee already covers.

Separate the contractor's invoice from your coordination charge. Owners should see whether the company earned a fee, passed through a cost or provided the work itself. Do not use vague expense labels to combine those items.

A common repair may serve several units, but allocation should follow the governing documents and appropriate accounting treatment. Have the accountant review the method. Avoid charging every unit as though each received a separate full-price repair.

Washington's WAC 308-124D-215 requires compensation in a written management agreement. For other services, it addresses written relationship disclosure, prior fee disclosure and owner permission. Those are Washington rules, not permission to use the same terms nationwide.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

For large work, prepare a separate scope and approval record. Define how staff handle changes, vendor selection and completion checks. Do not call major project management ordinary coordination if the cost and duties differ.

Review whether an affiliate supplies maintenance. Give counsel the financial relationship and the proposed disclosure before offering the service.

## Which type is more profitable per door?

The more profitable property type is the one that leaves more company income after its service costs in your records. Do not compare collected rent as if it all belongs to the manager. Use earned company fees.

Start with each property's recurring fees and approved additional income. Subtract direct staff work, travel and other service costs under a consistent method. Then compare the groups using the same reporting period.

Review exceptions before changing a whole fee schedule. An aging building, distant home or demanding owner may explain a weak result better than the asset type. Compare similar service conditions where possible.

Keep profit and cash timing separate. A profitable leasing fee may arrive after your team has already done the work. Add the timing to the cash plan before taking a large group of vacant units.

Account for founder work too. If the founder handles all complex building issues without a recorded cost, the apparent margin can mislead. Estimate the cost of having qualified staff perform that work.

Do not promise a property will be profitable for your company before learning its condition and owner requirements. Give the quote a clear scope and a process for reviewing newly discovered work.

## How should I test a new price?

Test a proposed price against completed work records and sample statements before using it in sales. Ask operations staff to identify what the proposal leaves out. Ask accounting staff to calculate the bill independently.

Keep a short quote worksheet with property scope, travel assumptions, common systems, reporting needs and exclusions. Record who approved any discount and why. A discount should have an operating reason that remains true after signing.

Review the first statements with the owner. Check whether they can match each fee to the service they bought. Correct unclear descriptions before the confusion repeats.

When a property performs differently from the plan, find the cause. Update scope or process through the proper agreement path rather than quietly adding charges.
