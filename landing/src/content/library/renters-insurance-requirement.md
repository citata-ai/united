## Is requiring renters insurance legal?

Requiring renters insurance is legal in most states when the written lease says so. A few states set rules on how you require it. Read your state's landlord-tenant law before you add the clause.

Oregon is a clear example of a state with detailed rules. Under ORS 90.222:

- The requirement must be in a written rental agreement.
- Before a new tenancy, you must tell the applicant in writing about the requirement, the amount, and the exceptions.
- For a month-to-month tenant, you can add the requirement with at least 30 days' written notice.
- You must carry comparable liability insurance yourself and show proof if a tenant asks.
- You may not require a tenant to buy from a particular insurer.
- You may not require the tenant to name you as an additional insured.

Oregon also bans the requirement for some tenants. You may not require it if the household income is at or below 50% of area median income. You also may not require it for many units subsidized with public funds.

Other states have fewer rules, or none in statute. That does not mean anything goes. Your city may have rules too.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## Why do landlords require renters insurance?

Landlords require renters insurance because it moves some risk off the landlord's policy. Renters insurance has three parts, says the Insurance Information Institute (III):

| Part | What it pays for | Why it helps you |
|---|---|---|
| Personal possessions | Tenant's belongings after fire, theft, water and other listed perils | Fewer claims against you for lost tenant items |
| Liability | Defense and awards when the tenant causes injury or damage | Pays for damage the tenant causes to others |
| Additional living expenses | Hotel and costs if the unit cannot be lived in | Tenant has a place to stay during repairs |

Your landlord policy covers the building. It usually does not cover the tenant's belongings. A tenant without insurance who loses everything in a fire may look to you for help, even when you were not at fault.

## What liability limit should I ask for?

Ask for a liability limit that matches your market and any state cap. Many landlords ask for $100,000 per occurrence. The III says liability limits generally start at about $100,000, and some experts suggest at least $300,000.

Check the cap first. Oregon ORS 90.222 says the required amount may not exceed $100,000 per occurrence, or the customary amount for similar rentals in the same market, whichever is greater.

Write the exact amount into the lease. "Renters insurance required" is too vague. "Liability coverage of at least $100,000 per occurrence" is clear.

## Should I be an interested party on the policy?

Yes, ask to be named an interested party. An interested party gets notice from the insurer when the policy is canceled, not renewed, or reduced. It is the easiest way to learn about a lapse.

Know the difference between the two roles:

- **Interested party:** gets notices. Has no coverage under the policy.
- **Additional insured:** has some coverage under the policy. Some states, such as Oregon, ban requiring this.

Ask for interested-party status in the lease. Ask for proof at move-in, before you hand over keys.

## How do I check a tenant has coverage?

Check coverage with a written proof step at move-in and at each renewal. A simple process:

1. **Lease clause.** State the minimum liability amount and the interested-party request.
2. **Proof before keys.** Ask for the declarations page. It shows the insurer, policy number, dates and limits.
3. **Log the end date.** Put the renewal date in your calendar or software.
4. **Check at renewal.** Ask for the new declarations page 2 weeks before the end date.
5. **Act on any notice** from the insurer the same week.

Some rent platforms track policies for you. Some sell their own policy. If your state bans steering a tenant to one insurer, do not require the tenant to use yours.

## What if the policy lapses?

If the policy lapses, follow the lapse rule in your lease. A clear rule gives the tenant a short time to fix it and tells them what happens if they do not.

A sample lapse process:

- **Day 0:** you learn of the lapse. Send a written notice with the lease clause.
- **Day 10:** the tenant sends proof of a new policy.
- **No proof:** treat it as a lease breach under your state's process.

In Oregon, ORS 90.222 lets a landlord end a tenancy for cause if the tenant does not get insurance after notice, but the tenant can cure by getting insurance. Other states use their own breach rules. Some leases let the landlord buy a policy and add the cost to rent. Check that your state allows it before you use it.

Apply the same rule to every tenant. A rule you enforce for some tenants and not others creates Fair Housing risk.
