## What Agreement Term Is Most Common?

Do not choose a term solely because another firm calls it standard. Use your service costs, owner needs and reviewed contract terms.

Buildium's agreement guide describes a year as a common residential term. It does not supply a current national survey of signed agreements. Treat that description as context, not proof that a particular term fits your company.

Start with the work your company accepts. Intake, listing preparation and ongoing operations create different cost patterns. If you fund heavy setup work through future monthly fees, a very short relationship can leave a loss. A long contract can still fail if its service scope is unclear.

Ask what the owner needs to decide. An owner who plans to sell may need a predictable exit. Another may value stable service during a long lease. Do not confuse the resident lease term with the management agreement term. They govern different relationships.

Review the full combination of duration, notice and cancellation terms. A short term with a difficult notice process can be less flexible than a longer term with a clear exit. Show owners how the agreement works in real dates before they sign.

## Does Month-to-Month Service Help Sales?

Monthly service may reduce an owner's concern about being tied to a firm, but its sales effect needs testing. Compare signed work and service cost.

Ask lost prospects what stopped them. A term objection may actually be a concern about service quality or unclear charges. Offer a direct explanation of the exit process before changing the whole agreement.

Use this planning table to compare structures. These are business choices for review, not legal templates.

| Structure | Possible benefit | Cost or risk to examine |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed initial term | Stable planning period | Early-exit disputes and notice duties |
| Monthly arrangement | Simple ongoing choice | Short tenure after expensive intake |
| Fixed term with exit right | Defined service period and flexibility | Clear triggers and final charges |
| Automatic renewal | Less routine signature work | Notice, consent and renewal-law review |

Test the offer with the same service scope. If you change price and term together, you cannot easily tell which change affected sales. Track who signs, who leaves and why.

Price setup work deliberately. Do not advertise flexible service while placing an unclear recovery charge in the fine print. Use the [fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to discuss scenarios, then show the actual contract terms.

## How Do You Write an Automatic-Renewal Clause?

Have local counsel write renewal terms that explain what renews, when it renews and how either party can stop renewal. Generic wording can miss applicable duties.

Give counsel the operating process, not just a preferred sentence. Explain whether staff seek active approval, send reminders or change prices at renewal. The contract and the software calendar need to match.

Use a clause review checklist with these fields:

- Start date and initial term end.
- Length of each renewal period.
- Whether renewal is automatic or needs a new signature.
- Notice method, address and receipt rules.
- Any reminder, consent or disclosure duties counsel identifies.
- How fee changes become effective.
- Termination rights during the renewed period.

Avoid inserting a notice deadline from another state's contract. Review each jurisdiction and the specific owner relationship. Laws may apply differently to different agreements, so ask counsel to confirm the scope.

Keep the signed version and evidence of notices together. If an owner says they cancelled, check the message history and receipt records. Route disputes to a person who can assess the full file. Do not rely on an automated renewal flag as the only evidence.

## Which Term Lowers Owner Churn?

No term alone proves that owners stay because they value the service. Track cancellation reasons and distinguish retained work from delayed exits.

A long agreement may postpone an exit while leaving the complaint unresolved. Monthly service may expose problems faster. Neither result proves the offer is better without context about service and property outcomes.

Separate owners who sell or move back into a property from owners who leave over service. Record both the stated reason and the evidence. Avoid labeling every sale unavoidable if a long complaint preceded it.

Review retained accounts too. Ask whether owners understand reports, repairs and upcoming work. A silent owner is not necessarily a satisfied owner. Use direct questions tied to recent service rather than a general request for praise.

When testing a term change, compare similar groups and note the limits. An owner with a ready property can cost less to serve than an owner transferring unresolved issues. Keep property condition and service scope in the analysis. Choose a term that supports honest expectations and a workable exit.

## What Should Happen When an Agreement Ends?

A clear exit process should cover active tasks, records and funds regardless of the original term. Ending service is a handoff job.

Assign a lead who coordinates the transfer. Confirm the effective end date, the receiving owner or manager and the communication plan. Identify open repairs and resident concerns before access changes.

Prepare a closing record that lists fees already earned, disputed amounts and funds awaiting settlement. Ask counsel and accounting how applicable trust and deposit rules govern the transfer. Do not hold records or funds merely to pressure an owner over a disputed bill.

Tell residents the correct contact after the handoff. Confirm who handles incoming payments, emergency calls and notices. Keep a route for later questions about work performed during your term.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney. Before you revise the contract length, ask your team to explain the current renewal and exit process from the signed file.
