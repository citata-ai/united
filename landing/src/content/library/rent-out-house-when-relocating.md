## What does a long-distance landlord need?

A long-distance landlord needs a local response plan before the first tenant arrives. Decide who receives requests, who can enter lawfully, who hires a contractor, and how work gets paid. Test that process while you are still nearby.

Start with the home itself. Inspect equipment, resolve known defects, and save service details. Photograph the condition and label shutoffs. An unfamiliar contractor works more easily when the basic information is ready.

Then review permission and coverage. Read the loan documents and ask the servicer about the planned rental use. Check local registration, inspection, and contact requirements with the relevant office. An owner in another city still needs a plan that meets local rules.

NAIC warns that changing a home's use can affect insurance. Tell the insurer that you are moving out and describe the lease plan. Ask for written confirmation of suitable rental coverage.

| Need | Evidence to have before departure |
|---|---|
| Local contact | Named lead and backup with phone numbers |
| Repairs | Contractor list, access plan, and authority |
| Payments | Rent process and reconciled opening balances |
| Insurance | Coverage that reflects the actual use |
| Local rules | Required forms, approvals, and renewal dates |
| Records | Shared folder for leases, bills, and photos |

## Who handles repairs when you are not there?

A named local person must coordinate repairs, even when you keep final budget control. A property manager can provide that service if the agreement includes it. A friend may help, but an informal favor is not a complete operating plan.

Write the response process in plain words. Explain what the tenant should do during an urgent issue and which number to call. Provide the backup path when the first contact cannot answer.

Give your local lead the access tools and property details they need. Confirm where keys are stored, which spaces require building approval, and how contractors identify themselves. Follow local entry law rather than assuming owner permission is enough.

Set spending authority before an emergency. Distinguish routine work, urgent damage control, and large planned projects. A contractor should not need to wait for an overseas owner to wake up before a permitted urgent action.

Require completion evidence proportionate to the job. An invoice, dated photos, and a tenant update can help you check work from afar. Keep open items on a list until someone confirms the result.

## How much does a manager cost for one house?

The full cost comes from the actual management agreement. Ask for a written proposal for your address and planned lease. Do not assume a national average describes the service you need.

List base management, leasing, renewal, inspection, vacant-home, project, and cancellation charges. Ask whether vendor markups or required service packages apply. Confirm whether charges use scheduled rent or collected rent.

Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to test the quote. Keep taxes, insurance, repairs, vacancy, and loan payments in your broader property budget.

For a hypothetical example, assume collected rent of $24,000 per year. An assumed base fee of 8% equals $1,920. Add an assumed $1,000 leasing charge and $300 inspection cost for a total of $3,220.

Those figures are invented inputs, not local price evidence. The example shows why the base percentage is not the full annual cost. Replace every amount with the written proposal and expected lease events.

Also price self-management honestly. Include local task help, travel, missed work, and the time needed to coordinate repairs. A low software bill can hide work that still needs a person.

## When does selling make more sense?

Selling deserves a closer look when the rental needs more cash or attention than you can provide. Compare net sale proceeds with a realistic rental budget. Keep repair needs and access to your equity visible.

Ask for a sale estimate after costs and loan payoff. Then compare a normal rental year with a difficult year. Include a gap between tenants and a known major repair where relevant.

IRS Publication 523 explains the home sale exclusion and its ownership and residence tests. Renting can affect your later sale calculation. Gain tied to depreciation can also receive different treatment.

Ask a tax professional to compare a sale now with the dates you may sell later. Provide purchase, move-out, rental start, and improvement records. Do not assume an exclusion remains available indefinitely.

The decision also depends on your new home budget. A rental reserve is separate from money you need for relocation and your own housing. Keep enough liquidity for the commitments you choose to retain.

## What should you finish before the move?

Finish the work that becomes harder after you leave. Complete the property file and test the tenant contact process. Confirm that your local helper can act without repeated calls for missing information.

- **Review permissions.** Save lender, association, and local office responses.
- **Bind insurance.** Confirm use, ownership, liability, and any vacancy concerns with the insurer.
- **Document condition.** Record repairs, equipment, keys, and move-in photos.
- **Fund the plan.** Set aside cash for approved work and bills before distributions.
- **Assign oversight.** Choose who reviews statements and unresolved requests.
- **Set a review date.** Compare actual results with the budget after the rental begins.

IRS Publication 527 explains rental income and expense records. Keep invoices and transaction detail even when a manager supplies statements. A distribution total alone does not show how each cost should be treated.
