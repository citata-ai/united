## What should I say first?

Ask before you answer. When an owner says "your fee is too high", say: "Fair question. What are you comparing us to, and what does that offer include?" Then listen.

Most fee objections fall into one of four types. Each needs a different reply:

| What the owner means | Clue | Your move |
|---|---|---|
| "I found a lower rate" | Names a percent or a company | Compare total yearly cost |
| "I don't see what you do" | Asks "what do I pay for?" | Show the work log |
| "I can't afford it" | Talks about cash flow or a thin margin | Offer a lower tier with less service |
| "I want to pay less for the same" | Pushes on price after the math | Decide if you let them go |

Do not cut the price in the first call. A quick discount tells the owner the first number was not real.

## How do I compare total cost, not the headline rate?

Compare a full year, with every fee, on the same rent and the same events. A low base fee often comes with add-on fees. All Property Management notes that managers who charge a low monthly percent may pass on more costs for extra services, such as an advertising fee when they place a tenant.

Here is a worked example. The rent is $2,000 a month. In this year, the tenant moves out and a new one moves in, and there are $3,000 of repairs. All fees are example numbers, not market data.

| Cost line | Manager A (you) | Manager B (cheaper rate) |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly fee | 10% = $200 × 11 months = $2,200 | 7% = $140 × 11 months = $1,540 |
| Leasing fee | 50% of one month = $1,000 | 100% of one month = $2,000 |
| Advertising fee | Included | $250 |
| Maintenance markup on $3,000 | None = $0 | 15% = $450 |
| Renewal fee | Not used this year | Not used this year |
| Vacant days | 20 days = $1,315 | 40 days = $2,630 |
| **Total yearly cost** | **$4,515** | **$6,870** |

To keep the model simple, both columns charge the monthly fee for 11 months. Vacant days use $2,000 × 12 ÷ 365 = $65.75 per day.

In this example, the 7% manager costs the owner $2,355 more. The two biggest lines are the leasing fee and vacant days, not the rate. Your own numbers decide the result. If your total is higher, say so and explain why.

Two rules for the comparison:

1. **Use the competitor's written fee schedule.** Ask the owner to share it. Do not guess their fees.
2. **Use your real days-on-market data.** If you claim fewer vacant days, show your last 12 months of placements.

## How do I show the value behind the fee?

Show the work, in numbers, for that owner's property. Owners rarely see what a manager does between statements. Pull a 12-month log:

- [ ] Number of tenant messages answered
- [ ] Number of work orders, and the average days to close
- [ ] Number of inspections, with photo reports
- [ ] Rent collected on time, as a percent of months
- [ ] Renewals done and the rent change at each
- [ ] Vendor bids you got before large repairs
- [ ] Legal notices sent on time

Then name one or two moments that saved the owner money. For example: "In March we caught a slow leak at the inspection. The repair was $380. A ceiling replacement would have cost more."

Buildium's 2013 post by Loretta Morgan makes the same case from the manager side: compete on market knowledge, track record and service standards, not on discounts. Her view is that a lower fee lowers the client's opinion of you.

## When should I offer a lower tier?

Offer a lower tier only when you remove real work from it. A lower price for the same service is a discount, and a discount on every door lowers your margin on every hour.

A simple tier design:

| Tier | Includes | Leaves out | Who it fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full service | Leasing, rent, maintenance, inspections, renewals, notices | Nothing | Owners who want no calls |
| Rent and accounting | Rent collection, owner statements, notices | Maintenance coordination, inspections | Owners near the property who fix things |
| Leasing only | Marketing, showings, screening, lease signing | Everything after move-in | Self-managing owners |

All Property Management describes the leasing-only model as a one-time fee of 50–100% of one month's rent, with no fee after that. Set your own prices from your cost per task.

The owner who chose a cheaper tier now knows what each service costs. Some move up later, after the first late-night repair call.

## When should I let the owner go?

Let the owner go when keeping them costs you more than the fee pays, or puts tenants or your license at risk. Clear signs:

- The owner wants full service at a price below your cost per door.
- The owner refuses repairs that affect habitability or safety.
- Every invoice turns into a dispute, and staff time on the account is two or three times your average.
- The owner asks you to bend screening rules or fair housing rules.

How to exit well:

1. **Check the agreement.** Use the notice period and terms in your management agreement.
2. **Put it in writing.** Give the end date and the handover plan.
3. **Hand over on time.** Send the tenant ledger, lease, deposit records and keys. Transfer the security deposit as your state law requires.
4. **Stay polite.** Owners talk to other owners. A clean exit protects your referrals.

## What do other managers do about fee pressure?

Many managers add services instead of fees. In AppFolio's 2025 benchmark survey, as the National Apartment Association reports, 36% of fee managers planned to offer owners more services. Only 24% planned new fees for owners, down from 25% in 2024.
