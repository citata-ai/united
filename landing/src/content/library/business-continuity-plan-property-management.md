## Which data and systems need backup?

Back up the records and access needed to keep essential work safe during a disruption. Start with tasks rather than software brands. Identify what staff need to handle an urgent report, locate a property and account for money.

Ready.gov's Ready Business guidance includes communication, technology recovery and continuity planning. It also treats training and exercises as part of preparedness. Use that structure to build a plan your team can use under pressure.

List emergency contacts, property access instructions, vendor routes, current leases and accounting records. Define where a protected copy lives and who can retrieve it. A backup that nobody can open does not support recovery.

Do not put passwords or access codes in a broadly shared emergency document. Use controlled access and a reviewed recovery process. Test that a backup staff member can reach the approved records without borrowing another person's account.

Keep the plan for a physical emergency separate from a cyber incident where systems may be unsafe. The FTC's breach guide recommends expert response and evidence preservation. Do not reconnect systems merely because routine work is waiting.

Use the following generic plan as a starting checklist. Adapt it to the properties, local hazards and legal duties. Check local law before using it as an operating policy.

## Who contacts owners and residents during an event?

A named communications lead should coordinate updates, with a backup ready to take over. Separate safety instructions from account and scheduling updates. Staff need a consistent message and a clear route for new information.

Keep the detailed contact and access records in protected storage. The plan should point authorized people to them rather than expose them to every recipient.

## How do I keep paying owners and vendors?

Keep payments moving only through a verified, authorized process that still works during the event. Identify bank contacts and backup approvers before an outage. Do not make shared credentials the recovery method.

Record which payments can pause and who decides. Check the agreement, bank process and applicable rules with the responsible professionals. A continuity plan should not invent authority that staff do not have.

Use a controlled list of pending payments when the normal software is unavailable. Record approvals and completed transfers so the team can reconcile later. Prevent duplicate payment when the main system returns.

The FBI's business email compromise guidance recommends independent verification of changed payment instructions. Keep that control during an emergency. An urgent request to use a new account deserves the same check as an ordinary change.

If the event involves suspicious transfers, contact the bank through a known route and follow the incident process. Assign a person to preserve the original instructions and payment details. Do not promise successful recovery.

Keep company funds and client-held funds distinct in the recovery record. Ask the accountant and broker to review any manual procedure before use. This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## How often should I test the plan?

Test the plan on a risk-based schedule and after material changes to staff, systems or service scope. Do not assume a document review proves that the process works. Use a scenario that requires people to act.

Start with a normal system unavailable and the usual lead absent. Ask the backup to find the contact route, identify open urgent work and locate the required records. Observe the process rather than coaching every step.

Add a payment-change request to a later exercise. Check whether staff verify it through the trusted route and preserve separate approval. The exercise should reveal pressure to bypass controls.

For a cyber scenario, use the FTC's guidance to frame specialist involvement and evidence preservation. Staff should know who can approve recovery and public updates. They should not improvise technical cleanup.

Record each gap with a person responsible for fixing it. Repeat the failed step after the fix. Mark a plan item complete only when the backup can perform the work.

Use harmless test data and tell the relevant leads how the exercise will run. Do not create a real payment, false resident emergency or unsafe property access to test a procedure.

## What should the recovery review produce?

The recovery review should produce corrected records, closed gaps and a clear decision to resume normal work. Reconcile manual payments and service activity with restored systems. Check for duplicates and missing requests.

Ask owners, residents and vendors which messages they received and what remained unclear. Use that feedback to improve contact routes. Keep incident facts separate from speculation.

Review whether the backup roles had enough authority and access. Change the process when a task depended on the absent founder. Train the new backup before marking the gap closed.
