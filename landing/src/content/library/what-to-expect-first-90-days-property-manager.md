## What does property manager onboarding include?

Onboarding should establish who acts, which records transfer and how money will be tracked. Do not judge the start only by whether the owner portal works. Ask for a written plan that you and the manager can check together.

California DRE's Property Management chapter recommends a written contract with clear duties, fees and authority. Use that guidance to confirm your own agreement. Name the start date, repair approval process, reserve arrangement and reporting schedule before service begins.

The following is a suggested owner review plan. These dates are planning targets, not statutory deadlines or measured industry standards.

| Review point | Suggested result | Evidence to request |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Start | Contacts and authority confirmed | Signed agreement and contact sheet |
| Early handover | Records and funds checked | Document list and opening balances |
| First reporting cycle | Income and bills traceable | Statement, invoices and payment record |
| Midpoint review | Open repairs assigned | Work log with status and next action |
| Day 90 | Service compared with scope | Written review and unresolved-item list |

Ask who takes tenant calls during the transfer. Identify the cutoff for old and new payment channels. Confirm how the manager will avoid conflicting instructions. Do not leave that change to an automated welcome email alone.

Request a baseline condition review. Ask for dated photos and a list of known defects, access issues and open work. That record helps separate inherited problems from work that occurs after the manager starts.

## What documents should you give the manager?

Give the manager records that explain the tenancy, money, property and current obligations. Send a list first, then use the agreed secure transfer route. Mark missing records instead of filling gaps with estimates.

Start with the signed lease, addenda, renewal terms and tenant ledger. Include the deposit amount, holder and supporting records. Add move-in photos, inspection reports and notices that remain open. Provide only the personal information needed for the work.

The North Carolina Real Estate Commission's takeover guidance lists management agreements, leases, owner statements and paid invoices among important rental files. It also calls for review of records that identify deposits and owner reserves. Use those categories as a handover checklist.

Add property records that a lease cannot provide. Include utility responsibilities, appliance details, warranties, keys and emergency shutoff information. If the home belongs to an association, provide the current rules and management contacts.

Give the manager your insurance contact and relevant policy details. Identify open claims and work already approved. Tell the manager which vendors have keys or pending appointments. Ask the manager to confirm receipt of each active case.

Keep a document gap list with an action owner. If the former manager holds the records, name who will request them. Agree how unresolved balances will be shown until verified. A clear unknown is more useful than an invented opening figure.

## When should the first owner statement arrive?

Your first statement should arrive on the date agreed with the manager, using a clearly stated cutoff. Confirm that date before handover. A statement date and a bank payout date may differ, so ask about both.

California DRE recommends monthly accounting and a detailed annual statement in its reference chapter. That supports a useful service expectation. It does not establish a national rule that your first payout must arrive on a particular day.

Ask the manager to explain the first statement line by line. Check opening funds, rent received, bills paid, management fees, reserve changes and owner distributions. Match each payment to an invoice or ledger entry. Ask for a correction when a label hides the transaction.

Use a simple review equation: opening owner funds plus receipts, less bills and distributions, should explain closing owner funds. Keep tenant deposits distinct from funds available for owner payment. Ask the manager how the report shows liabilities and restricted money.

The North Carolina Commission's takeover guidance stresses reconciliation of bank, journal and ledger records. You do not need access to other owners' private data. Ask for the records that explain your balance and confirmation that the manager completed its reconciliation.

A delayed payout needs an explanation tied to actual events. Ask whether rent cleared, a bill remains unpaid or a reserve top-up was agreed. Compare that answer with the contract. Escalate an unexplained balance instead of accepting a vague software problem.

## What should you review at day 90?

Review service quality against the written scope, opening condition and agreed work plan. Do not use a short period's cash result alone. A necessary repair can reduce cash while fixing a problem that existed before the handover.

Check the repair log first. For each open item, request the status, last action and next step. Confirm that completed work has supporting invoices and photos where useful. Ask why any urgent issue remains open.

Review communication from the tenant's side as well as your own. Did the manager provide the correct contact route? Did requests receive a clear response? Ask for the case record when a tenant reports a missed request.

Then compare the actual fees with the agreement. Separate recurring management charges from leasing, maintenance and other charges. Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to review the expected budget. Investigate differences before assuming a fee error or accepting an extra charge.

End the review with a short written action list. Give every unresolved item a named contact and agreed target date. If the service does not meet the agreement, review the contract's correction and termination terms with a local attorney.

Keep the baseline records and review notes for the next cycle. Your goal is a service you can check through records. A friendly call helps, but clear balances and completed work give you a better basis for the next decision.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.
