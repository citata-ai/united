## How do costs and HOA fees compare?

Compare the full owner cost rather than the purchase price or mortgage alone. A condo's association dues can cover shared work and services. A house may require you to arrange and fund those items directly.

Use the same categories for each property:

| Cost or decision | Condo review | Single-family review |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shared upkeep | What dues cover | Owner's direct contracts |
| Major work | Association plans and assessments | Roof, exterior, and site plan |
| Insurance | Master policy and unit coverage | Rental-use building coverage |
| Rental permission | Governing documents and approval | Deed, association, and local checks |
| Management | Unit service and building coordination | Full property service scope |

A house can also belong to an association. Read the actual documents instead of assuming the property type settles the rules.

Ask for budgets, financial statements, reserve reports, and planned projects. Check whether a low monthly fee postpones needed work. For the house, get comparable repair quotes and a replacement plan. Put both sets of expected costs into the same cash worksheet before you compare returns.

## How do rent and tenant demand compare?

Use local lease evidence for the specific condo and house. Property type alone cannot prove stronger demand or higher rent. Ask what tenants actually leased and what the rent included.

Compare condition, floor area, parking, laundry, storage, outdoor space, and access. Note utility costs and amenities. A larger advertised rent may include services you must pay for from that rent.

For the condo, ask how the building handles move-in access, elevator bookings, deliveries, and tenant communication. Confirm whether the lease must follow a building process. Add any documented owner cost to the budget.

For the house, define lawn care, snow removal, utilities, and service access in the operating plan. A larger site can add work even if the interior is simple. Do not assign a tenant duty without checking the agreement and local requirements.

Describe property features without guessing which protected group should live there. Market the home, price, access, and services. Ask a local manager for evidence behind the rent range and the expected leasing process. Keep the same vacancy assumptions until you have support for a difference.

## Who pays for exterior repairs?

The governing documents and service arrangements determine who handles each repair. Do not assume the condo association pays for every outside problem. Do not assume a house has no shared obligations.

For a condo, ask for a written boundary between the unit and common areas. Review windows, balconies, doors, plumbing, roof, and shared systems. Ask who handles damage that crosses that boundary.

Get an insurance review at the same time. Ask how the master policy and your own policy fit together. Confirm deductibles and any gaps using the policy documents, not a broad description from the listing agent.

IRS Publication 527 distinguishes condominium maintenance assessments from assessments for improvements. That tax distinction makes a detailed association notice useful. Save the purpose and work scope when you receive an assessment.

For a house, inspect the roof, drainage, exterior, heating, and site. Make a replacement schedule from the inspection and quotes. Use the [management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) if you want a manager to arrange the work. A management fee does not fund the repair itself. Keep the replacement reserve separate.

## How do appreciation and resale compare?

Compare recent relevant sales and the buyer pool for each property. Neither condos nor houses have a guaranteed appreciation path. Treat a future sale price as a scenario rather than income you already earned.

For the condo, ask the lender whether the project and unit meet the loan product's requirements. A personal approval does not settle every property issue. Read the project records before you assume an easy financed resale.

For the house, check condition, title issues, and expected selling work. A property that rents well may still need work before sale. Include those costs instead of comparing gross sale values.

Use the same exit date for both properties. Deduct selling costs, debt payoff, and a tax estimate. Test a flat value case before adding expected growth. If the rental needs appreciation to avoid a poor result, state that dependence clearly.

Fannie Mae's occupancy guide distinguishes principal residences from investment properties and applies investment pricing adjustments. Tell the lender your true use. Do not use an owner-occupied mortgage quote to compare a house or condo you intend to rent without living there.

## What should you review before buying?

Build a document file for each candidate and list the missing facts. A condo review should include rental rules, association finances, insurance, repair boundaries, and planned work. A house review should include equivalent direct operating costs.

Ask a local attorney to check documents that control your right to rent. Ask an insurance agent to quote the planned use. Ask a lender to confirm the property and loan fit before you remove financing protections.

Then compare the same operating year. Include rent, vacancy, debt, taxes, insurance, management, repairs, and replacement savings. Keep special assessments visible rather than spreading them away without explanation.

Choose the home whose costs and duties you can understand and fund. A smaller purchase price or a simpler exterior can help. Neither feature replaces a complete review of the actual property you would own.
