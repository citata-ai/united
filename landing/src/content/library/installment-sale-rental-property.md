## What is an installment sale under IRS rules?

An installment sale includes a payment received after the tax year of sale. IRS Publication 537 uses that definition. Seller financing may fit, but the loan contract and the tax method remain separate questions.

Publication 537 says eligible gain generally follows the installment method unless you elect out. The method cannot spread a loss. Special rules also apply to some transactions, including sales of depreciable property to certain related people. Ask your preparer to test eligibility before setting terms.

A payment usually includes interest, a return of basis and sale gain. Basis means your tax investment in the property. Principal is not all taxable profit. Interest is not part of the gross profit percentage used to calculate installment gain.

The following illustration applies the method in IRS Publication 537. All dollar amounts are assumptions. Assume no debt, selling costs, ordinary recapture or special adjustments.

| Item | Illustrative amount |
| --- | --- |
| Sale price | $300,000 |
| Adjusted basis | $180,000 |
| Gross profit | $120,000 |
| Contract price | $300,000 |
| Gross profit percentage | 40% |
| Principal received during the year | $30,000 |
| Installment gain for that year | $12,000 |

The remaining principal received returns basis in this simplified example. Interest is reported separately under the applicable rules. A real sale can involve assumed debt and expenses that change the calculation. Have your preparer complete the actual worksheet.

## Is depreciation recapture spread across payments?

Ordinary depreciation recapture is taxable in the sale year, even when cash arrives later. IRS Publication 537 states that rule. A small down payment can therefore leave you with tax due before you collect enough cash.

However, ordinary recapture does not mean every gain amount linked to depreciation. IRS Publication 544 explains section 1250 property and additional depreciation. A building depreciated using the straight-line method can have a different result from assets subject to ordinary recapture.

The IRS Schedule D instructions separately address unrecaptured section 1250 gain in installment sales. They generally allocate that gain to installment gain received until the relevant amount is used. Do not assume the tax character spreads evenly across all payments.

Ask your preparer to separate land, building and other assets in the sale. Provide the full depreciation schedule, including improvements and equipment. A single sale price does not remove the need to identify the assets sold.

Also ask for a sale-year tax cash estimate. Include the down payment, expenses paid at closing and any loan payoff. Compare the cash left with the projected tax due. Review the payment plan again if the sale creates a shortfall.

Do not promise a buyer that a lower down payment works because you can defer all tax. First obtain the calculation. Then choose terms that leave room for tax, closing costs and your own needs.

## What happens if the buyer defaults?

Buyer default can interrupt your income and create legal, repair and collection costs. Treat the buyer as a credit risk before you treat the note as income. A signed promise to pay is not the same as collected cash.

Ask an attorney to review the security documents, lien position and remedies. Confirm the title, existing debt and any lender consent issue before closing. A seller note cannot resolve restrictions in the current loan by itself.

Request evidence of the buyer's funds, income and ability to maintain the property. Use lawful underwriting standards. Ask how payments, insurance and taxes will be tracked. A loan servicer may help with records, but it does not remove your credit risk.

IRS Publication 537 has separate rules for repossession. Taking the property back can create tax results that differ from simply reversing the sale. Send the original sale records and payment history to your preparer before agreeing to a workout.

Build a default file from the first payment. Keep the note, security documents, payment ledger and insurance records together. Save every notice and borrower request. Ask counsel about the proper response when a payment fails.

Do not take possession, change locks or cancel rights based on a generic online form. The remedy depends on state law and the transaction. Budget for the time and cost of lawful enforcement before you accept the risk.

## What terms should you negotiate?

Negotiate terms that match the buyer's ability to pay and your need for cash. There is no single standard down payment or interest rate for every rental sale. Compare the full payment schedule with a cash sale before accepting a price premium.

Review the purchase price, down payment, interest rate, amortization and final maturity date. Confirm whether the last payment is a large balloon amount. Ask what evidence supports the buyer's plan to pay that amount when due.

IRS Publication 537 explains that insufficient stated interest can trigger unstated interest or original issue discount rules. A nominal rate does not guarantee a low tax bill. Have your tax professional check the interest terms before signing.

Consumer lending rules may apply when the buyer will use the home as a dwelling. CFPB Regulation Z section 1026.36 includes seller-financer exclusions with specific conditions. Those provisions are not a blanket exemption from lending law. Have counsel review the buyer's purpose and the proposed loan.

Request a draft closing statement and written servicing plan. Name who collects payments, tracks principal and interest, confirms insurance and handles late payments. Confirm how the note will pass to your estate if you die before repayment.

For the wider ownership budget, use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) before comparing a sale with continued rental use. Take the resulting cash plan to your tax professional and attorney. Set the terms only after both reviews.
