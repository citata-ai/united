## Where do I find a property management book of business to buy?

The best sellers are usually close to home. Buildium (2026) advises you to start with local competitors whose owners are near retirement or burned out. A local book also fits your vendors, your team and your drive times.

Use these four channels, in this order:

1. **Call local owner-operators.** Make a list of every small manager in your market. Ask each owner one plain question: "Have you thought about your exit?"
2. **Tell your network.** Buildium notes that many deals start from casual talks at conferences and association events.
3. **Use a broker who knows the niche.** Brokers who work on property management deals bring sellers you will not find alone.
4. **Watch for opportunistic deals.** Buildium separates strategic deals (part of a plan) from opportunistic ones (a competitor suddenly wants out). Be ready for both.

Smaller local buyers can win against roll-ups. A 2025 summary by PMAssist of Peter Lohmann's M&A report says smaller buyers paid higher multiples on average than roll-up buyers. Sellers who talk only to national firms may get less.

## Asset purchase or stock purchase: which fits a book of business?

A book of business is usually an asset purchase. You buy the management agreements, and the seller keeps the company and its past liabilities. In a stock purchase you buy the entity itself, with its debts and obligations (Buildium, 2026).

| Point | Asset purchase | Stock purchase |
|---|---|---|
| What you buy | Agreements, and named equipment or files | The whole company |
| Past liabilities | Usually stay with the seller | Come with the company |
| Owner consent | Needed when an agreement bars assignment | Contracting entity does not change |
| Brand and vendor links | You decide what to keep | Stay in place |
| Common for | Small books, tuck-ins | Larger firms |

Second Nature (2024) adds a practical warning. If an agreement is not assignable, you need a new agreement with that owner. CT Acquisitions (2026) says buyers of larger firms often prefer a stock or membership-interest sale for this reason.

Read every management agreement before you sign a letter of intent. Count how many need owner consent and how many owners can leave on short notice.

This is general information, not legal or tax advice. The tax result of each structure differs. Ask a business attorney and a tax professional before you choose one.

## How do I price a property management book?

Price the book on earnings, then check the result per door. CT Acquisitions (2026) gives these ranges:

- Owner-operated firm: about 2.5x to 3x SDE (seller's discretionary earnings, profit before owner pay).
- Per-door cross-check: about $200 to $500 per door for residential books.
- Management-fee revenue check: about 0.75x to 1.5x.

Then adjust for risk. A 2012 Buildium post by Jo-Anne Oliveri says doors signed in the last 12 months carry the most risk. Those owners have little loyalty to the seller yet.

Here is a worked holdback example. The numbers are an example, not data.

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Doors in the book | 150 |
| Agreed price | $300,000 ($2,000 per door) |
| Paid at closing (80%) | $240,000 |
| Holdback (20%) | $60,000 |
| Retention window | 12 months |
| Doors lost to a manager switch | 9 |
| Holdback cut (9 × $2,000) | $18,000 |
| Holdback paid to seller | $42,000 |
| Total paid | $282,000 |

CT Acquisitions describes this type of structure: buyers "rarely pay the full price in cash at closing" for a small or owner-run firm. They hold back part of the price or pay an earnout based on retained doors or fee revenue.

Negotiate three terms in writing:

1. **The length of the window.** Match it to the notice periods in the agreements you buy.
2. **Which departures count.** An owner who sells the house is not the same as an owner who switches managers.
3. **Offsets.** Decide if new doors from the seller's referrals offset lost doors.

## How do I pay for the purchase?

You do not need all cash. Buildium (2026) lists cash, SBA loans and bank loans, seller financing and private investors. Seller financing keeps the seller invested in a smooth handover.

Compare the price with organic growth first. PMAssist (2025) estimates that a property management company spends about $1,500 all-in to win one new door organically. Todd Ortscheid, who wrote that post, says the average deal in the Lohmann report cost well above that per door.

## How do I keep the owners after closing?

Owner trust decides the deal's return. CT Acquisitions calls a joint letter the best tool you have for keeping doors through the retention window.

Use this 30-day plan after closing:

- [ ] **Day 1:** Send a joint letter from seller and buyer. Introduce the new team, confirm that fees and terms do not change, and name one contact.
- [ ] **Week 1:** Call the 20 largest owners yourself.
- [ ] **Week 1:** Keep key staff. Buildium suggests retention bonuses for the people owners know.
- [ ] **Week 2:** Send tenants a written notice with the new payment method and contact.
- [ ] **Week 2–4:** Move data to one software platform in stages, not in one weekend.
- [ ] **Day 30:** Send the first owner statement from your system and check it line by line.

Buildium also warns against rushed integration. Fast system moves cause billing errors, and billing errors make owners leave.

If you plan to change fees later, wait until the retention window closes. Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to test the effect first.

Before you sign, run a full check of the seller's trust accounts, agreements and churn history.
