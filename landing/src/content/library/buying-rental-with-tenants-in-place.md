## What is an estoppel certificate?

A tenant estoppel certificate records the tenant's confirmation of current lease facts. iPropertyManagement describes it as a signed statement used during a sale or refinance. Ask your attorney which form and process fit the property.

Use the confirmation to compare the tenant's account with the seller's file. Ask about rent, term, deposit, prepaid rent, addenda, promised work, and disputed credits. Do not ask the tenant to certify a statement you already know is incomplete.

The certificate does not replace the lease or your own review. Compare signed documents, receipts, and the property's condition. If the records disagree, identify the cause before treating the rental income as secure.

Build this review file:

| Evidence | What to compare |
| --- | --- |
| Lease and addenda | Rent, term, rights, and duties |
| Rent ledger | Charges, receipts, and credits |
| Bank or processor records | Money actually received |
| Tenant confirmation | Differences from the seller's account |
| Repair history | Open work and repeated problems |
| Deposit records | Amount held and required transfer |

Ask counsel about the legal effect of the certificate and whether the lease requires a response. Do not invent a national deadline or pressure tenants to sign away a dispute just to keep the closing date.

## How do security deposits transfer?

Security deposit transfer follows local law and the closing arrangements. Reconcile the deposit ledger before closing and have counsel confirm the required transfer and notices. A seller's credit alone may not describe the full process.

Washington RCW 59.18.270 requires affected deposit funds to move to an equivalent successor trust account when landlord status transfers. The successor must promptly notify the tenant about the transfer and new depository. These are Washington rules, not a national model.

California Civil Code 1950.5 provides a different transfer-or-return process. Its transfer route includes notice about the deposit and successor. Read the full applicable provision with local counsel rather than importing Washington's account rule.

Prepare a schedule by tenant showing deposits, prepaid rent, credits, and any separate balances. Match it to the leases, receipts, and tenant confirmations. Resolve a missing receipt or disputed amount before signing the closing statement.

Keep proof that the agreed money and records reached the right recipient. Ask who sends each required notice and who keeps the receipt. A clear handoff protects the tenant's records as well as your own. Do not merge deposit money into the operating budget as cash available for repairs.

## Can you change lease terms after buying?

Do not assume a change of owner gives you a right to rewrite an existing lease. Have counsel review the lease, local law, and proposed change. Your purchase plan should start from verified current terms.

List the changes you hope to make, such as rent, utility duties, payment route, or service arrangements. Separate administrative contact updates from substantive lease changes. Ask which steps need consent, notice, a renewal, or another legal process.

Do not underwrite the purchase only from hoped-for future rent. Use actual current collections for the base case. Add a separate scenario for a lawful future change and state the conditions clearly.

If you intend to live in the home, tell your lender and attorney before closing. Check whether the existing occupancy fits your loan and possession plan. Do not promise the lender a move-in date based on an assumption that tenants will leave.

Fannie Mae's rental income guidance considers the lease and other evidence when a lender reviews rent. The lender's acceptance does not replace your legal review. Ask both advisers to explain their separate requirements so the financing and the tenant plan remain consistent.

## What should you tell tenants after closing?

Tenants need clear, verified ownership and service information after the sale closes. Prepare the communication before closing, then send it through the process counsel approves. Do not announce a transfer that has not occurred.

State the new contact, repair route, emergency contact, and payment instructions where appropriate. Identify the effective date and explain how tenants can verify the notice. Coordinate with the seller so messages do not conflict.

Ask counsel to confirm any required owner, agent, deposit, or depository notices. Use the local rules rather than a generic letter from another state. Save proof of delivery with the transfer records.

Confirm the status of open repairs and promised work. Give tenants a practical way to report missing information. Keep the first messages focused on continuity and accurate records rather than immediate changes to every process.

If a manager will handle the property, make the role clear. Confirm access to leases, ledgers, keys, and service history before the first rent cycle. Use the [management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to check service costs while you compare firms, then put the actual terms in the agreement.

## What should stop or delay the purchase?

Unresolved lease, deposit, collection, or repair gaps deserve attention before closing. Ask for the missing evidence and have the purchase team explain the effect. Do not replace a missing record with an optimistic assumption.

Compare the property cash plan with the verified tenancy facts. Include unpaid amounts, concessions, upcoming work, insurance, management, and cash needed at takeover. Keep a difficult case with lower collections and a repair delay.

Ask the seller to identify all service contracts and any obligations that could carry forward. Have counsel review assignment and termination terms. Confirm which vendors you will retain and which contracts need a new agreement.

Your next step is a signed-off tenant handoff schedule attached to the closing plan. Name who transfers money, documents, keys, and notices. Close only after you understand the current tenancy and can fund the property under its actual terms.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.
