## Is a 10% maintenance markup normal?

A 10% maintenance markup shows up in real files, but we found no reliable national survey that sets a "normal" rate. So we do not give one. What we can show is what regulators see in real files.

A California Department of Real Estate (DRE) accusation filed on 2026-04-14 (case H-07502 SAC) describes markups of 10%, 15% or 20% of vendor invoices. The audit covered a broker who managed about 41 residential properties from 2023 to 2024. An accusation is a set of claims, not a final ruling.

The invoices in that accusation show how fast a markup adds up:

| Vendor invoice | Markup to broker | Rate |
|---|---|---|
| $195.00 | $29.25 | 15% |
| $541.24 | $54.12 | 10% |
| $655.95 | $98.39 | 15% |
| $436.08 | $65.41 | 15% |

The rate was not the cited problem. The accusation says the owner statements showed the vendor amount and the markup as 1 payment to the vendor. The markup was not disclosed as its own line. The agreements only said a "modest markup added to each invoice billed to Owner".

## What did regulators cite brokers for?

California regulators cite brokers for markups and vendor money that owners could not see. The legal hook is Business and Professions Code section 10176(g). It lets the Real Estate Commissioner suspend or revoke a license for "the claiming or taking by a licensee of any secret or undisclosed amount of compensation, commission, or profit".

Two DRE accusations show the pattern:

- **H-07502 SAC (2026).** Undisclosed 10% to 20% repair markups, cited under 10176(g). The same file also cites an unlicensed entity, unlicensed business names and a trust account not held in the broker's name as trustee.
- **H-39498 LA (2014).** The agreement did not allow maintenance markups. The accusation also alleges $419,337.99 in "Volume Discount" payments from 2 repair vendors to the management company, for work on owner properties, with no written disclosure to owners.

Each case is an accusation, so the outcome may differ. The point for your company is simple: the risk comes from hidden money, not from charging for your work.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## How do I disclose a markup in the management agreement?

You disclose a markup by writing the rate, the scope and the statement format into the management agreement. Buildium's agreement guide gives the same advice: if you charge markups, state the percentage and which types of bills it applies to.

Some states also set rules on what an agreement must state. Arizona Revised Statutes 32-2173 says a property management agreement shall "state the terms and conditions of compensation the property owner pays for services". A markup is compensation, so it belongs in that list.

A clear markup clause covers these points:

- [ ] **Rate.** For example, "10% of the vendor invoice amount".
- [ ] **Scope.** Which bills: repairs, turnovers, capital projects, or all vendor work.
- [ ] **Cap.** An optional dollar cap per invoice, so large jobs do not get a large markup.
- [ ] **Exclusions.** Utilities, taxes, insurance and HOA dues are usually pass-through costs.
- [ ] **Statement line.** "The markup appears as a separate line on each owner statement."
- [ ] **In-house work.** If your own staff or an affiliate does the work, say so and state the rate.
- [ ] **Vendor money.** "Manager receives no rebate, referral fee or volume discount from vendors", or a full disclosure of what you receive.

Then make your software match the clause. A clause that says "separate line" fails if your statement template still merges the two amounts.

## Markup or flat coordination fee: which is easier to defend?

A flat coordination fee is easier to explain, and a percent markup scales better with large jobs. Both are fine when the owner agreed in writing and can see them.

| Factor | Percent markup | Flat coordination fee |
|---|---|---|
| Owner understands it | Medium | High |
| Pays you more on large jobs | Yes | No |
| Pays you fairly on small jobs | No: 10% of $95 is $9.50 | Yes |
| Looks like a conflict of interest | Higher: pay grows with spend | Lower |
| Statement format | Separate line per invoice | Separate line per work order |
| Best fit | Turnovers, larger repairs | Routine work orders |

A mixed model also works: a flat fee per work order up to a set amount, then a percent above it. Whatever you pick, use one method per bill type. Two methods on one invoice confuse owners.

## Must I disclose vendor volume discounts?

You should disclose any money a vendor pays you because of owner work. That includes volume discounts, rebates, referral fees and earnings credits. California's 10176(g) covers "any secret or undisclosed amount of compensation", and the 2014 DRE accusation treated vendor volume discounts that way.

The same 2014 file also alleges an undisclosed bank "earnings credit" on trust accounts that cut the company's own bank charges. Interest and credits on trust money are a disclosure topic too. Arizona's 32-2173 requires the agreement to provide for "the disposition and allocation of interest earned on trust account monies".

Steps to stay clean:

1. **List every vendor deal.** Rebates, discounts, gift cards and referral pay.
2. **Pass it to the owner or disclose it.** Pick one and write it down.
3. **Check your bank.** Ask if your trust accounts earn interest or credits.
4. **Audit 10 statements.** Each markup and fee must show as its own line.
5. **Repeat each year.** New vendors bring new deals.
