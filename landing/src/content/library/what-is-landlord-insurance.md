## What does landlord insurance cover?

Landlord insurance covers the rental building, the owner's liability, and in most policies the rent lost during repairs. The Insurance Information Institute (Triple-I) calls it a "landlord or rental dwelling policy".

Typical parts of a landlord policy:

| Part | What it pays for | Example |
|------|-----------------|---------|
| Dwelling | Physical damage to the structure from covered perils | Kitchen fire damages walls and cabinets |
| Other structures | Detached garage, shed, fence | Wind knocks down a fence |
| Owner's property on site | Items the owner leaves for tenants | Appliances the owner supplies |
| Liability | Legal and medical costs if someone is hurt and sues | A guest falls on a broken step |
| Loss of rent | Rent lost while the unit cannot be rented | Unit empty 3 months after a fire |

Triple-I notes that "Most landlord policies provide coverage for loss of rental income," usually for a set period while repairs make the unit unrentable.

What landlord insurance does not cover:

- **The tenant's belongings.** Triple-I says: "Your tenant's personal possessions are not covered under your policy."
- **Flood and earthquake,** unless you buy separate coverage.
- **Normal wear and tear** and maintenance problems.
- **Unpaid rent from a tenant who stops paying.** That is a different product, rent guarantee insurance.

## Is homeowners insurance enough for a rental?

Homeowners insurance is usually not enough for a long-term rental. Triple-I says that if you lease a home for about 6 months or a year, "you will likely need a landlord or rental dwelling policy." A homeowners policy is written for a home the owner lives in.

Cost: Triple-I says landlord policies "generally cost about 25 percent more than a standard homeowners policy." The extra cost pays for added protections, such as lost rent and rental liability.

Risk of the wrong policy: if you rent out the home on a homeowners policy, the insurer may deny a claim. Tell your insurer before the first tenant moves in. Short-term rentals, such as nightly stays, often need a different policy again.

## What is dwelling fire insurance?

Dwelling fire insurance is the standard policy type for rental homes, and it comes in 3 forms: DP-1, DP-2 and DP-3. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) defines dwelling fire policies as coverage for dwellings, other detached structures and contents, caused by specified perils. It notes they may also give liability coverage.

The NAIC's 2025 data call defines the 3 forms:

| Form | NAIC name | What it covers |
|------|-----------|----------------|
| DP-1 | Basic Form | Named perils, such as fire, lightning and windstorm |
| DP-2 | Broad Form | DP-1 perils plus more named perils, such as falling objects, weight of snow and vandalism |
| DP-3 | Special Form | "All-risks" for the dwelling: all perils except those the policy excludes, such as floods or earthquakes |

How to read the forms:

- **Named perils (DP-1, DP-2).** The policy pays only for the causes it lists. If the cause is not on the list, there is no coverage.
- **All-risks (DP-3).** The policy pays for any cause it does not exclude. This is broader, and it usually costs more.

Also ask how the policy pays a claim. Actual cash value subtracts wear and age. Replacement cost pays to rebuild with new material. Forms and payment terms differ by insurer, so read the policy.

## What does loss-of-rent cover pay?

Loss-of-rent cover pays the rent you lose while a covered loss makes the unit unrentable. Policies call it loss of rents, fair rental value or rental income coverage. It pays for a set period or up to a dollar limit.

Worked example, with made-up numbers:

| Line | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Monthly rent | $1,900 |
| Repair time after a covered fire | 4 months |
| Rent lost | $7,600 |
| Policy limit for loss of rent | 12 months or $20,000 |
| Paid by the policy (before any waiting period) | $7,600 |

Watch for: the limit and any waiting period. A policy with a 6-month limit would pay only $11,400 on a 7-month rebuild, if the rent is $1,900. The cause of loss must also be covered. Lost rent after a flood is not paid if the policy excludes flood.

Loss-of-rent cover does not pay when a tenant just stops paying. For that risk, see rent guarantee products.

## Why should tenants also have renters insurance?

Tenants need renters insurance because the landlord's policy does not cover their belongings or their own liability. Triple-I notes that "many landlords require a tenant to buy renters insurance before signing a lease" to avoid disputes over damaged belongings.

What renters insurance covers, per Triple-I:

- **Personal belongings** against perils such as fire, smoke, lightning, vandalism and theft. Floods and earthquakes are excluded.
- **Liability** for injury or damage the tenant, family or pets cause to others. Limits "generally start at about $100,000."
- **Additional living expenses** if a covered loss makes the home unlivable.

Cost: Triple-I, citing the NAIC, reports the average renters insurance premium was $171 in 2022.

How renters insurance helps the owner:

- A tenant's kitchen fire can be paid from the tenant's liability coverage, not only from the owner's policy.
- Fewer disputes after a loss, because the tenant has their own claim.
- A tenant with a place to stay during repairs is less likely to break the lease.

## Can a landlord require renters insurance?

Many landlords require renters insurance in the lease, and Triple-I describes this as common. Rules can differ by state, city and housing program, so check them before you add the clause. Write the requirement in the lease, set a minimum liability amount, and ask for proof each year.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## Landlord insurance checklist

- **Form.** DP-1, DP-2 or DP-3? Named perils or all-risks?
- **Dwelling limit.** Is it enough to rebuild at today's costs?
- **Payment basis.** Replacement cost or actual cash value?
- **Liability limit.** Many owners add an umbrella policy.
- **Loss of rent.** Months or dollar limit, and any waiting period.
- **Exclusions.** Flood, earthquake, mold, vacancy rules.
- **Vacancy clause.** Some policies limit coverage if the unit is empty for a long time.
- **Tenant rule.** Lease requires renters insurance, with proof on file.
