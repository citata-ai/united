## How does a low rate change rental cash flow?

A lower loan payment leaves more rent available after debt service. That helps only if the property earns enough rent and you account for all costs. The old purchase price does not determine today's achievable rent.

Start with the current loan statement. Separate principal and interest from any escrow payment. Then list taxes and insurance outside the loan line. This avoids counting those bills twice or leaving them out entirely.

The following example uses invented planning inputs, not market data. It shows how to test the property without assuming a rent forecast.

| Monthly item | Hypothetical amount |
|---|---:|
| Collected rent after vacancy allowance | $2,400 |
| Property tax | $350 |
| Insurance | $150 |
| Repairs and capital reserve transfer | $300 |
| Management and other operating costs | $250 |
| Principal and interest payment | $1,000 |
| Cash left after listed items | $350 |

The worked result is $2,400 minus $350, $150, $300, $250, and $1,000. That leaves $350 per month, or $4,200 per year. These are assumed costs, not recommended reserve levels.

The result is cash after a reserve transfer, before income tax. A tax return uses different rules. IRS Publication 527 explains why depreciation, improvements, and loan costs need separate treatment.

## What does the low rate not cover?

A low rate does not pay for repairs, vacancies, insurance changes, or property tax changes. Those costs can rise while the loan payment stays fixed. Build your decision around the property, not the rate alone.

Ask for an insurance quote based on rental use. Review the tax bill for owner-occupant benefits that may end. Obtain repair quotes for known problems rather than assigning a small general allowance to every system.

A reserve transfer is a cash planning choice. It is not proof that the next repair will fit the budget. List major systems and their condition, then decide what cash must remain available.

Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) with a manager's actual proposal. Include charges outside the monthly fee. A leasing charge may occur during the same period when you receive no rent.

Test a lower-rent case and a repair case separately. Then test both together. A property that only works with uninterrupted rent may require more cash than you want to keep available.

## What is the opportunity cost of your equity?

The opportunity cost is what you give up by leaving equity in the home. Estimate what a sale would actually put in your account. Deduct loan payoff, selling costs, repair credits, and an adviser-reviewed tax estimate.

Use net proceeds, not the home's sale price. The mortgage balance belongs to the lender. Selling costs also reduce the money available for your next use.

In another hypothetical example, assume net proceeds would be $150,000. The earlier $4,200 annual cash result equals 2.8% of that amount. This calculation is $4,200 divided by $150,000, not a claim about typical rental returns.

That cash measure leaves out principal paydown and changes in value. Show those separately rather than adding an assumed appreciation rate to make the deal look stronger. Future prices and sale costs remain uncertain.

Compare alternatives with similar care. Paying down your next loan, keeping liquid savings, or buying another asset creates different risks and tax results. A percentage alone does not capture access to cash or the time required.

## How do you compare keeping with a sale?

Compare both choices over the same period and with the same assumptions. A keep-versus-sell worksheet should show cash, debt, equity, taxes, and work. Do not compare gross rent with net sale proceeds.

Create separate columns for a normal year and a difficult year. Include a planned sale date in each. A rental may produce cash now but require costly work before sale.

IRS Publication 523 describes the home sale exclusion. Its general rules include ownership and residence tests within the period before sale. Renting the home can affect the timeline and treatment of gain.

Publication 523 also explains that gain tied to depreciation allowed or allowable after May 6, 1997, cannot receive the home sale exclusion. Ask for a property-specific calculation before you treat all sale profit as excluded.

Do not let tax planning decide the whole investment. Compare the tax cost with expected rent, risk, and the value of access to your equity. Keep the assumptions visible so you can update them.

## What evidence should you collect before deciding?

Collect written inputs before you commit the property to a tenant. A signed lease limits your flexibility, so finish the main financial review first.

- **Price the rent.** Compare homes with the same layout, condition, location, and included services.
- **Review the loan.** Confirm rental use with the servicer and read the signed documents.
- **Price the coverage.** Tell the insurer the home will become a rental.
- **Inspect the systems.** Get quotes for known work and set a cash plan.
- **Estimate a sale.** Ask for expected proceeds after costs and loan payoff.
- **Check tax timing.** Give your adviser move-in, move-out, purchase, and improvement dates.
