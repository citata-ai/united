## What is the owner reserve used for?

An owner reserve is money held for agreed property expenses when funds are needed between regular collections and payouts. It should support a defined purpose, not act as an unexplained deduction.

Buildium's owner distribution guide says the management agreement should state the reserve set aside for needs such as maintenance or vacancy. Its guidance also distinguishes available cash from unpaid bills and holds. Use those distinctions when explaining the reserve.

| Item | Reserve planning question | Record to keep |
|---|---|---|
| Routine repairs | What work may need payment before rent arrives? | Recent invoices and approved scope |
| Vacancy bills | Which costs continue without rent? | Utility and service schedule |
| Known replacement | Does this need separate funding? | Estimate and owner approval |
| Pending invoices | Has cash already been committed? | Open bill list |
| Emergency response | Who can authorize immediate action? | Reviewed agreement and contact plan |

A reserve is not the same as repair spending authority. Holding cash does not automatically permit every expense. Set both the funding rule and the approval rule.

Explain where the balance appears on the owner statement. Owners should see the opening amount, uses, contributions and closing amount. Keep tenant deposit balances distinct in the records and follow local handling rules.

Do not promise the reserve will cover every repair. A major replacement can need separate owner funds even when the agreed balance is fully funded.

## Should the reserve apply per unit or per property?

Choose a reserve structure that matches how costs arise and how the accounts are held. A unit-based rule can be simple, but shared building costs may need a separate assessment.

For scattered homes, review each property's condition and recurring obligations. For a building, identify common systems and shared bills. Do not assume that multiplying a single-home amount by unit count captures a major common repair.

PURE Property Management of Texas publishes a $500 reserve balance in its investor FAQ, read on 2026-10-19. It says rental income replenishes that balance. That is one company's stated policy, not a state requirement or national recommendation.

Use your own invoices and funding cycle to choose a proposed amount. State what the balance is meant to cover and what it excludes. Ask the owner to review known work separately.

For an invented planning example, assume $200 in expected vacancy utilities and $300 in near-term approved work. The total is $500 before any other buffer. These inputs are examples, not a claim about typical costs.

Review whether cash can lawfully move across properties or accounts before using a shared owner balance. Have local accounting and legal advisers confirm the structure. Do not use a portfolio view to hide a property shortfall.

## What happens when the reserve runs low?

A low reserve should trigger a clear review and funding request before the next payout decision. Check pending bills first so you do not overstate available cash.

Buildium recommends reviewing unpaid invoices, exceptions and reserves before owner distributions. Use a repeatable sequence: reconcile receipts, confirm obligations, calculate the required balance and explain the proposed payout.

Show the owner why a refill is needed. Include the work order or expense group, amount requested and payment route. Distinguish a confirmed bill from a planning estimate.

Set a backup contact for an owner who cannot respond. State what staff may do when funds are missing and a repair is urgent. Have counsel review the duties and emergency authority rather than creating an informal exception.

Do not silently advance another client's funds. If your company offers an advance from its own money, use a separately reviewed policy with clear terms. Do not assume an advance is part of ordinary management.

Record the refill and link it to the owner ledger. Confirm the funds are available through your approved accounting process before treating the request as resolved.

## How do trust account rules treat the reserve?

Trust account treatment depends on local rules and the legal ownership of the funds. Calling the amount a reserve does not turn client money into company cash.

California DRE guidance describes trust funds as money or value received for others during licensed activity. It says the funds belong to the client or beneficiary. The guidance addresses account designation, transaction records and reconciliation.

For California, the DRE cites Regulations 2831 and 2831.1 for control and beneficiary records. It cites Regulation 2831.2 for reconciliation. Those California references show the need to trace each owner's balance; they are not a national rule sheet.

Ask your responsible broker how the reserve should appear in your state and software. Check where the bank holds it, who may withdraw it and how the ledger identifies the beneficiary.

Keep company operating cash forecasts separate from client cash reports. A large trust account total does not prove that the company can pay payroll. Review the company and property reports with different purposes.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## When should I review the reserve amount?

Review the reserve when property obligations, funding timing or service scope changes. A long-standing amount can stop fitting the property without anyone noticing.

Check the record after a vacancy, major repair or change in recurring bills. Ask whether staff repeatedly delay work or hold distributions because the balance is too low. Also ask whether owners understand why unused cash remains held.

Propose a change with the cost basis and effective process. Obtain the authority your agreement and local rules require. Do not change the balance through a software setting before the owner receives the proper explanation.

At exit, reconcile the account and identify open obligations before calculating any refund. Follow the contract and local rules for the final transfer and records.
