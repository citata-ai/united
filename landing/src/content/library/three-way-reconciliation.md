A trust account can look fine at the bank and still be short. The bank does not know that $1,200 of the balance is Owner A's rent and $900 is Tenant B's deposit. Only your ledgers know that. A three-way reconciliation is the monthly check that the bank, your books and your ledgers tell the same story.

Rules differ by state. This guide cites California and Florida as examples. This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## What are the three balances?

The three balances are the bank, the book and the ledgers. Each one answers a different question.

| Balance | Where it comes from | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Adjusted bank balance | Bank statement, plus deposits in transit, minus outstanding checks | How much money is really in the account? |
| 2. Book balance | Your trust cash journal or cash ledger | How much money do your records say is in the account? |
| 3. Ledger total | Sum of every owner, tenant and beneficiary ledger | How much money do you owe to others? |

All three must be equal. The California DRE trust fund guide (RE 13) calls the third number your "aggregate trust fund liability". It says the money on deposit must always equal that liability.

The DRE guide describes two reconciliations at the end of each month. One compares the bank record with the bank statement. The other compares the bank record with the separate beneficiary records. Together they are the three-way check.

## How do I do a three-way reconciliation, step by step?

Do these steps for each trust account, once a month, after the bank statement closes.

1. **Close the month in your software.** Post all receipts, payments and bank fees through the last day.
2. **Get the bank statement.** Note the ending balance and date.
3. **List deposits in transit.** These are deposits in your books that the bank has not shown yet.
4. **List outstanding checks.** These are checks you wrote that the bank has not paid yet.
5. **Work out the adjusted bank balance.** Statement balance, plus deposits in transit, minus outstanding checks.
6. **Read the book balance** from your trust cash journal.
7. **Run the ledger total report.** Add every owner, tenant and other beneficiary balance.
8. **Compare the three numbers.** They must match to the cent.
9. **Explain every difference.** Write the cause and the fix on the report.
10. **Sign and date the report.** Florida Rule 61J2-14.012 says the broker must review, sign and date the monthly statement.
11. **File it** with the bank statement and the ledger report.

## A worked example

Here is one month for a small trust account. The numbers are an example, not client data.

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Bank statement balance, May 31 | $48,250.00 |
| Plus deposit in transit (rent received May 31) | + $1,850.00 |
| Minus outstanding check #2214 (plumber) | − $425.00 |
| Minus outstanding check #2217 (owner draw) | − $3,100.00 |
| **Adjusted bank balance** | **$46,575.00** |
| **Book balance (cash journal)** | **$46,575.00** |

Now the ledgers:

| Ledger | Balance |
|---|---|
| Owner ledgers (12 owners) | $21,340.00 |
| Tenant security deposits | $24,600.00 |
| Prepaid rent | $635.00 |
| **Ledger total** | **$46,575.00** |

All three numbers are $46,575.00. The account is in balance.

Now suppose the bank charged a $35 fee that you did not record. The adjusted bank balance falls to $46,540.00. The book and the ledgers still say $46,575.00. You are $35 short. In California, the DRE guide allows up to $200 of broker money in the trust account to cover bank charges. That cushion is there for this case, and it must be recorded on its own ledger.

## What causes a mismatch?

Mismatches often come from a short list of causes. Check them in this order.

- [ ] **Bank fees or interest** posted by the bank but not in your books.
- [ ] **Returned tenant payments** (NSF or ACH return) not reversed in the ledger.
- [ ] **Transposed numbers**, for example $1,580 entered as $1,850.
- [ ] **A receipt posted to the wrong owner** or the wrong property.
- [ ] **A check voided in the books** but cashed at the bank, or the reverse.
- [ ] **An owner paid more than that owner's balance.** This creates a negative ledger, paid with other owners' money.
- [ ] **Management fees not moved out**, which shows as an overage.
- [ ] **Old outstanding checks** that a vendor never cashed.

A tip: if the difference divides evenly by 9, look for a transposed number first.

## Which reports do I need?

You need four reports each month. Property management accounting software can usually produce them.

| Report | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Bank statement | The bank's ending balance and every item it cleared |
| Bank reconciliation report | Deposits in transit, outstanding checks, adjusted balance |
| Trust cash journal or general ledger for the trust account | The book balance and every transaction in date order |
| Ledger balance report (owners, tenants, deposits) | Each beneficiary's balance and the total |

Florida Rule 61J2-14.012 lists the minimum content of the monthly statement: the date it was done, the bank and account names and numbers, balances and dates, deposits in transit, outstanding checks, and an itemized list of the broker's trust liability. Keep the four reports together as one package per month.

## How do I fix a shortage?

A shortage means the bank holds less than you owe. Treat it as urgent.

1. **Find the cause** with the checklist above.
2. **Correct entry errors** in the books if the money is really there.
3. **Deposit your own funds** to cover a real shortage, such as an unrecorded bank fee or a negative owner balance. Record the deposit on its own ledger.
4. **Collect from the owner** whose ledger went negative.
5. **Write the cause and the fix** on the reconciliation report.

Florida Rule 61J2-14.012 says that when trust liability and bank balances do not agree, the reconciliation must explain the difference and the corrective action for any shortage or overage.

In California, the DRE guide says a payment that pushes the account below total liability breaks Regulation 2832.1, unless every owner of the funds agreed in writing first. Check your state's rule on shortages.

An overage needs a fix too. Find whose money it is. If it is your earned fee, move it out. If you cannot find the owner, ask your state regulator or an attorney what to do with it.

## Who should do the reconciliation?

A person who did not enter the month's transactions should do or review the reconciliation. This split of duties makes errors and theft harder to hide.

Many firms have a bookkeeper prepare it and the broker review and sign it. Florida requires the broker's signature. California's DRE guide says the broker stays responsible for trust funds even when others can sign on the account.
