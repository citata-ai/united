## Which owners cost more than they pay?

Assess an owner account using collected fees and the direct work it requires. A difficult conversation alone is not a reason to end service. Start with facts about the account.

Track staff time, repeat visits, unpaid invoices and unresolved funding requests. Include the cost of work your contract includes but your pricing does not cover. Separate an unusually busy repair period from a pattern that continues without resolution.

For an invented example, assume an account pays $150 a month. If direct staff work costs $180 before software and overhead, the account has a $30 shortfall. These inputs illustrate the method; they are not market costs.

| Issue | Evidence to review | Possible response |
|---|---|---|
| Service scope mismatch | Agreement and repeated requests | Clarify scope or offer a different plan |
| Delayed funding | Ledger and requests | Set a documented funding process |
| Abusive conduct | Messages and incident notes | Set conduct limits and review exit |
| Unsafe instructions | Work orders and written directions | Escalate promptly to broker and counsel |
| Repeated unpaid fees | Statements and collection history | Review contract remedies and transition |

Do not treat all complaints as poor client behavior. Check whether your team missed a promise or gave unclear advice. Fix a service error before deciding the owner is the problem.

Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to inspect the fee structure. A pricing problem may require a better agreement rather than an immediate exit.

## What red flags should intake reveal?

Intake should reveal whether the owner will fund the home, accept the service scope and use the agreed decision process. Ask direct questions before taking control of the account.

Discuss the property's condition and open repair issues. Ask who can approve work and how the owner will supply funds. Review the owner's goals so your service does not depend on a plan the owner has not accepted.

Ask how the owner handled prior management problems. Request relevant documents rather than relying only on a complaint about the former manager. Check whether expectations about rent, repairs and access match the actual property.

Explain your screening process and who may approve exceptions. The FTC warns that a blanket refusal to rent to anyone with a criminal record may violate the Fair Housing Act. Do not accept owner instructions that replace your reviewed screening policy with an unchecked demand.

Give the owner examples of decisions that need a prompt reply. Ask whether someone else can act during an absence. Record the contact and funding plan in the intake file.

An owner who asks detailed questions may be a good fit. An owner who rejects essential funding or insists on improper conduct needs further review. Make the decision from the facts, not from personal preference.

## How should I end the agreement cleanly?

End the agreement through the notice and transition process that applies to your contract and state. Have local counsel review the plan when the facts involve risk or a dispute.

Read the term, notice, delivery, fee and handover clauses together. Confirm who can receive notice and when it takes effect. Do not invent a national notice period.

If the relationship can be repaired safely, state the specific problem and requested change. Give a clear next step and record the response. Do not promise a cure process your agreement or legal duties do not support.

When you decide to exit, write a short notice with the effective date and practical transition contact. Avoid accusations that do not help the handover. Keep the detailed evidence in the internal file for review.

Assign a transition lead who can answer account questions. List open repairs, scheduled visits, pending applications and funds held. Confirm which tasks your team will complete before the effective end date.

If the owner disputes the exit, involve counsel rather than improvising restrictions on records or payments. Keep urgent service reports visible while responsibility changes. An argument about fees should not leave staff unsure who receives an emergency call.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## What records and funds must I hand over?

The handover needs the records and balances required by your agreement and local law. Create an inventory and have the receiving party confirm receipt.

A practical inventory includes leases, approved amendments, resident contacts, key records, inspection reports and open maintenance files. Include vendor invoices, owner approvals and account detail needed to explain balances. Share personal data through a controlled route.

Buildium's owner distribution guide recommends reconciling accounts and checking unpaid bills and holds before calculating a payout. Use that sequence for the final owner account. Explain pending payments, reserves and disputed charges separately.

Do not present an unreconciled balance as the final cash due. Record the date and scope of the statement. If a later invoice remains possible, say how the final review will work under the agreement.

Keep a copy of the handover index and delivery record. Check local retention duties before deleting your working files or closing system access. Confirm that residents and vendors have the authorized new contact information.

Separate physical access from account access. A returned key does not remove a former staff member's portal rights, and a disabled login does not prove the successor received the lease files.

## How can I learn from an owner exit?

Review the exit to improve intake, pricing and service boundaries. The aim is a clearer operating process, not a list of owners your team dislikes.

Ask whether the first warning appeared before signing. Review what the sales call promised, what the agreement said and what staff delivered. Find the exact point where those expectations diverged.

Record whether the issue involved poor service, insufficient funding, abusive conduct or a business model mismatch. Several causes may apply. Keep the owner's explanation alongside the team's findings.

Update the intake checklist or fee scope when the review shows a gap. Train staff on the new boundary and show where they can escalate concerns. Do not change policies through informal exceptions that the next employee cannot see.

Start with a review of your most time-consuming account. Check the records, identify the specific issue and decide whether a clear change could make the relationship workable.
