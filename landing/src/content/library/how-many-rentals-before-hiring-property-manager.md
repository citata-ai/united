## How do management hours per unit add up?

Management hours depend on the work each property creates, rather than its unit count alone.

Start with a task log. Record rent questions, repairs, vendor scheduling, travel, inspections, leasing, and bookkeeping. Include time spent waiting for a contractor or resolving a failed payment. A month without a turnover may hide work that arrives later.

Separate recurring tasks from event-driven tasks. A renewal differs from an urgent water leak. Both need capacity, but they affect your schedule differently. Note which tasks you can plan and which interrupt paid work or family time.

| Work category | What to record | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Leasing | Inquiries, visits, screening, and handover | Work can cluster around vacancy |
| Repairs | Calls, access, quotes, and follow-up | Unplanned tasks interrupt other work |
| Money | Collections, bills, and statements | Delays can create funding problems |
| Travel | Distance and time at the property | Remote homes need local coverage |
| Oversight | File review and vendor checks | Delegation still needs owner attention |

Review a period that includes a real leasing event if possible. If you have no history, ask managers for a task list and use explicit assumptions. Do not present that estimate as a landlord industry average.

The goal is a record you can act on. Identify the tasks that cause missed responses before deciding which service to buy.

## What is the value of my own time?

Use the realistic value of the time you can recover, then compare it with the full cost of management.

A hypothetical example can clarify the choice. Assume you spend 12 hours per month on work a manager would actually take over. At an assumed value of $40 per hour, that time is worth $480. These are planning assumptions, not published owner workload or wage data.

Now compare the complete proposal. Include monthly charges, leasing, renewals, inspections, vendor markups, setup, and termination terms. Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to test the recurring fee. Add charges the calculator does not cover.

Not every recovered hour becomes paid work. Some hours protect rest or make your schedule more reliable. You can value those benefits, but name them honestly. Avoid counting the same hour as both extra income and reduced stress in a cash budget.

Keep oversight time in the comparison. You still review statements, fund reserves, approve major work, and assess performance. A manager removes tasks only when the contract includes them and the manager performs them.

IRS Publication 527 lists management fees among common rental expenses. A tax deduction does not reimburse the whole fee. Ask your preparer how the expense applies and compare costs after your own tax circumstances, rather than assuming the service is free.

## Does the unit type change the decision?

Unit type, location, condition, and tenant turnover can change workload more than the number of doors.

Scattered houses may require separate vendor trips and systems. A small apartment building may share systems but produce common-area tasks. A furnished home may add supplies and inventory work. Map the actual responsibilities before using unit count as a shortcut.

Distance can make even a small rental hard to cover. Ask who can reach the property during an urgent repair. A nearby contact needs both access and clear authority. A friend who can collect mail may not be able to manage a serious leak.

Condition also changes the work. A recently serviced building still needs a reserve and routine checks. An older system with repeated faults can demand sustained vendor coordination. Do not assume a low vacancy rate means little management effort.

Compare the manager's experience with your property type. Ask for a sample inspection record, owner statement, and work-order process. Review how the manager handles urgent calls and vendor follow-up. Specific documents reveal more than a general promise of full service.

Choose the scope you need. Leasing help, bookkeeping, local repair coverage, and full management solve different problems. Obtain clear responsibility boundaries so no task falls between the owner and the service provider.

## What other signs show it is time to hire?

Missed responses, poor records, uncovered urgent calls, and repeated delays are useful signals that your current process needs help.

The decision is about service capacity as well as cost. Track unresolved work orders and owner tasks that remain unfinished. Ask whether the current system treats tenants consistently and keeps the property in sound condition. Fix the gap before adding more homes.

Request written terms from managers who handle similar properties. Ask how they receive money, report expenses, hold reserves, and transfer records when service ends. California DRE's trust guidance offers a state-specific example of the records and reconciliation worth discussing. Other states have their own requirements.

Check what the manager needs from you during onboarding. Supply leases, deposit records, condition reports, vendor contacts, warranties, and current repair issues. A new manager cannot fix a missing file without your help.

Keep enough cash to use the service properly. A manager cannot fund your building's major work from a small monthly fee. Set approval and reserve terms before the first urgent call. Confirm who acts when you cannot respond.

Ask each finalist to explain a routine month and a difficult turnover. Compare the work, exclusions, records, and annual cost. Hire when that clear service plan fills a real gap in your own capacity.
