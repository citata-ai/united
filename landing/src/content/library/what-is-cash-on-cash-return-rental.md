## What is cash-on-cash return?

Cash-on-cash return is the yearly cash a rental puts in your pocket, before income tax, divided by the cash you put into the deal. The Corporate Finance Institute (CFI) describes it as a ratio that "calculates the total cash earned on the total cash invested."

The formula:

**Cash-on-cash return = yearly pre-tax cash flow ÷ total cash invested × 100**

- **Yearly pre-tax cash flow** = rent collected − operating expenses − mortgage payments (principal and interest).
- **Total cash invested** = down payment + closing costs + upfront repairs and other cash you put in.

The metric is simple and answers one question: what does my cash earn this year? It does not count loan paydown, appreciation or tax effects. CFI notes that because it is pre-tax, investors must also think about taxes.

## What counts as cash invested?

Cash invested is every dollar of your own money that the deal needs before it runs on its own. Borrowed money does not count.

| Count it | Do not count it |
|---|---|
| Down payment | The loan amount |
| Closing costs and title fees | Future repairs paid from rent |
| Loan origination fees and points | Money you could have invested elsewhere |
| Upfront repairs to make the unit rent-ready | Appreciation |
| Reserves you fund at purchase | Principal paid down later |
| Furniture or appliances bought at the start | |

Watch for: a low cash invested number that hides a later bill. If the roof needs $12,000 in year 2, you should count it in the deal plan, not only in year 2 cash flow.

## How do you calculate it for a rental? A worked example

Here is one rental, financed 3 ways. The numbers are an example, not market data, except the mortgage rate.

**The property**

- Price: $300,000. Closing costs: $9,000. Upfront repairs: $6,000.
- Rent: $2,600 a month, or $31,200 a year.
- Vacancy and credit loss: 5%, or $1,560. Rent collected: $29,640.

**Yearly operating expenses**

| Expense | Amount |
|---|---|
| Property tax | $3,600 |
| Insurance | $1,500 |
| Management at 8% of collected rent | $2,371 |
| Maintenance | $1,800 |
| Capital reserve (roof, HVAC, water heater) | $1,200 |
| **Total** | **$10,471** |

The 8% management fee is close to the 8.49% average that iPropertyManagement found in its fee study, updated September 23, 2026.

Net operating income (NOI) = $29,640 − $10,471 = **$19,169**.

**The loan rate.** The 30-year fixed rate averaged 7.40% for the week of October 15, 2026 (Freddie Mac data on FRED). Investment property loans can cost more; we use 7.40% to keep it simple.

| | All cash | 25% down | 20% down |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loan amount | $0 | $225,000 | $240,000 |
| Monthly payment (30 years, 7.40%) | $0 | $1,558 | $1,662 |
| Yearly debt service | $0 | $18,694 | $19,941 |
| Cash flow (NOI − debt service) | $19,169 | $475 | −$772 |
| Cash invested | $315,000 | $90,000 | $75,000 |
| **Cash-on-cash return** | **6.09%** | **0.53%** | **−1.03%** |

## How does financing change cash-on-cash return?

Financing changes cash-on-cash return in 2 directions at once. It cuts the cash you invest, which pushes the return up. It adds a mortgage payment, which pushes cash flow down. Which effect wins depends on the loan cost compared with the property's yield.

In the example, the property's cap rate is $19,169 ÷ $300,000 = 6.39%. The loan's yearly cost (the mortgage constant) at 7.40% over 30 years is about 8.3% of the loan amount ($18,694 ÷ $225,000). The loan costs more than the property earns, so each dollar borrowed lowers your return. That is called negative leverage.

The rule of thumb that follows from the math:

- **Loan constant below the cap rate:** more debt raises cash-on-cash return.
- **Loan constant above the cap rate:** more debt lowers cash-on-cash return, and can make cash flow negative.

What to do: before you buy, run the deal at 3 down payments, as in the table. If cash-on-cash falls as you borrow more, the price, the rent or the rate does not work yet for a leveraged buy.

## What is a good cash-on-cash return?

A good cash-on-cash return is one that beats your next-best use of the same cash, at a risk you accept. There is no single correct number, and any rule you read online assumes a rate and a market.

Use 3 checks instead:

1. **Compare with the cap rate.** If cash-on-cash is below the cap rate, your loan terms are a drag.
2. **Compare with a safe yield.** If the cash could earn a similar amount with much less work and risk, the rental needs another reason to make sense, such as long-term growth.
3. **Stress test it.** Raise vacancy to 10% and maintenance by 50%. A deal that turns negative under a mild stress test is fragile.

## Cash-on-cash return vs cap rate: which to use?

Use cap rate to compare properties and cash-on-cash return to compare deals. Buildium's dictionary gives the cap rate formula as net operating income divided by current market value or purchase price, and notes that it "does not include mortgage payments or financing costs."

| | Cap rate | Cash-on-cash return |
|---|---|---|
| Formula | NOI ÷ price | Cash flow after debt ÷ cash invested |
| Includes the loan? | No | Yes |
| Best for | Comparing properties and markets | Comparing your financing choices |
| Example result | 6.39% | 0.53% at 25% down |

A property manager affects both. Lower vacancy and better maintenance raise NOI, which lifts cap rate and cash-on-cash return at the same time. Owners can model how fees and vacancy change their numbers with our [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator).
