## What is a lease guarantor?

A lease guarantor is a person or company that promises to pay rent and other lease costs if the tenant does not. The guarantor signs a separate document, often called a guaranty or a guarantor agreement. The guarantor does not live in the unit and has no right to live there.

A guarantor is a backup payer. The landlord still collects from the tenant first in normal practice. The guaranty gives the landlord a second source of money when the tenant defaults.

Typical guarantors:

- A parent or other relative with stable income.
- A friend or employer who agrees in writing.
- An institutional guarantor, a company that sells a guarantee to the renter.

## When should a landlord ask for a guarantor?

A landlord should ask for a guarantor when an applicant does not meet a written screening rule, and the landlord still wants to approve the applicant. Common cases:

| Applicant situation | Why a guarantor helps |
|---------------------|----------------------|
| Student with no income | Shifts the payment risk to a person with income |
| First-time renter with thin credit | Adds a credit history the landlord can check |
| New job, income starts next month | Covers the gap until pay stubs exist |
| Income just below the rent multiple | Adds a second income source |

Fair Housing: write the policy down and apply it the same way to every applicant. Base it on income, credit or rental history, not on who the applicant is. Some states and cities also limit how landlords use income tests or ask for extra security. This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## What income must a guarantor have?

No federal law sets a guarantor income rule. Each landlord sets one in its screening policy. The most cited numbers come from New York City.

Brick Underground reports that a New York City guarantor must often earn "an annual income of at least 80 to 90 times your monthly rent". For a $3,200 apartment, that is about $256,000 to $288,000 a year. Many landlords outside New York use a lower multiple, so set your own and write it down.

Worked example, with made-up numbers. Monthly rent is $1,800. Your written policy says a guarantor must earn 60 times the monthly rent per year.

| Line | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Monthly rent | $1,800 |
| Guarantor multiple in your policy | 60x |
| Minimum guarantor income | $108,000 per year |
| Guarantor's documented income | $115,000 |
| Result | Meets the rule |

Ask the guarantor for the same proof you ask from tenants: pay stubs or tax returns, ID, and consent to a credit check.

## What does a guarantor agree to pay?

A guarantor agrees to pay what the guaranty document says, which is often the full rent and other lease costs. Brick Underground notes that under most standard agreements, a guarantor can be held liable for the full rent, including roommates' shares, if the tenants stop paying.

A guaranty usually covers some or all of these items. Read the document, because each one changes the risk:

- **Rent.** Monthly rent for the lease term.
- **Fees.** Late fees and other charges the lease allows.
- **Damage.** Repair costs above the security deposit.
- **Legal costs.** Court and attorney fees, if the lease and local law allow them.
- **Renewals.** Some guaranties continue through each renewal. Others end with the first term.
- **Cap.** Some guaranties limit the total, for example to 12 months of rent.

Checklist for a written guaranty:

1. Names the tenant, the unit and the lease date.
2. States which costs the guarantor covers.
3. States whether the guaranty covers renewals.
4. States any dollar cap or time limit.
5. Says how the landlord gives notice of a default.
6. Is signed and dated by the guarantor.
7. Has a copy of the lease attached.

## Guarantor vs co-signer: what is the difference?

In common rental use, a guarantor signs a separate guaranty, while a co-signer signs the lease itself. Neither lives in the unit, in most cases. The legal duty depends on the exact document, not on the label.

| Point | Guarantor | Co-signer |
|-------|-----------|-----------|
| Signs | Separate guaranty | The lease |
| Lives in the unit | No | Usually no |
| Pays when | Tenant fails to pay | Often from day 1, same as the tenant |
| Typical scope | Set by the guaranty | Set by the lease |

The Federal Trade Commission explains the co-signer risk for loans in plain terms: "If the main borrower misses payments, you must make the payments." The FTC also notes a creditor can often collect from the co-signer without first going after the borrower. A rental co-signer takes a similar risk under the lease.

## What are guarantor services?

Guarantor services are companies that act as the guarantor for a fee that the renter pays. They help applicants who have no family guarantor or whose guarantor does not meet the landlord's rule.

Two examples:

- **TheGuarantors.** Its help center gives the price of Rent Coverage for a 1-year lease. It is about 40% of one month's rent up to "a bit more than a full month's rent". It is a one-time fee for the lease term. Its Deposit Coverage is "usually between 25% and 50% of a full month's rent" and varies by state.
- **Insurent.** Its site says it is accepted at over 775,000 units across NY, NJ, MA, VA, MD, DC, IL, NV, CA and FL.

Worked cost example, with made-up rent. For $2,000 monthly rent, a 40% to 110% fee is $800 to $2,200 for the year.

What a landlord should check before accepting a service:

- The coverage limit, in months of rent.
- What the policy excludes, such as damage or legal fees.
- How to file a claim and how fast it pays.
- Whether coverage ends at renewal.

## How a property manager handles guarantors

A property manager runs the guarantor step as part of screening and keeps the guaranty with the lease. Ask your manager these questions:

- What income multiple do you use for tenants and for guarantors?
- Do you accept institutional guarantors? Which ones?
- Who checks the guaranty language, and does it cover renewals?
