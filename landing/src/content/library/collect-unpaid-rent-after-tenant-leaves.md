## What should I do first?

First, find out exactly what the former tenant owes, after the security deposit. A clear, itemized number is the base for a demand letter, a court claim or an agency file. A vague number weakens all three.

Use this order:

1. **Do the move-out inspection.** Take dated photos the day you get the unit back.
2. **Apply the deposit.** Deduct unpaid rent and allowed damage, as your state law permits.
3. **Send the itemized statement.** Send it by your state's deadline. In California, Civil Code 1950.5 gives 21 days.
4. **Send a demand letter.** State the balance, how to pay, and a deadline, such as 14 days.
5. **Offer a payment plan.** A former tenant who cannot pay $3,000 at once may pay $250 a month.
6. **Choose small claims or an agency.** Do this if the deadline passes with no payment.

A worked example: the tenant left owing 2 months of rent at $1,400, so $2,800. Repairs beyond normal wear cost $600. The deposit was $1,400. The balance is $2,800 + $600 − $1,400 = $2,000. Your demand letter asks for $2,000, with the itemized statement attached.

Keep copies of the lease, the ledger, the photos, the invoices and each letter. You need them for any path you choose.

## Small claims court or a collection agency?

Small claims court fits when the balance is under your state's limit and you know where the former tenant lives. A collection agency fits when you cannot or do not want to go to court, or when you cannot find the person. Compare the two:

| Factor | Small claims court | Collection agency |
|---|---|---|
| Your cost | A filing fee and a service fee | A fee from what the agency collects; ask for the rate in writing |
| Your time | Prepare evidence and attend a hearing | Send the file, then answer questions |
| Lawyer needed | Usually not | No |
| Result | A court judgment | A payment, a settlement, or nothing |
| Finds the debtor | No, you must give an address | Many agencies do skip tracing |
| Rules for you | Court rules | The agency follows the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act |

Small claims limits differ by state:

| State | Limit | Source |
|---|---|---|
| California | $12,500 for a natural person; $6,250 for corporations and other businesses; no more than 2 claims over $2,500 a year statewide | CCP 116.221; Monterey County Superior Court |
| Texas | Justice court: civil claims up to $20,000, not counting interest | Government Code 27.031 |

A judgment does not pay you by itself. If the former tenant still does not pay, ask the court how to enforce the judgment. Each state has its own tools, such as wage garnishment or a bank levy.

## Does debt collection law apply to me?

Federal debt collection law mostly applies to agencies, not to a landlord who collects his or her own rent. The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, at 15 U.S.C. 1692a(6), defines a "debt collector" as a business whose main purpose is to collect debts, or a person who regularly collects debts owed to another. The same section adds a creditor who collects its own debts under a different name. So do not send letters under a fake agency name.

State laws can be stricter than the federal act, and some apply to creditors too. Stay factual and polite in every letter and call. Never threaten jail or a step you will not take.

## How do I find a former tenant?

Start with what you already have. Then use paid tools with a legal purpose.

- **Forwarding address.** Texas Property Code 92.107 says a landlord need not return the deposit or describe the deductions until the tenant gives a written forwarding address. Ask for the address in your move-out process in every state.
- **Rental application.** It lists the employer, emergency contacts and past addresses.
- **Payment records.** An online rent account may show a new email or phone number.
- **Consumer report.** The Fair Credit Reporting Act, 15 U.S.C. 1681b(a)(3)(A), allows a report for the review or collection of an account of the consumer. Use a vendor that confirms your permissible purpose.
- **Agency skip tracing.** A collection agency can search for the person as part of its service.

Do not contact the former tenant's employer or family about the debt beyond what your state law allows. Ask only for contact information.

## What does a collection agency charge?

Collection agency fees vary, so get a written quote before you send the file. Ask each agency these questions:

1. Is the fee a share of what you collect, a flat fee, or both?
2. Is there any fee if the agency collects nothing?
3. Does the agency report to the credit bureaus?
4. Does the agency hold a license in your state, if your state requires one?
5. Can it sue for you, and who pays the court costs?
6. How often will it report progress?

Then compare the expected net amount. Suppose the balance is $2,000. If an agency quotes a 40% share (a sample rate for the math, not a market average) and collects in full, you keep $1,200. If you win in small claims and the former tenant pays, you keep the $2,000 plus any costs the court awards, less your time. Plug in the rate each agency quotes you.

## How long do I have to collect?

Each state sets a deadline, called the statute of limitations, for a claim on a lease. After it passes, you may lose the right to sue. Written leases often get a longer period than oral ones.

| State | Deadline for a claim on a written contract | Source |
|---|---|---|
| California | 4 years | Code of Civil Procedure 337(a) |
| Texas | 4 years for an action for debt | Civil Practice and Remedies Code 16.004(a)(3) |
| Florida | 5 years | Statute 95.11 |

The clock usually starts when the rent came due or when the tenant left, but courts differ on the start date. Do not wait until the last year. Former tenants are harder to find, and evidence goes stale.
