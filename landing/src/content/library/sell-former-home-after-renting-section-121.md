## What is the 2-of-5-year test (IRS Pub 523)?

The 2-of-5-year test is the core of the home sale exclusion. You must have owned the home and lived in it as your main home for 24 months. Those months must fall in the 5 years before the sale date.

IRS Publication 523 sets out the steps:

| Test | Rule (Pub 523) |
|------|----------------|
| Ownership | You owned the home for at least 24 months (2 years) of the 5 years before the sale (the closing date). For a married couple filing jointly, only one spouse must meet it. |
| Residence | You owned the home and used it as your residence for at least 24 months of the previous 5 years. |
| Look-back | In general, you did not exclude gain on another home sold in the 2 years before this sale. |

If you pass, Pub 523 lets you exclude up to $250,000 of gain. The limit is $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly, when both meet the use test and other conditions.

The 24 months do not need to be in one block. They must fall inside the 5 years that end on the sale date.

## Why do owners talk about a 3-year window?

Owners talk about a 3-year window because 5 years minus 2 years of living there leaves 3 years. If you lived in the home for at least 2 years, then moved out and rented it, you can rent for up to about 3 years and still pass the test.

Timeline example, with made-up dates:

| Date | Event |
|------|-------|
| 2021-06-01 | Buy and move in |
| 2024-06-01 | Move out; rent the home from 2024-07-01 |
| 2027-05-31 | Last day to close and still have 24 months of use in the prior 5 years (use from 2022-06-01 to 2024-06-01) |
| 2027-08-01 | Too late: only 22 months of use fall in the 5 years before this date |

Watch the closing date, not the listing date. A sale that closes one month late can fail the test.

Pub 523 lists exceptions for some owners, such as members of the uniformed services, Foreign Service and intelligence community on qualified extended duty. Ask a tax professional if one applies to you.

## What is nonqualified use?

Nonqualified use is time after 2008 when neither you nor your spouse used the home as your main home. Pub 523 says gain allocated to periods of nonqualified use cannot be excluded.

The key exception for a former home: Pub 523 says nonqualified use does not include any part of the 5-year period after the last date you or your spouse used the home as your main home.

So the order of events matters:

| Pattern | Rental time counts as nonqualified use? |
|---------|-----------------------------------------|
| Live there first, then rent, then sell | No. Rental after your last day of living there, within 5 years of the sale, is excluded from nonqualified use. |
| Rent first, then move in, then sell | Yes. Rental before you moved in, after 2008, is nonqualified use. |

Pub 523's own example: Finley buys a property on January 1, 2020, for $400,000 and rents it for 2 years, claiming $20,000 of depreciation. Finley moves in on January 1, 2022, moves out on January 1, 2024, and sells on January 1, 2025, for $700,000. The total gain is $320,000. The $20,000 of depreciation is taxed separately, and part of the remaining gain is allocated to the 2 rental years before Finley moved in.

Pub 523 also lists other exceptions, such as a temporary absence of up to 2 years in total due to a job change, health or other unforeseen circumstances.

## Does depreciation still get taxed?

Yes. The exclusion does not cover gain from depreciation. Pub 523 says you cannot exclude the part of your gain equal to any depreciation allowed or allowable for periods after May 6, 1997.

"Allowed or allowable" matters. IRS Publication 946 says you must reduce basis by the depreciation allowable even if you did not claim it. So an owner who skips depreciation on the rental years still pays the tax on it at sale.

Worked example, live-then-rent, with made-up numbers:

| Line | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Purchase price (2021) | $300,000 |
| Depreciation claimed, 2024 to 2027 | −$25,000 |
| Adjusted basis | $275,000 |
| Sale price net of selling costs (2027) | $420,000 |
| Total gain | $145,000 |
| Gain equal to depreciation (not excludable) | $25,000 |
| Remaining gain | $120,000 |
| Excluded under section 121 (single, under $250,000) | $120,000 |
| Taxable gain | $25,000 |

The owner pays tax on $25,000 and excludes $120,000. Without the exclusion, the full $145,000 would be taxable.

## A checklist before you sell a former home

1. **Write down your move-in and move-out dates.** Keep proof: utility bills, a driver's license address, tax returns.
2. **Count back 5 years** from your planned closing date. Check you have 24 months of use inside it.
3. **Total your depreciation** from each year's Schedule E and Form 4562.
4. **Check the look-back test** if you sold another home in the last 2 years.
5. **Decide early.** If the window is closing, list 3 to 6 months ahead of the deadline.
6. **Ask about a 1031 exchange** if you will not move back. Pub 523 says a property can sometimes qualify for both, under Revenue Procedure 2005-14. A tax professional must check this.
