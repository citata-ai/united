## Which revenue lines belong in the company budget?

Include the fees your company earns for its services. Keep rent collected for owners separate from company income. A large volume of client cash does not equal a large management business margin.

Buildium's owner distribution guide distinguishes management fees from owner funds, bills and reserves. Use that distinction in your budget model. Have your accountant confirm the treatment of each revenue and cash line.

Start with recurring management fees. Add leasing, renewal, inspection or other service income only when the agreement and your accounting policy support it. Do not count the same charge in several categories.

Use this generic planning template. Fill it from your own records and have your accountant review the assumptions. Check local law before using any proposed fee or employment arrangement.

The template is a planning structure, not a tax form. Do not fill blank lines with industry averages you have not checked.

## Which costs are fixed and which grow with doors?

Classify costs by how they actually change under your contracts and staffing model. Some costs grow per unit, while others rise when you add a team member or capacity.

Software may charge by account, unit, usage or a minimum plan. Read your invoice and agreement before assuming a per-door cost. A fixed base with usage fees needs both parts in the budget.

Staffing often needs a capacity review. More units may fit the current team until work exceeds available time. Then a new role can increase costs before its full capacity produces revenue.

Keep staff training and supervision visible. A hire does not become fully productive on the first payroll date. Include coverage during absences and the work retained by the company owner.

Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to inspect recurring fee assumptions. Then add actual service costs from your own records. Equal fee revenue does not mean equal profit across different property types.

The SBA's business planning guidance recommends clear financial projections and cost assumptions. Apply that principle by keeping the source and date beside each budget input. A quote, signed contract and planning estimate have different levels of certainty.

## How should I plan for owner churn?

Plan lost accounts explicitly by month and reason rather than applying a hidden reduction to all revenue. State that loss assumptions are forecasts.

Use your own cancellation and sale records where available. Distinguish service-related exits from sales, owner occupancy and accounts your company chooses to end. The categories help you choose a response later.

For an invented example, start with 100 active units, add 10 and lose 6. The closing count is 104. That closing count does not mean all 104 paid fees for the full year.

Model when units become billable and when fees stop. Add leasing revenue only where the expected event supports it. Do not book new-owner leads as signed accounts.

Test a downside case with fewer starts, more exits or delayed collections. Use clearly labeled assumptions. Do not present a single growth forecast as a commitment.

Check whether cost savings follow account losses immediately. Staff and software commitments may remain even when revenue drops. Your cash forecast should show that lag.

Keep marketing spend linked to a testable acquisition plan. If the budget assumes more owner accounts, name the channel, responsible person and evidence you will review. Avoid using an unverified industry churn number to fill a planning gap.

## How do I track budget against actual results?

Use the same categories and definitions in the budget and accounting reports. Otherwise a difference may reflect changed labels rather than changed performance.

Close the records through your approved accounting process before reviewing results. Mark incomplete periods clearly. Do not compare a full-month budget with a partial actual period without showing the scope.

For each material difference, record the amount, cause and action. Separate timing from a lasting change. A delayed software invoice differs from an approved new subscription.

Review revenue using both active account counts and fee realization. A growing unit count can hide fee waivers, vacancy effects or poor collections. Check the actual fee terms before drawing a conclusion.

Review company cash separately from the operating result. Payment timing, borrowing, equipment purchases and owner transactions can change cash without the same effect on the period's result.

Ask your accountant how to handle tax estimates and accounting adjustments. This is general information, not tax advice. Check the current law or ask a tax professional.

Keep the original approved budget available. Update a forecast when facts change, but do not overwrite the old plan and remove the evidence needed for review.

## What should I do before approving the budget?

Have the people responsible for each cost check their inputs. Ask operations about workload, payroll about full staffing costs and accounting about revenue and cash definitions.

Check the assumptions that most affect the result. Confirm the starting account list, fee schedules and committed expenses. Mark uncertain items and identify when you expect better evidence.

Approve a review process as well as a spreadsheet. Name who prepares the report, who decides changes and how the team records them.

Start with your latest company income statement and active account list. Reconcile those starting records before forecasting new growth.
