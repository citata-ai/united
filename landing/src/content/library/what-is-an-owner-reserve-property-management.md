## What is an owner reserve?

An owner reserve is a fixed balance of the owner's money that the property manager keeps back from rent payouts. It sits in the manager's trust account on the owner's ledger. The manager uses it to pay the property's bills fast, without waiting for the owner to send money.

TenantCloud's help center describes a required reserve as "a specific amount of money that the property manager can access to deal with daily obligations." The owner's balance must not fall below that amount.

Owner reserves have other names: maintenance reserve, operating reserve, or minimum balance. They all work the same way.

## Why does a property manager keep an owner reserve?

A property manager keeps an owner reserve because trust account rules bar paying one owner's bills with another owner's money. The reserve makes sure each owner's ledger can cover its own costs.

The California Department of Real Estate (DRE) Trust Funds guide (RE 13) sets out the rules for brokers who manage property:

- **No negative ledgers.** Disburse from a beneficiary's account only when the payment will not create "a negative or deficit balance."
- **Why it matters.** Paying an owner's bill beyond that owner's balance is, in effect, paid with funds that belong to other beneficiaries.
- **The total must match.** The trust account must always equal the total owed to all beneficiaries. If it is less, that is a shortage, which breaks Commissioner's Regulation 2832.1.
- **No commingling.** The manager may not cover the gap with its own money, except up to $200 for bank fees.

The DRE notes that many licenses have been revoked after an audit found a trust account shortage. So the reserve protects the owner, the other owners and the manager.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## What is a typical reserve amount per door?

There is no standard reserve amount. Each management agreement sets its own number.

One public example: a Doorvest management agreement filed with the SEC in 2021 says "the standard reserve is $400.00 per home," and $200 per home for each additional home after the first. If the balance falls below that amount, the manager may deduct the shortfall from monthly income to bring it back up.

TenantCloud's software shows the per-unit math: for multi-unit property, the system multiplies the reserve by the number of units. Its example is 3 units at $400 each, for a total reserve of $1,200.

How to size a reserve:

| Factor | Points to a larger reserve | Points to a smaller reserve |
|---|---|---|
| Age of property | Older systems, roof, water heater | New build |
| Approval limit | High limit for repairs without owner OK | Low limit |
| Owner response time | Owner slow to fund large bills | Owner funds bills in 1 day |
| Property type | Multi-unit with shared systems | Single condo with HOA covering exterior |
| Vacancy | Long vacancies, utilities in owner's name | Low turnover |

Worked example: an owner has 3 single-family homes. Under the Doorvest structure, the reserve is $400 + $200 + $200 = $800. Another approach is to tie the reserve to the approval limit. If the agreement lets the manager spend up to $300 on a repair without asking (as the Doorvest agreement does), a $400 reserve per home covers one such repair plus a small bill.

## When does the manager use the reserve?

The manager uses the reserve for property bills that come due before enough rent is in the owner's ledger. Common uses:

1. **Repairs within the approval limit,** such as a plumber or an appliance part.
2. **Emergency work** that cannot wait, such as a burst pipe. The Doorvest agreement excludes emergency repairs from its expense limits.
3. **Utilities during vacancy,** when no rent comes in.
4. **Turnover costs** such as cleaning and rekeying.
5. **Recurring bills** the manager pays for the owner, such as HOA dues, if the agreement says so.

After the manager uses the reserve, it refills it from the next rent before paying the owner. The owner statement should show the reserve balance at the start, every charge against it, and the refill.

Worked example: rent is $1,800 and the management fee is $144. The reserve is $400. A $250 plumbing bill is paid from the reserve mid-month, leaving $150. Next month, the manager collects $1,800, takes the $144 fee, refills the reserve with $250, and pays the owner $1,406.

Watch for: a large repair beyond the reserve. The manager cannot pay it until the owner adds funds. Agree in advance how fast the owner will fund large repairs.

## Is the reserve held in the trust account?

The reserve is held in the trust account, because it is the owner's money. It sits on the owner's ledger, apart from 3 other balances:

| Balance | Whose money | Can it pay owner bills? |
|---|---|---|
| Owner reserve | Owner | Yes |
| Owner's current rent balance | Owner | Yes, before payout |
| Tenant security deposits | Tenant | No |
| Manager's earned fees | Manager | No; the DRE says to move them out within 25 days |

Do not use tenant deposits to cover an owner's repair. Do not leave your own fees in the account to cushion owner ledgers either; the DRE treats both as violations.

## What should owners ask about the reserve?

Owners should ask 5 questions before they sign:

1. **How much is the reserve,** per property or per door?
2. **When is it funded:** at signing or from the first rent?
3. **How is it refilled** after use?
4. **What is the approval limit** for repairs paid from it?
5. **When is it returned** if the agreement ends?
