## What review patterns are a warning?

A review pattern is a warning when several owners or tenants describe the same problem in their own words. One angry review can be a bad day. Five reviews that say "deposit never came back" describe a system.

Read the 1-star and 2-star reviews first. Then read the manager's replies. Sort what you find into this table:

| Pattern in reviews | What it can mean | Weight |
|--------------------|------------------|--------|
| Many tenants say repairs take weeks | No maintenance process, or the owner approval step stalls | High |
| Tenants say deposits come back late or not at all | Weak trust accounting, or deductions without proof | High |
| Owners say they could not reach anyone | Too many doors per manager | High |
| Owners mention surprise fees | Fees not clear in the contract | Medium |
| Staff names change every few months | High turnover; your file may get lost | Medium |
| Only 5-star reviews, all in one month | Reviews may be solicited in a burst | Medium |
| Manager replies blame the reviewer every time | Poor culture of fixing problems | Low to medium |

Tenant reviews matter to you as an owner. Tenants who wait 3 weeks for a repair move out. Each move-out costs you a leasing fee and empty weeks.

## What contract terms are a warning?

A contract term is a warning when it makes the fee hard to predict or the exit hard to use. Read the management agreement before you compare monthly rates. The monthly rate is only one line.

For a baseline, iPropertyManagement's 2026 survey found an average management fee of 8.49% of monthly rent. It found an average setup fee of $185.24. Managers that also manage the home charged an average leasing fee of 70.6% of one month's rent. A contract far above these numbers needs a reason.

Contract red flags checklist:

- **Long term with a large exit fee.** A 1-year term is common. A 3-year term with a fee of several months' management is a trap.
- **Fee on the full lease if you leave.** Some contracts charge the remaining management fee for the whole lease term when you cancel.
- **The manager keeps the tenant.** A clause that says you cannot keep a tenant the manager placed unless you pay a fee.
- **No maintenance approval limit.** The contract should say the dollar amount above which the manager must call you. iPropertyManagement found a typical maintenance reserve of $250 per unit.
- **Undisclosed markup.** If the manager adds a percentage to vendor bills, the contract should state the number.
- **Fees for vague items.** "Admin fee" or "technology fee" with no amount.
- **Late fees kept by the manager.** Find out who keeps late fees from tenants. Either answer can be fair. It must be written down.
- **No duty to send statements.** The contract should name a monthly statement date.

Ask for the full fee schedule on one page. A good manager can send it in a day. Then put the numbers into our [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to see the yearly cost in dollars.

## What reporting gaps are a warning?

A reporting gap is a warning when you cannot see where each dollar of rent went. You should get a monthly owner statement that ties to the bank deposits you receive.

A complete owner statement shows:

1. **Rent charged and rent collected**, per unit, with dates.
2. **Each expense**, with the vendor name and a copy of the invoice.
3. **Management and leasing fees**, as separate lines.
4. **Reserve balance** held for your property.
5. **Security deposit held**, per tenant.
6. **Net payout** and the date it was sent.

Year-end check: the IRS instructions for Form 1099-MISC say a property manager must use that form to report the rent it paid over to the property owner. If the manager cannot produce the form, or the total does not match your 12 statements, stop and ask why.

Other reporting gaps to watch:

- **Statements arrive later each month.** Late statements often come before late payouts.
- **Lump-sum expenses.** "Maintenance: $1,840" with no invoice.
- **No move-in or move-out photos.** Without them, you cannot back a deposit deduction.
- **No vacancy report.** You should know how many days the home sat empty and why.

## Can a property management company put my rental at risk?

Yes. A property management company can put your rental at risk through unlicensed work, mixed funds, and legal mistakes made in your name. The manager acts as your agent. Many of its errors become your problem.

Three risks, with state examples:

| Risk | State example | What to check |
|------|---------------|---------------|
| No license | Texas Occupations Code 1101.002 counts a person who controls the acceptance or deposit of rent from a single-family rental as a broker. Section 1101.351 says a person may not act as a broker without a license. | Look up the license on the state real estate commission site. |
| No license | Florida Statutes 475.01 defines a broker as a person who rents or offers to rent property for another for pay. Section 475.42 makes operating as a broker without a license a third-degree felony. | Ask for the broker license number in writing. |
| Mixed deposit funds | Florida Statutes 83.49 says the landlord or the landlord's agent must hold deposit money in a separate account in a Florida bank and must not commingle it. | Ask which bank holds the deposits and how the account is titled. |

Rules differ by state. Some states exempt certain managers, and some have no license rule for property management. Check your own state's real estate commission before you rely on any one example.

Other risks to your rental:

- **Fair Housing errors.** A manager who screens or advertises in a way that excludes a protected group exposes you too.
- **Wrong notices.** An eviction notice with the wrong number of days can restart the whole process.
- **Lapsed insurance or permits.** Some cities require a rental license or inspection. Ask who tracks the renewal dates.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## A 10-minute red flag check before you sign

Use this list on any manager you are about to hire. Each "no" is a point against them.

- [ ] License number given and confirmed on the state site.
- [ ] Full fee schedule on one page, with dollar amounts.
- [ ] Contract term of 12 months or less, with a clear exit.
- [ ] Written maintenance approval limit.
- [ ] Sample owner statement that shows invoices.
- [ ] Security deposits held in a separate trust or escrow account.
- [ ] Named contact person and a stated reply time.
- [ ] Reviews read, with no repeated pattern from the table above.
- [ ] Form 1099-MISC sent to owners each year.
- [ ] Move-in and move-out photo reports included.

If a manager scores 8 or more, you have a good base for talks. If you want more on how owners compare managers, read [how owners choose a property manager with AI](/guides/how-owners-choose-a-property-manager-with-ai).
