## What counts as rental income?

Rental income is any payment you receive for the use or occupation of property. That is the definition in IRS Publication 527. It is not limited to the normal monthly rent.

Pub 527 and IRS Tax Topic 414 list these as rental income:

| Payment | Is it rental income? | When |
|---------|---------------------|------|
| Monthly rent | Yes | The year you receive it (cash basis) |
| Advance rent | Yes | The year you receive it, whatever period it covers |
| Payment to cancel a lease | Yes | The year you receive it |
| Expenses the tenant pays for you (for example, your water bill) | Yes, and you may deduct the expense too | The year paid |
| Property or services instead of rent | Yes, at fair market value | The year received |
| Security deposit you will return | No | Not income |
| Part of a deposit you keep | Yes | The year you keep it |

Most individual owners use the cash method. Topic 414 says that means you count income when you actually or constructively receive it, and deduct expenses when you pay them.

This is general information, not tax advice. Check the current law or ask a tax professional.

## Are advance rent and security deposits income?

Advance rent is income in the year you receive it. A security deposit is not income until you keep it.

Pub 527 gives an example of advance rent. On March 18, 2025, an owner signs a 10-year lease. In 2025, the owner receives $9,600 for the first year's rent and $9,600 for the last year's rent. The owner reports $19,200 for 2025, because both payments arrived that year.

The deposit rules from Pub 527 and Topic 414:

- **You plan to return the deposit.** Do not include it in income.
- **You keep part of it because the tenant broke the lease.** Include the amount you keep in that year's income.
- **The "deposit" is really the last month's rent.** Treat it as advance rent. Include it when you receive it.

Worked example, with made-up numbers:

| Event | Amount | Income in that year? |
|-------|--------|----------------------|
| 2026: tenant pays deposit | $2,000 | $0 |
| 2026: tenant pays first and last month | $4,000 | $4,000 |
| 2027: tenant moves out, you keep $600 for damage | $600 | $600 in 2027 |

Topic 414 adds a detail on damage. If you keep a deposit to pay for repairs, include it in income if your practice is to deduct the repair cost as an expense.

## Which form reports rental income?

Most owners report rental income and expenses on Schedule E (Form 1040), Supplemental Income and Loss. IRS Tax Topic 414 names Schedule E for real estate rentals.

The exception is a rental with substantial services. Topic 414 says if you provide substantial services mainly for your tenant's convenience, you report on Schedule C instead. Pub 527 says basic services, such as heat and light, cleaning of public areas and trash collection, do not count as substantial.

What goes on Schedule E for each property:

1. Rents received.
2. Expenses by type: advertising, cleaning, insurance, management fees, mortgage interest, repairs, taxes, utilities and more.
3. Depreciation, from Form 4562 in the first year.
4. Net income or loss.

Pub 527 also notes a special case. If you use a home as a residence and rent it for fewer than 15 days in the year, you do not report the rent and do not deduct rental expenses (Tax Topic 415).

## How is net rental income figured?

Net rental income is rent minus deductible expenses and depreciation. You pay tax on the net, not on the gross rent.

Worked example for one house, with made-up numbers:

| Line | Amount per year |
|------|-----------------|
| Rent received (11 months x $2,000) | $22,000 |
| Mortgage interest | −$7,800 |
| Property tax | −$3,600 |
| Insurance | −$1,400 |
| Repairs | −$1,200 |
| Management fees | −$2,200 |
| Depreciation | −$7,273 |
| Net rental income (loss) | −$1,473 |

This owner collected $22,000 in rent and reports a loss. Depreciation is the reason: it is a deduction with no cash out that year. See our [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to estimate the management line.

## Is rental income earned or passive income?

Rental income is generally passive income, not earned income. IRS Publication 925 says a rental activity is a passive activity even if you materially participated, unless you qualify as a real estate professional.

What this means for you:

- **Self-employment tax.** Pub 527 raises self-employment tax for owners who provide substantial services and report on Schedule C. A plain long-term rental on Schedule E is not that case. Ask a tax professional about yours.
- **Losses are limited.** Passive losses generally offset only passive income.
- **The $25,000 allowance.** If you actively participated, Pub 527 lets you deduct up to $25,000 of rental loss against other income. The allowance is $12,500 for married people filing separately who lived apart all year.
- **The phase-out.** The full allowance applies if your modified adjusted gross income is $100,000 or less ($50,000 or less if married filing separately). It shrinks above that.
- **Real estate professional status.** Pub 925 requires more than half of your work time in real property businesses and more than 750 hours in the year.

Pub 527 says you actively participated if you owned at least 10% of the property and made management decisions or arranged for others to provide services in a significant and bona fide sense. Examples it gives: approving new tenants, deciding on rental terms and approving expenditures. An owner with a manager can still make those decisions.

Watch for: the Net Investment Income Tax. Pub 527 notes a 3.8% tax that may apply to net investment income, which can include rental income, above certain income levels.

## A year-end checklist for rental income

- **Total all rent received**, including advance rent and fees.
- **List deposits held** and any amount you kept.
- **List expenses the tenant paid for you.**
- **Gather the Form 1099s** from your manager or payment platform.
- **Keep receipts for every expense.**
- **Ask a tax professional** about the passive loss rules for your income level.
