## What is RUBS utility billing?

RUBS allocates a shared utility cost by a defined ratio instead of measuring each unit directly. The term commonly describes allocation based on approved factors such as occupancy or floor area.

The key distinction is measurement. A ratio can estimate a unit's share of cost, but it cannot show how much water a particular tenant actually used. Explain that distinction before the tenant signs.

Start with the provider's master bill and the legal method. Do not build a formula first and look for permission afterward. Different rules can govern water, electricity, internet and other services.

The Texas regulation at 16 TAC 24.281 sets out specific methods for allocated water and wastewater charges. It also addresses charges that cannot enter the tenant bill. [Read the Texas calculation rule](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/16-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-24-281).

Keep the term RUBS out of an explanation unless you define it. Tenants need to understand the actual charge, formula and evidence. A billing label alone answers none of those questions.

## How is each unit's share calculated?

Calculate the share from the billable cost pool and the approved allocation factor. Both parts need review before the tenant receives a charge.

A general arithmetic structure is: permitted shared cost multiplied by the unit's allocation share. That describes the math, not legal permission for a particular formula.

| Calculation input | Question to resolve | Supporting record |
|---|---|---|
| Provider bill | Which service and period? | Original utility invoice |
| Allowed cost pool | Which amounts may pass through? | Reviewed legal method |
| Exclusions | Which owner costs stay outside? | Deduction worksheet |
| Unit factor | Which lawful measure applies? | Occupancy or area record |
| Total factor | Which units and dates count? | Property allocation schedule |
| Tenant charge | Does the result reconcile? | Calculation and bill copy |

Texas 16 TAC 24.281 includes approved allocation approaches and common-area deductions. Its water rule does not mean the same method works for electricity or for another state.

Track changes to the input facts. A move-in, move-out or correction to occupancy can affect the calculation. Preserve the basis used for each billing period rather than overwriting the old schedule.

Have another person reproduce a sample bill. If the result cannot be explained from the provider invoice and worksheet, fix the inputs before billing.

## Is RUBS legal everywhere?

No. Permission depends on state utility rules, tenancy law, local requirements and the service involved. Check the property's legal framework rather than relying on a vendor's national product page.

Texas 16 TAC 24.281 has detailed water allocation rules, including prohibited owner charges. That is a specific regulated process.

Oregon ORS 90.315 allows specified utility charges when a written rental agreement provides for them. It requires explanation of allocation and access to the provider bill under the stated conditions. It also distinguishes utility charges from rent. [Read Oregon's utility statute](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors090.html).

These examples show different duties rather than a complete state survey. Ask local counsel or the utility regulator which rule applies to your building.

Check whether a mid-tenancy change is allowed. Existing lease language may not authorize a new billing method. A new software setting does not amend the agreement or satisfy a notice requirement.

Keep the review date and source in the property file. Recheck when you change providers, formulas or included services.

## How does RUBS compare with submetering?

RUBS allocates cost, while submetering measures consumption through unit-level equipment. Compare the accuracy, installation needs and legal process before choosing.

A submeter needs suitable equipment and a way to read and maintain it. The bill still needs the approved rate and lawful cost treatment. A meter does not eliminate the need to review the rules.

RUBS may avoid unit meter installation, but it needs reliable allocation inputs. Tenant questions often concern why their charge changed when their own habits did not.

Compare total management work. Include data updates, bill checks, tenant explanations and dispute handling. A cheap invoice service can still create a costly review process.

Use the [management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) for management planning, then add actual vendor quotes. Do not use a made-up per-unit fee as an industry benchmark.

If the building has unexplained high use, investigate leaks before changing billing methods. Allocation does not repair a running toilet or a faulty main line.

## What should the lease and tenant bill explain?

The lease and bill should explain the lawful method in terms the tenant can verify. Match the content to the actual governing requirements.

Use this recommended setup checklist:

- **Identify the service.** Name the utility and account structure.
- **Confirm permission.** Review the law and current lease before charging.
- **Define the formula.** Record allowed factors and cost exclusions.
- **Test the math.** Reconcile a sample charge to the provider bill.
- **Explain access.** Tell tenants how to review the supporting information.
- **Handle disputes.** Name the person who checks errors and issues corrections.

Do not blend an unsupported surcharge into the shared cost. Keep permitted charges separately labeled where the applicable rule requires that treatment.

Review the first live bills closely. Compare the unit roster, service period and provider invoice. Correct mistakes through a visible credit or adjustment, not by deleting the earlier history.

Your next step is a property-specific legal and billing review. Confirm the formula before asking tenants to pay a ratio-based charge.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.
