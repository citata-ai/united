## What is a security deposit account?

A security deposit account is a bank account that holds tenants' security deposits apart from the landlord's own funds. Buildium's dictionary defines it as "a separate account where landlords or property managers hold tenants' security deposits."

The money in it is not the landlord's money. It belongs to the tenant until the tenancy ends and the landlord makes lawful deductions. That is why states regulate where it sits.

There are 3 common ways to hold deposits:

| Setup | Who uses it | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| Separate deposit account in the owner's name | Self-managing landlords | Only deposits in it; nothing else |
| Broker trust account | Licensed property managers | Holds deposits and rents for many owners, with a ledger for each |
| Surety bond instead of an account | Allowed in some states, such as Florida | The landlord keeps the cash but posts a bond |

## Must security deposits be kept in a separate account?

Security deposits must be kept in a separate account in many states, but the exact rule depends on the state. Here are 2 clear examples.

**Florida (Stat. 83.49(1)).** The landlord must choose 1 of 3 options:

1. **A separate non-interest-bearing account** in a Florida financial institution, with no commingling.
2. **A separate interest-bearing account** in a Florida institution. The tenant gets at least 75% of the annualized average interest rate on the account, or 5% simple interest a year, at the landlord's choice.
3. **A surety bond** filed with the clerk of the circuit court, for the total deposits or $50,000, whichever is less. The landlord also pays 5% simple interest a year.

Florida landlords with 5 or more units must also tell the tenant in writing, within 30 days, where the money is held and whether interest is paid (Stat. 83.49(2)).

**New York (Gen. Oblig. Law 7-103).** Deposit money "shall be held in trust" and may not be mixed with the landlord's personal funds. If the money is in a bank, the landlord must tell the tenant in writing the bank's name and address and the amount. The bank must have a place of business in New York.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## Do deposits earn interest for the tenant?

Deposits earn interest for the tenant only where state or local law says so. In states without a rule, a non-interest account is usually fine, but check your city.

- **New York:** for property with 6 or more family units, deposits must be in an interest-bearing account at a New York bank, at the prevailing rate. The landlord may keep 1% a year as an administrative fee. The rest of the interest belongs to the tenant (GOL 7-103).
- **Florida:** interest is owed if the landlord picks the interest-bearing account or the surety bond. It must be paid at least once a year, to the tenant or as a rent credit (Stat. 83.49(9)).

Worked example in New York: a $2,400 deposit sits in an account that pays 3% a year. Interest for 1 year is $72. The landlord may keep 1% of the deposit, or $24, as the administrative fee. The tenant is owed $48.

## Is the deposit held in the manager's trust account?

When a licensed property manager holds the deposit, it usually sits in the manager's trust account, with a separate ledger for each owner and tenant. The California Department of Real Estate (DRE) explains the rules for brokers in its Trust Funds guide (RE 13).

Key points from the DRE guide:

- **No commingling.** A broker's own funds may not be mixed with trust funds. The guide allows only 2 exceptions: up to $200 of broker money to cover bank fees, and earned fees left in the account for no more than 25 days.
- **Broker-owned rentals are not trust funds.** Rents and deposits from properties the broker owns may not go into the trust account.
- **The balance must match the liability.** The account must always equal the total owed to all beneficiaries. A shortage is a violation.
- **No negative ledgers.** Paying out more for one owner than that owner has in the account uses other people's money.
- **Monthly reconciliation.** Reconcile the bank record with the bank statement and with each beneficiary's record every month.

Watch for: a manager who uses tenant deposits to cover an owner's repair bill. The deposit is not the owner's reserve. Keep the 2 balances apart in your ledgers.

## Who holds the deposit when a property manager changes?

The deposit moves with the management, to the new manager or back to the owner, and the tenant should be told. The old holder must pass on the money and the records.

Florida spells this out. When an owner sells or an agent changes, deposits and advance rent, with earned interest and an accounting for each tenant, must transfer to the new owner or agent. The old holder is released once it transfers the funds and records and gets a written receipt (Stat. 83.49(7)).

What to do at a manager change:

1. **Get a per-tenant deposit list** with amounts, dates and interest owed.
2. **Match the total** to the money being moved.
3. **Sign a receipt** for the funds and records.
4. **Send the tenant a new notice** of where the deposit is held, if your state requires it.

## What records must a landlord keep for deposits?

A landlord must keep a clear record of each deposit from receipt to return. The record protects you in a dispute and in an audit.

- [ ] Tenant name, unit and lease dates
- [ ] Deposit amount and date received
- [ ] Account or bond where it is held
- [ ] Interest rate and interest paid or credited
- [ ] Any written notice you sent about the deposit
- [ ] Move-in report and photos
- [ ] Move-out deductions with receipts
- [ ] Date and method of the final return

The California DRE says brokers must keep trust records for 3 years under Business and Professions Code 10148. Use that as a floor if your state sets no period.
