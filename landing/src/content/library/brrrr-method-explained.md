## What does each BRRRR step involve?

BRRRR combines a repair project with a rental business and a later loan application.

Baselane's BRRRR guide defines the steps as buy, rehab, rent, refinance, and repeat. The order is easy to remember. The hard part is making each step work without assuming the next step will rescue a bad decision.

Buy with both the building and the financing in view. Check condition, title concerns, insurance options, local rental requirements, and likely repair scope. Request financing terms before you commit. A cheap purchase can still require more cash than you can safely provide.

Rehab means completing the work needed for a safe, lawful, rentable home. Use a written scope and keep change orders. Rent means finding a tenant under consistent, lawful standards. Refinance means applying for a new loan under the lender's current conditions.

| Step | Evidence before moving on |
|---|---|
| Buy | Inspection, cost plan, and financing terms |
| Rehab | Completed scope, invoices, and required approvals |
| Rent | Lease, move-in records, and supported rent |
| Refinance | Appraisal, underwriting approval, and closing costs |
| Repeat | Funded reserves and sustainable cash flow |

Do not make repeat an automatic instruction. Finish the records and review the actual result first. A sound project can still leave less available cash than you expected.

## How does after-repair value affect a refinance?

After-repair value is an estimate of the property's value after work, and the lender relies on its accepted valuation.

A contractor's budget does not guarantee an equal increase in market value. A listing price also does not prove what an appraiser will conclude. Use comparable sales and ask a local lender how the valuation and loan process work.

An illustrative formula is value multiplied by the lender's permitted loan-to-value ratio. Then subtract the old loan payoff, closing costs, and other required deductions. The remainder is potential cash returned. A lender can apply other limits before approving that amount.

Ask about property condition, lease evidence, borrower qualifications, and timing conditions. Do not treat an assumed loan ratio as an industry promise. Obtain a term sheet for your own project and confirm what remains subject to underwriting.

Prepare a lower-value case before you buy. If a weaker appraisal leaves cash in the property, can you still pay the bills? If the lender offers less favorable terms, does rent cover the revised debt payment? Those questions matter more than recovering every dollar quickly.

Keep your value estimate and your lender's appraisal separate in the project file. Record when each estimate was made and what facts support it. Update the plan when the repair scope or local market changes.

## What are the main BRRRR risks?

BRRRR exposes you to repair, leasing, valuation, debt, and cash shortage risks at the same property.

Repair risk starts with hidden defects and unclear bids. Ask vendors to define included work and payment milestones. Keep a contingency based on the building's condition and the work still unknown. Do not spend the entire reserve on cosmetic upgrades before essential work ends.

Leasing risk includes a longer vacancy or lower supported rent. Check local competition before setting the purchase budget. A renovated house still needs a tenant who accepts its location, terms, and price. Apply screening standards consistently and review local lease duties.

Debt risk includes a loan that cannot close as planned. Confirm how you will carry the original financing if the refinance takes longer. Review maturity dates, extension costs, and any terms that change after the repair phase.

Tax records need their own care. IRS Publication 527 distinguishes repairs from improvements and explains depreciation. Do not assume every rehab invoice is an immediate deduction. Give the preparer itemized work records and the date the property became ready for rent.

Compare ongoing management costs with the [fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator). Add capital work and financing costs outside that estimate. A positive operating result can still leave too little cash after debt and future replacement needs.

## Can you show a worked BRRRR example?

A worked example should show both the cash recovered and the cash still tied up in the property.

All figures in this example are assumptions, not quoted loan terms or expected investment results. Assume a $150,000 purchase, $40,000 rehab, and $10,000 in purchase and carrying costs. Total project cost is $200,000. Assume an initial loan balance of $140,000 and owner cash of $60,000.

Now assume a lender accepts a $250,000 value and a 75% loan-to-value ratio. The resulting loan amount is $187,500. Subtract the $140,000 payoff and assumed refinance costs of $5,000. Potential cash returned is $42,500. The owner still has $17,500 of the original cash invested.

Test a weaker appraisal using the same assumptions. At a $220,000 value and the same assumed ratio, the new loan is $165,000. After the payoff and costs, cash returned falls to $20,000. Cash left invested rises to $40,000.

Neither example proves positive rental cash flow. Add a separate rent and expense budget with the actual new debt payment. Include vacancy, insurance, taxes, repairs, management, and replacements. Compare the monthly result with the cash you must keep available.

Before another purchase, compare estimates with actual invoices and collected rent. Identify which assumptions failed and update the next budget. Repeat only when the finished property can operate without using the next project's cash.
