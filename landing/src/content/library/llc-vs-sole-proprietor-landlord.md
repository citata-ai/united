## How does liability differ between personal ownership and an LLC?

An LLC creates a separate legal structure, while holding title yourself does not. The SBA explains that a sole proprietor has unlimited personal liability. The SBA also describes LLC protection as limited and recommends insurance for gaps.

The term sole proprietor can confuse rental owners. Holding a rental in your own name does not mean every rental activity has the same tax treatment as a service business. Decide who owns the property, then decide how that activity must be reported.

| Question | Personal ownership | LLC ownership |
|---|---|---|
| Name on the deed | Individual owner | The entity after a valid transfer |
| Separate entity records | No LLC records | Formation and ongoing entity records |
| Liability planning | Personal exposure needs review | Protection has limits and exceptions |
| Existing mortgage | Borrower remains responsible | Deed transfer does not release borrower |
| Federal income tax | Often Schedule E for ordinary rentals | Often the same for a disregarded single-member LLC |

Ask a local attorney to assess your actual exposure. The property condition, contracts, ownership, and conduct all matter. Do not buy an entity filing package based only on a claim that tenants cannot sue you.

## How do costs and paperwork differ?

An LLC adds formation and upkeep work. State filing fees, reports, registered agent needs, and local requirements differ. Obtain those costs from the state where the property sits before making a budget.

You also need an operating agreement suited to the owners. The SBA describes an operating agreement as a document for financial and management decisions. Agree on authority, distributions, funding, and a sale before a dispute arises.

Keep the entity's records useful. Record owner contributions and distributions. Identify which account pays repairs. Save the deed, insurance documents, bank records, and contracts under the same ownership name.

Personal ownership still needs proper books. A simpler legal structure does not make mixed bank transactions easier to prove. Keep a property ledger and match payments to receipts regardless of your entity choice.

Build a cost worksheet with written quotes rather than a national LLC fee estimate. Include legal advice, recording costs, possible lender work, insurance changes, and annual administration. Separate initial costs from recurring costs.

## How do loans differ?

The loan contract controls your duties, and an LLC deed does not cancel them. Ask the servicer about title transfers before you record any document. Confirm the investor rules and whether approval or a specific exemption applies.

Fannie Mae's servicing guide allows certain transfers to a borrower-controlled or majority-owned LLC. Its rule applies to loans purchased or securitized on or after June 1, 2016. Other conditions include compliance with the security instrument and permitted occupancy changes.

That rule is not permission for every mortgage. The servicer must determine whether the loan and proposed transfer qualify. Ask for a written response tied to your loan number and proposed ownership.

Fannie Mae also says the property must return to a natural person to qualify for a refinance under its selling requirements. Consider that future step before transferring title. A structure that fits today may add work when you borrow again.

Get new financing terms directly from a lender when buying through an entity. Compare rate, fees, guarantees, term, and repayment structure. Do not assume a loan marketed for investors matches a consumer home mortgage.

## How does tax reporting differ?

A single-member LLC often leaves federal income tax reporting unchanged. The IRS generally treats it as disregarded unless it elects corporate treatment. Its activity then appears on the owner's return under the rules for that activity.

IRS Publication 527 says ordinary residential rental income and expenses generally belong on Schedule E. Providing substantial services can change reporting. An LLC name alone does not choose the correct tax schedule.

An LLC with additional members may have different filing duties. The IRS also describes special rules for spouses and community property. Ask a tax professional to classify the actual ownership rather than copying another landlord's return.

Keep state tax separate from federal treatment. A disregarded federal entity can still have state fees or filings. Ask your adviser to identify each recurring duty in writing before you form the company.

## What does insurance still need to cover?

Insurance should match the property use and the parties who need protection. Tell the insurer who holds title, who manages the home, and how the home is rented. Ask for written confirmation of the proposed policy setup.

The SBA warns that entity protection has limits. Treat insurance and entity choice as parts of the same review. Ask what happens if a claim names both the owner and the LLC.

Budget for suitable coverage before comparing returns. You can include quoted operating costs in the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator). Do not use a projected tax saving to justify insurance you have not priced.

## How should you make the decision?

Choose the structure after you review the property, loan, insurance, and state rules together. A single answer for all owners hides the costs that matter.

- **Ask the attorney** how the entity changes your actual legal exposure.
- **Ask the servicer** whether the current loan permits the proposed deed transfer.
- **Ask the insurer** which owner names and rental uses the policy must list.
- **Ask the tax professional** how the entity changes federal and state reporting.
- **Write the upkeep plan** for reports, bank records, and owner decisions.
