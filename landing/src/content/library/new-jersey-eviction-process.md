New Jersey protects most residential tenants with the Anti-Eviction Act, N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.1 to 61.12. Under it, you can remove a tenant only for a listed good cause, and only through the court. I read the statute text that the Department of Community Affairs (DCA) publishes, plus the DCA Truth in Renting guide, on 2026-10-19.

| Rule | Limit or deadline | Statute |
|---|---|---|
| Good cause needed | Yes, for most residential rentals | N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.1, 61.3 |
| Notice to quit, nonpayment of rent | None before filing | N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.2 |
| Notice to quit, disorderly conduct or damage | 3 days | N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.2(a) |
| Notice to quit, rule or lease breach, habitual late rent | 1 month, after a notice to cease | N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.2(b) |
| Notice to quit, refusal of reasonable lease changes | 1 month | N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.2(e) |
| Notice to quit, owner move-in (3 units or fewer) | 2 months, and not before a written lease ends | N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.2(f) |
| Notice to quit, retire the building from residential use | 18 months | N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.2(d) |
| Notice to quit, condominium conversion | 3 years | N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.2(g) |

## What notice must a New Jersey landlord give before filing an eviction for unpaid rent?

For unpaid rent, New Jersey law needs no notice to quit before you file. N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.2 requires a written notice for each good cause except nonpayment of rent and nonpayment of a rent increase. The DCA guide adds one exception: in federally subsidized housing, it says a 14-day notice comes first.

Check the late fee before you file. N.J.S.A. 2A:42-6.1 gives some tenants 5 business days of grace when rent is due on the first. It covers tenants on Social Security old age or disability pensions, SSI, other government pensions and Work First New Jersey. You may charge no late fee inside that grace period.

The tenant can also stop a nonpayment case. N.J.S.A. 2A:18-55 says the case stops if the tenant pays the clerk the rent claimed plus costs. The tenant may pay at any time on or before final judgment.

## How long does an eviction take in New Jersey?

A New Jersey eviction takes at least the notice period, the time to trial, and two short waits after judgment. The statute does not set a date for the trial. The court calendar in your county decides that. The fixed waits come from the statute:

1. **Serve the notice.** Use the period for your cause, from 0 days for nonpayment to 3 years for a condominium conversion (N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.2).
2. **File the complaint** after the notice period ends.
3. **Go to trial.** The DCA guide says both sides must appear. If you miss court, the judge may dismiss. If the tenant misses court, the judge may enter a default judgment.
4. **Wait 3 days after judgment.** No warrant of removal may issue before then (N.J.S.A. 2A:18-57). The DCA guide counts this as 3 business days.
5. **Serve the warrant.** The officer may execute it no earlier than the third day after personal service. Saturdays, Sundays and court holidays do not count (N.J.S.A. 2A:42-10.16).

The DCA guide says you must ask for the warrant within 30 days of the judgment. It also says a tenant may ask the court for a stay of up to 1 year in hard cases. N.J.S.A. 2A:18-59.1 allows 1-year stays for a terminally ill tenant who meets its tests.

## Where does a landlord file an eviction case in New Jersey, and what does it cost?

A New Jersey landlord files the eviction complaint with the clerk of the Special Civil Part, Superior Court, Law Division. The DCA guide says you file in the county where the rental unit is. The clerk has the landlord-tenant complaint form.

The filing fee is set by the New Jersey courts. I could not open the court fee page at njcourts.gov on 2026-10-19, so I give no number here. Ask the county Special Civil Part clerk for the current fee before you file.

## Can a New Jersey landlord evict a tenant for a lease violation or with no cause?

A New Jersey landlord can evict for a lease violation, but not with no cause. N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.3 says no landlord may evict or fail to renew a lease except for good cause. The end of a lease term alone is not good cause.

The good causes in N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.1 include:

- **Nonpayment of rent** (a), and nonpayment of a lawful rent increase (f).
- **Disorderly conduct** after a written notice to cease (b).
- **Willful or grossly negligent damage** to the unit (c).
- **Breach of reasonable rules** after a notice to cease (d).
- **Breach of a lease covenant** after a notice to cease, if the lease reserves a right of re-entry (e).
- **Refusal of reasonable lease changes** at the end of a lease (i).
- **Habitual late rent** after a notice to cease (j).
- **Owner move-in or sale** to a buyer who will live there, in a building of 3 units or fewer (l).
- **End of a job** that the tenancy depended on, such as a superintendent (m).

For a lease violation, the steps are fixed. First serve a notice to cease. If the conduct goes on, serve a notice to quit. N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.2 says the notice must state the cause in detail. Serve it in person, at the home with a family member over 14, or by certified mail.

The Anti-Eviction Act does not cover owner-occupied premises with 2 or fewer rental units. For a holdover there, N.J.S.A. 2A:18-56 needs a 1-month notice for a month-to-month tenancy. A tenancy from year to year needs 3 months.

## Who removes the tenant after a judgment in New Jersey?

A court officer removes the tenant after a judgment, never the landlord. N.J.S.A. 2A:18-57 says the court issues its warrant to an officer of the court. N.J.S.A. 2A:42-10.16 says the warrant may be executed only by a court officer.

The same section sets the hours. The officer executes the warrant between 8 a.m. and 6 p.m., unless the court orders otherwise for good cause. The warrant must tell the tenant about the right to ask for a stay and about county welfare help.

After the removal, belongings follow N.J.S.A. 2A:18-72 to 84, the abandoned property law. The DCA guide says you may not keep the tenant's things.

## Is a self-help eviction (lockout, utility shutoff) legal in New Jersey?

No, a self-help eviction is illegal in New Jersey. N.J.S.A. 2A:39-1 bars entry into a home without the occupant's consent, unless it follows legal process. A person who breaks this rule is a disorderly person.

The DCA guide says a lockout includes new locks, a padlock or a utility shutoff meant to force the tenant out. The tenant may sue. N.J.S.A. 2A:39-8 gives possession plus all damages, court costs and reasonable attorney's fees. If a return to the unit is not a fit remedy, the court awards treble damages.
