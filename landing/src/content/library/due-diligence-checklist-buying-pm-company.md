## How do I use this due diligence checklist?

Due diligence is a review of the seller's records before you close. It shows whether the price and the risk match. Do it under a condition in the purchase contract. Jo-Anne Oliveri, writing for Buildium in 2012, compares it to a building inspection: if the review finds a problem, you can renegotiate or walk away.

There are two parts:

- **Financial due diligence:** the numbers. Buildium (2026) advises at least three years of profit and loss statements, tax returns and cash flow reports.
- **Operational due diligence:** the doors, the people and the processes. Oliveri calls this "just as critical" as the financial review.

Work through the five groups below. Mark each line done, missing or a problem. Bring an attorney and an accountant who know property management for the legal and tax parts.

## How do I check the trust account?

Check that every dollar the seller holds for owners and tenants is in the bank. Owner funds and tenant security deposits belong to clients, not to the company. CT Acquisitions (2026) notes that they do not transfer as part of the purchase price.

State rules set the standard. California is a clear example. The California Department of Real Estate (DRE) describes these rules in its list of common audit violations:

| California rule | What it requires |
|---|---|
| Regulation 2831 | A columnar record of all trust funds in and out, with a daily running balance |
| Regulation 2831.1 | A separate record for each beneficiary, with a running balance |
| Regulation 2831.2 | Reconcile the beneficiary records to the trust record at least once a month |
| Regulation 2832.1 | Written consent from all owners before a payment that drops the account below what the broker owes them |

The DRE says reconciliation also checks that broker funds above $200 do not sit in the trust account. Your state may have different rules. Check them with your state's real estate regulator.

Trust account checklist:

- [ ] List every trust account: operating, security deposit, HOA.
- [ ] Get 12 months of bank statements for each account.
- [ ] Get 12 months of reconciliations, with the date and preparer.
- [ ] Check that the sum of owner and tenant ledgers equals the reconciled bank balance.
- [ ] List every security deposit by tenant and compare it with the leases.
- [ ] Look for negative owner ledgers. A negative balance can mean one owner's money paid another owner's bill.
- [ ] Look for unidentified deposits or company money in the trust account.
- [ ] Ask if a regulator audited the accounts, and get the results.

A gap found here can delay or reprice the deal, says CT Acquisitions. A large gap is a reason to stop.

This is general information, not legal or accounting advice. Check your state's current trust account rules or ask a local attorney.

## Which agreements must transfer?

The management agreements are the asset you buy. Read every one, not a sample.

The deal structure decides what happens to them. In a stock or membership-interest sale, the contracting company stays the same. In an asset sale, agreements that require owner consent to assign need a notice and consent process (CT Acquisitions, 2026).

Agreement checklist:

- [ ] Get a signed agreement for every door on the rent roll.
- [ ] Note doors with no signed agreement.
- [ ] Read the assignment clause: is owner consent needed?
- [ ] Read the termination clause: notice period and fees.
- [ ] Check that the fees charged match the fees in each agreement.
- [ ] List all vendor contracts, software contracts and office leases.
- [ ] Check which vendor and software contracts can transfer.
- [ ] Get the current lease for every occupied unit.

Short notice periods matter. CT Acquisitions says buyers discount agreements that owners can exit quickly.

## How do I check owner churn history?

Churn history shows how many doors you can expect to keep. Ask for a door-by-door list, not a summary.

Oliveri recommends a breakdown of how long each property has been under management. If most doors are 12 months old or less, the risk is high. Those owners have not built loyalty to the seller.

Use a table like this one. The numbers are an example, not data:

| Years under management | Doors | Share | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Less than 1 | 60 | 30% | High |
| 1 to 3 | 70 | 35% | Medium |
| More than 3 | 70 | 35% | Low |
| Total | 200 | 100% | — |

Churn checklist:

- [ ] Get doors gained and lost by month for at least three years.
- [ ] Get the reason for each lost door: owner sold, owner self-managed, owner switched.
- [ ] Get the start date of every current door.
- [ ] Find the share of doors held by the largest five owners.
- [ ] Read the last 12 months of owner complaints and online reviews.
- [ ] Ask which owners know about the sale. Oliveri warns that owners who were not told may feel deceived.

## Which legal and license risks should I look for?

License gaps and open disputes come with the company in a stock purchase. In an asset purchase you may still inherit unhappy owners and tenants. Buildium (2026) advises a legal review of contracts, leases, vendor obligations and pending disputes, and a check of licensing and trust accounting.

Legal and license checklist:

- [ ] Confirm the broker or property management license for the company and its responsible broker.
- [ ] Check the regulator's site for past discipline.
- [ ] List all lawsuits, claims and fair housing complaints from the last five years.
- [ ] List all pending evictions and security deposit disputes.
- [ ] Get insurance policies and claims history: errors and omissions, general liability.
- [ ] Check that the company uses the fictitious business name on its license. The California DRE lists the wrong name as a common audit finding.
- [ ] Check employee status, pay records and any non-compete or non-solicit terms.
- [ ] Check tax filings and any unpaid taxes.

## Which operational items should I check?

Operations decide how hard the handover will be. Buildium (2026) advises a review of staff roles, tenure and pay, software, vendor agreements and maintenance processes.

- [ ] List staff by role, tenure and pay. Mark who owners and tenants know by name.
- [ ] Name the software for accounting, maintenance and owner portals.
- [ ] Get open work orders and their age.
- [ ] List the top 20 vendors and their insurance certificates.
- [ ] Find out how much work the seller does in person.

If the seller does most of the work, plan for a long transition period. Write the seller's help after closing into the contract.
