## Which records do screening reports search?

Tenant screening reports search court records for eviction cases filed under the applicant's name. A consumer reporting agency (a company that sells screening reports) collects these records and matches them to the applicant.

The data is messy. The CFPB's 2022 report on tenant background checks says screening companies get eviction and criminal records from more than 13,000 federal, state and local courts. There is no single government database for these records.

Matching errors follow. The same CFPB report cites a study of 3.6 million eviction court records from 12 states. The study found that "on average, 22% of state eviction cases are ambiguous or false records."

| What the report may show | What it may miss |
|---|---|
| Case filed, date, court, landlord name | How the case ended |
| Judgment amount, if any | Dismissal, settlement or tenant win |
| A match by name and date of birth | A wrong match to a person with a similar name |
| The same case twice | That the two entries are one case |

What to do: treat each eviction line as a lead to check, not a final answer.

## How do I run an eviction check?

Run the check in this order. It keeps you inside the FCRA (Fair Credit Reporting Act) and gets you the real outcome.

1. **Get written consent.** The FTC says a landlord needs a permissible purpose to get a consumer report. Written permission from the applicant shows it.
2. **Order the report.** Use a screening company that searches eviction records in the states where the applicant lived.
3. **List each case.** Note the court, the case number and the filing date.
4. **Look up the case at the court.** Find the outcome: judgment, dismissal or settlement.
5. **Ask the applicant.** Give the applicant a chance to explain or show proof, such as a dismissal order.
6. **Apply your written criteria.** Use the same rule for every applicant.
7. **Send an adverse action notice if you deny.** The FTC requires it when a consumer report played any part in the decision.

## Are court records free to search?

Court records are public in many places, but access differs by court. Some courts have an online case search. Some charge per search or per copy. Some need a visit to the clerk. Each state runs its own system, so check the court site in each county where the applicant lived.

Court records have 2 limits:

- **Name matching.** A court search by name can return another person's case. Confirm with the case details, the address and the applicant.
- **Access limits.** Some states restrict access to eviction records, as the next section shows.

A court lookup is also how you confirm what a screening report says. If the report shows a filing, the court docket shows if it ended in a judgment.

## Do sealed evictions show up?

Sealed evictions should not show up, but sometimes they do. In October 2023, the FTC and CFPB announced a settlement with TransUnion and its rental screening unit. The agencies alleged the unit failed to put in place reasonable procedures to keep sealed eviction records out of its reports. They also alleged it reported cases as filed without noting a later dismissal. The settlement totaled $15 million: $11 million for consumers and a $4 million civil penalty.

States seal or restrict eviction records in different ways. Here are 2 examples.

| State | Rule | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Minnesota | The court must expunge an eviction case if the tenant won, the case was dismissed for any reason, the parties agreed, or 3 years passed since the eviction was ordered | Minn. Stat. 484.014, subd. 3 |
| California | Eviction case records are not open to the public for 60 days after filing. They open to the public only if the landlord wins a judgment, under set conditions | Cal. Code Civ. Proc. 1161.2 |

If an applicant says a case is sealed or expunged, ask for the court order. Do not use a sealed record against the applicant.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## How old an eviction should count?

Federal law sets an outer limit on what a screening report can show. Under 15 U.S.C. 1681c(a)(2), civil suits and civil judgments generally cannot appear on a consumer report once they are more than 7 years old. Other adverse items, except criminal convictions, have the same 7-year limit.

Within that limit, you choose your own lookback, and you write it down. A shorter lookback than 7 years is a valid choice. Whatever you pick, apply it to every applicant.

Here is a sample written policy. Adjust it to your state and local law.

| Record | Sample policy |
|---|---|
| Filing that was dismissed or won by the tenant | Do not count |
| Sealed or expunged record | Do not count |
| Judgment for the landlord, older than your lookback | Do not count |
| Judgment for the landlord, within your lookback, debt paid | Review with context |
| Judgment for the landlord, within your lookback, debt unpaid | May deny, with an adverse action notice |

Some cities and states limit how far back you may look, or bar some eviction records outright. Check local rules before you set your lookback.

## Checklist: eviction history check

- [ ] Written consent from the applicant
- [ ] Screening report from a consumer reporting agency
- [ ] Case number, court and date for each eviction line
- [ ] Case outcome confirmed in court records
- [ ] Sealed or expunged records excluded
- [ ] Written lookback period, the same for every applicant
- [ ] Adverse action notice sent if you deny or add conditions
- [ ] Report stored securely, then disposed of securely, as the FTC requires
