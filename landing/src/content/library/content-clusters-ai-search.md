## What is a content cluster vs a pillar page?

A content cluster is a group of pages about one owner topic, and the pillar page is the hub of that group. The pillar covers the broad question. Each supporting page answers one narrow question in full. Links join them in both directions.

| Part | What it does | Example for a Columbus manager |
|---|---|---|
| Pillar page | Covers the broad topic, links to every supporting page | "Renting out your home in Columbus: an owner guide" |
| Supporting page | Answers one narrow question, links back to the pillar | "How much rent can I charge for a 3-bed in Columbus?" |
| Tool or table | Gives a number the reader can use | A rent or fee calculator, a fee table |
| City or service page | Turns the reader into a call | "Property management in Columbus" |

Property Manager Websites describes the same model in its post on clustering. A pillar page covers a central topic in depth, and supporting pages cover narrower subtopics and link back.

## Why do clusters matter for AI search?

Clusters matter for AI search because AI answers break one question into many smaller searches. Google says AI Overviews and AI Mode may use "query fan-out": they run several related searches across subtopics and data sources. While they write a response, they find more supporting pages.

So an owner who asks "Should I hire a property manager in Columbus?" may trigger searches on fees, screening, local law and vacancy. A site with one clear page for each subtopic gives the engine more places to find you. A site with one long page has one chance.

Google also says there are no special requirements to appear in AI Overviews. A page must be indexed and eligible to show with a snippet. Clusters do not replace that base. They make each page easy to find through internal links, which Google lists as a best practice.

## How many posts should one cluster have?

Plan 1 pillar and 6 to 12 supporting pages per cluster. Property Manager Websites gives that range in its clustering post. It is a rule of thumb, not a Google rule.

Google says it has no preferred word count. Its helpful content guide asks: "Are you writing to a particular word count because you've heard or read that Google has a preferred word count? (No, we don't.)" Let the owner questions set the size.

A simple test for the right size:

- **Too small:** an owner reads the cluster and still has a common question with no page.
- **Right size:** each real owner question you hear on calls has one page.
- **Too big:** two pages answer the same question in different words.

Google's guide also warns against "producing lots of content on many different topics in hopes that some of it might perform well." Finish one cluster before you start a second one.

## Which owner topics make good clusters?

The best clusters come from questions owners ask you on the phone. Property Manager Websites lists owner education, tenant placement, maintenance, legal compliance, pricing and local market insights as core cluster areas. Here is how those become clusters with real pages.

| Cluster | Pillar page | Supporting pages (examples) |
|---|---|---|
| Fees | What property management costs in [city] | Leasing fee, monthly fee, renewal fee, what "no hidden fees" means, flat fee vs percent |
| Tenant screening | How we screen tenants in [city] | Credit and income rules, application fee, how long screening takes, Fair Housing basics |
| Local landlord law | Landlord rules in [city, state] | Security deposit limit and deadline, notice to enter, late fees, eviction steps |
| Rent and vacancy | How to price a rental in [city] | Rent by bedroom count, days on market, when to lower rent, cost of a vacant month |
| Maintenance | How repairs work with a manager | Who approves repairs, repair limit, emergency calls, turnover costs |
| Section 8 | Renting to voucher holders in [city] | Payment standards, inspections, paperwork, timing of first payment |

Write each page for the owner, not for the search engine. Google's guide asks whether content shows "first-hand expertise and a depth of knowledge". Your own numbers, such as average days to lease in your area, give that proof. If a page cites a law, link the official statute or city page.

## How do you link a cluster?

Link every supporting page to the pillar, and link the pillar to every supporting page. Google says every page you care about needs a link from at least one other page on your site. A cluster makes sure no page is left alone.

Step list:

1. **Build the pillar first.** Give each planned supporting page a short summary and a link slot.
2. **Publish a supporting page.** Link it to the pillar in the first 100 words.
3. **Add the link back.** Put a link from the pillar to the new page inside the matching paragraph.
4. **Link sideways where it helps.** Link the deposit page to the move-out page, for example, if a reader needs both.
5. **Link to the money page.** End each page with one link to your city service page or fee table.
6. **Check the links.** Use normal `a href` links. Google says it can generally crawl only links in an `a` element with an `href`.

Anchor text rules from Google's link guide:

- **Be descriptive.** "Columbus security deposit rules" tells the reader and Google where the link goes. "Click here" does not.
- **Be short.** Keep the anchor to a few words.
- **Do not stuff keywords.** Write as you would talk.
- **Do not stack links.** Put each link in its own sentence, so the text around it gives context.

Google adds that there is "no magical ideal number of links" for a page. Link where the reader needs the next step.

## How do you start your first cluster this month?

Start with the cluster closest to a signed contract. For many managers, that is fees.

1. **List 10 fee questions** from calls, emails and owner reviews.
2. **Merge duplicates.** Keep 6 to 12 questions.
3. **Write the pillar** with a full fee table.
4. **Write 2 supporting pages a week.** Each one answers one question first, then gives detail.
5. **Check after 6 weeks.** Run the fee prompts in ChatGPT and Gemini and see which pages they cite.
