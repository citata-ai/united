## Rent vs sell at a glance

Selling turns your equity into cash now. Renting keeps the asset and gives you monthly income, with costs and work.

| Factor | Sell now | Rent it out |
|--------|----------|-------------|
| Cash now | Equity minus selling costs | None, and you may spend on make-ready |
| Monthly cash | None | Rent minus all costs and the loan |
| Your time | One sale process | Ongoing: tenants, repairs, taxes |
| Market risk | Ends at closing | You keep the risk and the upside |
| Tax on gain | Up to $250,000/$500,000 excluded if you pass the 2-of-5 test (Pub 523) | Exclusion fades after about 3 years of renting; depreciation is taxed at sale |
| Mortgage | Paid off | Stays; check the occupancy clause if under 1 year |

Check one item first: the standard Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac mortgage (Illinois Form 3014) requires you to live in the home for at least 1 year after you move in, unless the lender agrees in writing. If you are inside that year, call your servicer before you rent.

## What does it cost to sell a house versus rent it out?

Selling has large one-time costs. Renting has smaller costs that repeat every month.

Cost to sell:

- **Agent commissions.** Get a written figure from each agent.
- **Seller closing costs.** Title, escrow, recording, and transfer taxes where your state or city charges them.
- **Repairs and prep.** Paint, fixes from the buyer's inspection, staging.
- **Carrying costs while listed.** Loan, taxes, insurance and utilities each month until closing.

Cost to rent:

- **Make-ready.** Paint, clean, safety items, locks.
- **Vacancy.** The US rental vacancy rate was 7.3% in the second quarter of 2026 (Census Bureau).
- **Repairs and capital reserves.** Roof, HVAC, water heater.
- **Insurance change.** A landlord or dwelling policy instead of homeowners.
- **Management.** iPropertyManagement's 2026 average is 8.49% of rent, plus a leasing fee averaging 70.6% of one month's rent.

Ask a local agent for a "net sheet": a written estimate of what you walk away with at your price.

## How much cash flow will my house make as a rental?

Your house's cash flow is rent minus vacancy, operating costs, reserves and the loan payment. Run it with every line, even if you plan to self-manage.

Worked example, with made-up numbers:

| Line | Per month |
|------|-----------|
| Market rent (from comps) | $2,400 |
| Vacancy (7%) | −$168 |
| Property tax | −$350 |
| Landlord insurance | −$140 |
| Repairs (5%) | −$120 |
| Capital reserve (5%) | −$120 |
| Management (8.49%) | −$204 |
| Leasing fee reserve (70.6% of rent / 24 months) | −$71 |
| Principal and interest (existing loan) | −$1,050 |
| Monthly cash flow | $177 |
| Of which principal paid down (part of the loan payment) | about $350 |

This owner has a low-rate loan, so the house cash-flows. A new buyer at today's rates might not. That is a common reason owners rent rather than sell.

## How does renting affect the home sale tax exclusion?

Renting can end your home sale exclusion if you rent too long. IRS Publication 523 lets you exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly, if you owned the home and used it as your main home for at least 24 months of the 5 years before the sale.

Three points for an owner who rents:

1. **The 3-year window.** If you lived there 2 years, you can rent up to about 3 years and still pass the test.
2. **Rental after you move out is not nonqualified use.** Pub 523 excludes from nonqualified use any part of the 5-year period after the last date you used the home as your main home.
3. **Depreciation is taxed.** Pub 523 says you cannot exclude gain equal to depreciation allowed or allowable after May 6, 1997.

Once you rent, depreciation starts when the home is ready and available for rent (IRS Pub 527). The basis is the lower of your adjusted basis or the fair market value on the date it becomes a rental.

This is general information, not tax advice. Check the current law or ask a tax professional.

## How do I compare the two options with real numbers?

Compare the cash you have at the end of a set period, such as 3 years, under each option. Use the same house and the same sale price assumptions.

Worked 3-year comparison, with made-up numbers. Single owner, lived in the house 4 years before moving out.

| Line | Sell now | Rent 3 years, then sell |
|------|----------|-------------------------|
| Sale price | $400,000 | $430,000 (example: about 2.5% a year growth) |
| Selling costs (example: 7%) | −$28,000 | −$30,100 |
| Loan payoff | −$220,000 | −$207,400 (after 3 years of principal) |
| Cash from sale | $152,000 | $192,500 |
| 3 years of cash flow ($177 x 36) | $0 | $6,372 |
| Tax on gain | $0 (gain under $250,000, excluded) | Tax on about $21,000 of depreciation |
| Gain excluded? | Yes | Yes, if the sale closes inside the window |
| Total before depreciation tax | $152,000 | $198,872 |

In this example, renting wins by about $47,000 before the depreciation tax, but only because the price grows and the tenant pays. If the price stays flat, renting adds only the cash flow and principal paid down, minus the depreciation tax and any big repair.

Test these changes before you decide:

- **Price flat for 3 years.**
- **A $10,000 repair** in year 2.
- **3 months of vacancy** at once.
- **Selling after the window closes.**

## When does selling make more sense than renting?

Selling makes more sense in these cases:

- **Negative cash flow** after all costs.
- **You need the equity** for your next home or to clear debt.
- **You are near the end of the 3-year window** and the gain is large.
- **The house needs major work** you cannot fund.
- **You do not want to be a landlord.** Time and stress count.

Renting makes more sense when the cash flow is positive, your loan rate is low, you can manage or pay a manager, and you plan to sell inside the window or hold for the long term. For more owner guides, see [/guides](/guides).
