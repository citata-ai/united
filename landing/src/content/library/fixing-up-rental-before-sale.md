## Which fixes can raise the sale price?

Choose fixes that solve a known defect or support a local buyer's price decision. A fresh finish has little value if the roof still leaks. Start with a condition review, then ask a local sales agent for evidence from closed sales.

Do not treat a renovation budget as a price forecast. Ask for an as-is price range and a separate range after the proposed work. Both estimates should use homes with similar size, condition and sale terms. Ask which changes support the difference between those ranges.

Use this work list before you ask for bids:

| Work group | Owner's question | Proof to request |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Active damage | Will delay make the damage worse? | Inspection notes and repair scope |
| Safety defect | What must a qualified trade inspect? | Written findings and completion record |
| Basic condition | Does the defect block a normal showing? | Photos and local sale feedback |
| Cosmetic work | Will buyers pay for this finish? | Relevant closed sales |
| Major upgrade | Can the sale recover the full added cost? | Detailed bid and revised sale estimate |

This is a decision checklist, not a claim that each project earns a fixed return. Keep your own taste out of the budget. Choose neutral work that makes the home's condition clear.

Request bids with labor, materials, permits and cleanup shown separately. Keep a written scope for each contractor. A low quote is hard to compare when it omits part of the work.

## Are fix-up costs deductible or added to basis?

Classify each cost by what the work does, not by its distance from the sale date. IRS Publication 527 says repairs may be deductible when capitalization is not required. Improvements generally must be capitalized.

The IRS defines improvements through betterment, restoration or adaptation to a new use. A project that replaces a substantial building part may qualify as restoration. Calling that invoice a repair does not settle the tax issue.

IRS Publication 551 says improvements increase basis, your tax investment in the property. Depreciation reduces adjusted basis. Keep the original cost records, later improvement invoices and depreciation schedules together. A new sale estimate cannot replace those records.

Split a mixed invoice into distinct jobs. Ask the contractor to describe the defect, work done and items replaced. Record when the work finished and whether the property remained available for rent. Send these facts to your tax professional before filing.

Keep selling costs in a separate folder. IRS Publication 544 explains gain and loss on property sales, including the role of selling expenses. Do not place every pre-sale payment in that folder simply because you expect to sell.

Do not claim a cost both as a current expense and as an added basis amount. Ask your preparer to show where each major invoice appears. The record should explain the treatment without relying on your memory.

## How long should the rental sit empty?

Keep the empty period only as long as your work and sale plan require. There is no single waiting period that makes a renovation worth doing. Build the schedule from access, contractor availability and the sale plan.

IRS Publication 527 makes an important tax distinction. A vacant property held for rental use may still have deductible ordinary and necessary expenses. A property listed for sale but unavailable for rent does not have deductible rental expenses under that guidance.

Record the actual change in use. Save the last rental listing, any withdrawal notice and your written sale instruction. Do not keep a token rental advertisement merely to support a tax position. Your records should match your actions.

Ask your insurer how vacancy and planned construction affect the policy. Give the agent the true occupancy status and work scope. Request a written answer about needed coverage changes before work starts.

Build a weekly cash plan with utilities, insurance, loan payments and work invoices. Separate cash spending from tax deductions. For example, a loan payment can affect cash even when its full amount is not a current expense.

If the home has a tenant, ask a local attorney about access, notices and the lease before setting the work schedule. Do not assume a sale plan ends a lease or grants unrestricted entry.

## Should you sell the rental as-is?

Sell as-is when the likely gain from work does not justify its cost, delay and risk. Compare net proceeds, not listing prices. An as-is offer and a renovated sale can have very different costs to close.

Use this illustrative calculation, not a market forecast. Assume work could raise proceeds before project costs by $20,000. If work costs $14,000 and the added holding period costs $4,000, the expected gain is $2,000. A cost overrun could remove that gain.

The example uses assumed figures only. Replace them with written bids, a local sale estimate and your own holding costs. Include likely concessions and selling fees in both paths. Ask your tax professional to compare the after-tax outcomes separately.

Also compare certainty. A firm as-is offer may suit an owner who needs a known closing date. A work plan may suit an owner with cash reserves and time to manage contractors. Neither choice fits every rental.

Do not confuse as-is sale terms with permission to hide known defects. Disclosure duties depend on state law and the facts. Have a local attorney review the sale documents and required disclosures.
