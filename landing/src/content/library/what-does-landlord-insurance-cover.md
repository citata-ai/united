A pipe bursts in a rented house at 2 a.m. The tenant moves to a hotel. The owner now has three questions. Who pays for the floor? Who pays for the hotel? And who pays the rent while the floor dries?

The answers sit in one document: the landlord's policy. This guide explains what those policies cover, what they leave out, and what to add.

## How Does Landlord Insurance Differ From Homeowners Insurance?

Landlord insurance is written for a home you rent to someone else. A homeowners policy is written for a home you live in. The North Carolina Department of Insurance (NCDOI) says a dwelling fire policy is typically used when the owner does not make the property a primary residence. It lists rental properties, vacant homes and second homes as common uses.

The two policies differ in three main ways:

| Feature | Homeowners policy (HO-3) | Landlord dwelling policy (DP) |
|---|---|---|
| Who lives there | The owner | A tenant |
| Liability coverage | Included in the package | Typically not included (NCDOI) |
| Owner's belongings | Covered | Only items the owner keeps there |
| Extra living costs | Pays for the owner to move out | Pays lost rent instead, if included |

The NAIC 2023 report says homeowners policies are a package of building, contents and liability. It says dwelling fire policies give less coverage in each range.

If you rent out your old home and keep the homeowners policy, a claim may fail. Tell your insurer before the first tenant moves in.

## What Do the DP-1, DP-2 and DP-3 Forms Cover?

Dwelling policies come in three forms. NCDOI calls them basic, broad and special.

| Form | Perils covered | Claim payment | Rent coverage (NCDOI) |
|---|---|---|---|
| DP-1 Basic | Named perils: fire, lightning, internal explosion; more with extended coverage | Actual cash value; replacement cost may cost extra | Can include fair rental value |
| DP-2 Broad | Named perils: adds vandalism, windstorm, hail, burglary damage, freezing, falling objects, water discharge and more | Typically replacement cost | Loss of rent may be included |
| DP-3 Special | Open perils for the building, except listed exclusions; named perils for personal property | Check your policy | Check your policy |

Two terms matter in a claim:

- **Replacement cost.** The Texas Department of Insurance (TDI) says this pays to repair or replace at current prices.
- **Actual cash value.** TDI says this pays replacement cost minus depreciation.

TDI gives an example. A roof costs $10,000 to replace today. With a $2,000 deductible, a replacement cost policy pays $8,000. An actual cash value policy pays less, because it subtracts wear on a 10-year-old roof.

TDI also says most companies require you to insure the house for at least 80% of its replacement cost. Some require 100%.

## What Do Dwelling, Liability and Loss of Rent Cover?

Most landlord policies hold the same building blocks. Use this checklist when you read your declarations page:

- **Dwelling.** Pays to repair the house after a covered loss.
- **Other structures.** Pays for detached garages, sheds and fences.
- **Owner's personal property.** Covers items you own and keep there, such as a lawn mower or appliances you supply.
- **Fair rental value or loss of rent.** Replaces rent while a covered loss makes the home unfit to live in.
- **Liability.** Pays if someone is hurt and you are legally responsible. NCDOI says dwelling policies typically do not include it, so ask.
- **Debris removal and code upgrades.** NCDOI lists debris removal as a coverage. TDI notes that extra costs to meet building codes are often not covered unless you add them.

Back to the burst pipe. The DP policy may pay for the floor if water discharge is a covered peril. Loss of rent may pay the owner's rent gap. The tenant's hotel is not the owner's coverage. The tenant's renters policy may pay it.

## What Is Usually Excluded?

Exclusions vary, so read your own policy. NCDOI lists typical DP-3 exclusions:

| Typical exclusion (NCDOI) | Why it matters to a landlord |
|---|---|
| Neglect | A slow problem you did not fix may not be paid |
| Intentional loss | Damage someone meant to cause, in many cases |
| Mold, rust, rot | Often excluded unless a covered peril caused it |
| Constant or repeated leakage | A slow leak under a sink is a common denied claim |
| Earth movement, such as earthquakes | Needs a separate policy or endorsement |
| Wear and tear, deterioration | Old roofs and worn carpet are maintenance, not claims |
| Birds, rodents, insects, animals | Pest damage is usually not covered |

TDI adds flood to the list. It says most home policies don't cover flood damage, and floods can happen outside flood zones. TDI also says most policies won't pay for damages during short-term rentals. If you rent nightly, ask your agent.

## Does Landlord Insurance Cover Tenant Damage?

Sometimes. It depends on the cause and the form. NCDOI lists vandalism and malicious mischief as covered perils in the DP-2 broad form. A DP-1 may add them by endorsement. Intentional loss and neglect are typical exclusions, so a claim for tenant damage can be denied.

Normal wear by a tenant is not a claim. Scuffed walls and worn carpet are costs of renting. The security deposit and your lease handle those, under your state's deposit law.

A tenant's own belongings are never covered by your policy. Ask tenants to carry renters insurance, which the NAIC reports as the HO-4 tenant form. Many leases require it, where state law allows.

## How Do You Check Your Own Policy?

Use these steps:

1. **Find the form name** on the declarations page: DP-1, DP-2, DP-3 or a carrier's own form.
2. **Check replacement cost or actual cash value** for the dwelling.
3. **Confirm the dwelling limit** is at least the share of replacement cost the insurer requires.
4. **Look for fair rental value** and its limit.
5. **Look for liability.** If it is missing, ask for it or price an umbrella.
6. **List the exclusions** that fit your area, such as flood or earthquake.

IRS Publication 527 lists insurance as a common rental expense, so keep the premium receipt.
