## How many photos should a rental listing have?

A rental listing should have at least 10 photos. That is enough to show every main room, the outside and the features renters ask about. RentSpree, which sends listings to Zillow, Redfin, Apartments.com, Zumper and realtor.com, sets a range of 3 to 50 photos and tells landlords to "aim for 10 or more."

Each platform sets its own limits. Check the one you use before you shoot.

| Platform | Minimum | Maximum | Advice from the platform | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| RentSpree | 3 | 50 | Aim for 10 or more | RentSpree help |
| TurboTenant | Not stated | 20 at setup, more through Edit Listing | 5 to 7 clear, well-lit photos; first 3 go on the flyer | TurboTenant help |
| HousingLink | Not stated | 20 per property | Same camera, same orientation, no panoramas | HousingLink help |

Too few photos hurt you. Apartments.com says that too few photos "create uncertainty" for renters. A renter who cannot see the bathroom may assume it is bad and skip the unit.

A full shot list for a 2-bedroom unit comes to about 15 to 20 photos:

- [ ] Front of the building or house, from the street
- [ ] Entry
- [ ] Living room, 2 angles
- [ ] Kitchen, 2 angles, plus 1 of the appliances
- [ ] Dining area
- [ ] Each bedroom, 1 to 2 angles
- [ ] Each bathroom
- [ ] Closets that are large or walk-in
- [ ] Laundry, in-unit or shared
- [ ] Yard, patio or balcony
- [ ] Parking or garage
- [ ] Shared amenities, such as a gym or a lobby
- [ ] The view from the best window, if it is good

## Which rooms should come first?

Put the strongest wide shot first. For most units that is the living room or the kitchen. The first photo is the thumbnail in search results, so it decides if a renter clicks.

The first 3 photos matter most. TurboTenant prints the first 3 on its listing flyer. A renter who scrolls a list of units sees the first photo before anything else.

A good photo order:

1. **Best room, wide shot.** Living room or kitchen, with daylight.
2. **Kitchen.** Renters want to see counters and appliances.
3. **Main bedroom.**
4. **Main bathroom.**
5. **Outside.** Front of the building, then the yard or the balcony.
6. **Other rooms**, in the order a person walks through the unit.
7. **Features.** Laundry, parking, storage, amenities.

Do not lead with a photo of the outside unless the outside is the best feature. A street view of a large building tells a renter little about the unit.

## How do you take good rental photos with a phone?

You can take good rental photos with a modern phone. The steps matter more than the camera. Apartments.com advises you to declutter each room, open the blinds for natural light, and shoot when the unit is clean and move-in ready.

Use these steps:

1. **Clean first.** Wipe counters, clean mirrors and windows, remove trash bins.
2. **Clear the clutter.** Remove personal items, cords, magnets and toiletries.
3. **Open every blind and turn on every light.** Mixed light is better than dark corners.
4. **Use the main camera, not the ultra-wide, for most rooms.** Ultra-wide lenses bend walls at the edges.
5. **Hold the phone level, at chest height.** Turn on the grid so vertical lines stay straight.
6. **Shoot from a corner or a doorway.** That shows the most of the room.
7. **Keep one orientation.** HousingLink asks for the same orientation across all photos. Landscape works best for rooms.
8. **Skip panoramas.** HousingLink asks landlords to avoid them.
9. **Take 3 shots of each angle.** Pick the sharpest one later.
10. **Edit lightly.** Fix brightness and straighten lines. Do not hide damage.

Clean up the outside too. Apartments.com says overgrown grass, peeling paint and full trash bins make renters assume the inside is neglected. Mow, sweep and move the bins before you shoot.

## What time of day is best for photos?

The best time is when daylight fills the rooms you shoot. That depends on which way the windows face. Visit once to check the light, then plan the shoot for that time.

Tips for light:

- **Avoid direct sun in the frame.** It makes bright spots and dark shadows.
- **Shoot the outside when the sun is behind you**, not behind the building.
- **Do not shoot at night**, except for a lit pool or a skyline view.
- **Avoid old photos.** Apartments.com warns that photos with old date stamps can look out of date or suspicious.

## Should you hire a photographer?

Hire a photographer when the unit is high-rent, hard to light, or one of many units you list at once. For a small, bright unit, a careful phone shoot is often enough.

| Choose | When |
|---|---|
| Phone, by you | Small unit, good daylight, you list a few times a year |
| Photographer | High-rent unit, dark rooms, a large building, or many units at once |
| Photographer plus 3D tour | Out-of-town renters, self-showings, or a slow market |

Get 2 quotes and ask what the price includes: number of photos, editing, a floor plan and the license to reuse the photos.

## Do you need a video or 3D tour?

A video or 3D tour helps renters who cannot visit, such as people who move from another city. It is not required, but it can add leads.

One study shows the effect. PlanOmatic, a company that sells 3D tours, tested more than 70 single-family rentals in Dallas and Houston from May 10 to June 30, 2021. Half got a Zillow 3D tour and half got only professional photos. PlanOmatic reported 40% more leads, more than 35% more applications per property, and 30% fewer days on market for the 3D tour group. The study is from a vendor with a stake in the result, so treat it as a hint, not a rule.

Two more rules for every photo, video and tour:

- **Fair Housing.** HUD rule 24 CFR 100.75(c)(1) lists "photographs, illustrations, symbols or forms" that convey a unit is or is not available to a group because of a protected class as discriminatory. Show the property, not people.
- **Scams.** The FTC warns that scammers copy photos and virtual tours from real listings and post them with their own contact details. Search your address after you list. Report copies to the listing site.
