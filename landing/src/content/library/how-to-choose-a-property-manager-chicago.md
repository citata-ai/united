## Which license must a Chicago property manager hold, and how do I look it up?

A Chicago property manager who leases and runs rentals for other owners works under the Illinois Real Estate License Act of 2000 (225 ILCS 454). The Illinois Department of Financial and Professional Regulation (IDFPR) issues the licenses. Its Division of Real Estate lists these types and first-time fees (IDFPR brochure):

| License | What it is | IDFPR fee |
|---|---|---|
| Broker | The standard license for real estate work for others | $150 |
| Managing broker | Runs a brokerage and sponsors brokers and leasing agents | $175 |
| Residential leasing agent | Leases homes and apartments only, under a sponsor | $100 |
| Real estate entity or firm | The company license | $250 |

Illinois REALTORS notes that there is no "salesperson" or "agent" license in Illinois (Illinois REALTORS license guide, 2024). When we checked on 2026-10-19, the Illinois General Assembly site (ilga.gov) did not answer, so we could not read the Act's section on who needs a license. Ask each manager which license covers its work, and ask its attorney if the answer is not clear.

How to check a license in 4 steps:

1. **Open the IDFPR License Lookup** at online-dfpr.micropact.com/Lookup/LicenseLookup.aspx.
2. **Search the firm name** and the name of the managing broker. Both should show.
3. **Check the status** is active and the expiry date is in the future.
4. **Look for discipline.** If you see an action, ask the manager about it.

If something does not match, you can call IDFPR Professional Licensing at 1-800-560-6420 (IDFPR brochure).

## Which local rules must a Chicago manager know?

A Chicago manager must know the Residential Landlord and Tenant Ordinance (RLTO), Chapter 5-12 of the Municipal Code. It covers most rentals in the city, except owner-occupied buildings with 6 units or fewer (City of Chicago Department of Housing). Mistakes here cost the owner money, so test the manager on it.

| RLTO topic | What a good manager says | Section |
|---|---|---|
| Deposit account | "A separate, federally insured, interest-bearing account at an Illinois bank. The bank name goes in the lease." | 5-12-080(a) |
| Deposit interest | "We pay it within 30 days after each 12-month period, at the city rate for the lease year." | 5-12-080(c) |
| Deposit return | "Within 45 days of move-out. Itemized damage costs within 30 days." | 5-12-080(d) |
| Owner disclosure | "We give the tenant the owner's or manager's name, address and phone in writing at move-in." | 5-12-090 |
| Notice before a lease ends | "30, 60 or 120 days, by how long the tenant has lived there. Same for a rent increase." | 5-12-130(j) |
| Late fee | "$10 on the first $500 of rent plus 5% of the rest, no more." | 5-12-140(h) |

Why this matters to you: under 5-12-080(f), a tenant can win 2 times the deposit plus interest when the deposit rules are broken. On a $2,011 deposit (the FY2027 HUD fair market rent for a Chicago-area 2-bedroom), that is $4,022.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## What should a Chicago management agreement say?

A Chicago management agreement should put every fee, every limit and every exit step in writing. Use this checklist before you sign:

- [ ] **Fees:** monthly fee (percent or flat), leasing fee, renewal fee, and any fee on vacant months, repairs or evictions.
- [ ] **Term and exit:** the length, the notice to end it, and any fee to end it early.
- [ ] **Deposits:** who holds tenant deposits, the Illinois bank, and how deposits and interest move to you or the next manager.
- [ ] **Repair limit:** the dollar amount the manager can spend without your approval, and the rule for emergencies.
- [ ] **Vendor markups:** whether the manager adds a fee on top of contractor bills.
- [ ] **Reports:** monthly owner statement, the date you get paid, and year-end 1099 forms.
- [ ] **Leases and files:** who keeps leases, tenant files and keys when the contract ends.
- [ ] **Notices:** who sends the RLTO summary, the deposit interest summary and the 5-12-130(j) notices.
- [ ] **Insurance:** the manager's liability and errors and omissions coverage, and whether you must name the manager on your policy.
- [ ] **Licenses:** the firm and managing broker license numbers, written in the agreement.

## Which questions should I ask a Chicago manager before I sign?

Ask the same 10 questions to 3 managers. Write down the answers. Vague answers are a warning sign.

1. **How many doors do you manage in Chicago,** and how many are like mine (2-flat, condo, single-family)?
2. **Who is my contact,** and how fast do you answer an owner email?
3. **What was your average days-on-market** for a rental like mine last year?
4. **How do you screen tenants,** and how do you follow fair housing law in each step?
5. **Where do you hold deposits,** and how do you pay the yearly RLTO interest?
6. **How do you track the 30, 60 and 120-day notice dates?**
7. **Do you manage Section 8 units?** How many, and how do you handle a failed Chicago Housing Authority inspection?
8. **What is your repair process,** and do you add a fee to contractor bills?
9. **How many evictions did you file last year,** and what does one cost me?
10. **Can I talk to 2 owners** who have used you for more than 2 years?

On question 7: the Chicago Housing Authority (CHA) runs inspections, rent increase requests and payment tracking through its HCV Owner Portal (CHA, For property owners). A manager with voucher units should know the portal well.

## Where do Chicago owners find manager reviews?

Chicago owners find manager reviews on Google, Yelp, Expertise.com, Thumbtack and the BBB. More and more owners also ask ChatGPT or Gemini for a short list. Those AI answers pull from the same review sites.

We tested this. On 2026-10-16, we ran 24 prompts about Chicago-area property managers on ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini, 2 runs each. In the 132 answers without a brand name in the prompt, the most cited sources were manager websites, Yelp (35 answers), Expertise.com (22) and Thumbtack (17) (our data, 2026-10). The test was for Fridman Properties, a Chicago manager and our client. Related party: Fridman Properties' owner is CEO of MyRemotely, our partner.

How to read reviews well:

- **Read the 2-star and 3-star reviews.** They show how the company handles problems.
- **Look for owner reviews, not only tenant reviews.** Both matter, but they answer different questions.
- **Check the dates.** A great review from 2019 says little about the team today.
- **Compare 2 or 3 sites.** One site can be skewed by a single campaign.
