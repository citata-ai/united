## What is a typical approval limit?

A typical repair approval limit sits in the low hundreds of dollars. We found no public survey that sets a national norm, so here are real examples.

| Source | Limit | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Green Residential | $500 | For new contracts. Older agreements have lower limits. |
| Oregon agreement, 2026 Real Estate Agency case | $400 | Expenses over $400 needed owner approval. |
| Mynd | Set per owner | The owner gets an approval request above the limit, unless it is an emergency. |

Green Residential explains why it does not let owners approve every small job: it "can significantly slow down the repair process". Instead it asks owners for a list of preferred vendors for each trade at the start. That is a good trade: the owner picks who, you decide when.

How to pick your number:

- **Look at your last 12 months of work orders.** Find the cost below which 70% to 80% of jobs fall. A limit at that point lets most jobs move with no call.
- **Raise it for higher-value homes.** A $500 limit on a $4,000 rental is a small share of a month's rent.
- **Lower it for owners who want control,** but tell them the cost: slower repairs.

The "not-to-exceed" (NTE, a cost cap on a job) also works for vendors. Latchel advises a clear NTE on every work order, so the vendor must ask before work above the cap.

## When must I call the owner first?

Call the owner first for any repair above the limit that is not an emergency. Also call first for any choice that is the owner's to make, even under the limit.

Two state board cases show what happens when a manager skips the call:

- **Oregon, 2026.** An owner complained that after a tenant moved out, the company billed him $7,000 for work he did not approve. His agreement required owner approval for expenses over $400. The Oregon Real Estate Agency found violations and the commissioner reprimanded the property manager's license.
- **Virginia, 2022.** The agreement required prior owner approval for all repairs, except emergencies or when the owner was not reasonably available. The manager charged $350 for one repair and $1,900 for a water heater, plus a $700 debris charge with no separate approval. The board found he failed to follow the agreement under Virginia Code § 54.1-2135.A.1. Because of his prior violations, it revoked his license.

Call first for:

- [ ] Any repair above the limit that is not an emergency.
- [ ] Replace or repair choices, for example a new water heater vs a fix.
- [ ] Upgrades, for example a better floor than the old one.
- [ ] A second vendor on a job you already paid one vendor for.
- [ ] Any charge type the agreement does not list.

## How do emergencies work above the limit?

Emergencies let you act above the limit first and report fast, but only if the agreement says so. Define the word "emergency" in writing.

A clear emergency rule has 4 parts:

1. **Definition.** A risk to life or safety, active damage to the home (a burst pipe, a roof leak), or loss of heat, water or power.
2. **Try to reach the owner.** Call and text, and log the time.
3. **Act only on what stops the risk.** Stop the leak today. Replace the floor after approval.
4. **Report within 24 hours** with the cost, the photos and the next step.

In the Virginia case, the manager said the water heater was an emergency. The board still found a breach for the debris charge, which no emergency covered. Keep the emergency repair narrow.

## How does slow owner approval hurt tenants and retention?

A slow approval delays the tenant's repair, and the delay can break repair deadlines in state law. It also makes the owner look bad to the tenant and makes you look bad to the owner.

State law sets repair timelines for some conditions. For example, Texas Property Code § 92.056(d) creates a rebuttable presumption that 7 days is a reasonable time to repair a condition that materially affects the health or safety of an ordinary tenant, once the landlord has notice. Other states use other rules. If the tenant waits 5 days for the owner to say yes, you have little time left.

Property Meld says that its customers reach owner approval in 4 to 6 hours. It recommends you track approval speed and look for bottlenecks. Here is a simple way to measure it:

| Metric | How to measure | Target (example) |
|---|---|---|
| Approval time | Hours from request sent to owner answer | Under 24 hours |
| Share of jobs needing approval | Jobs over the limit ÷ all jobs | Under 30% |
| No-answer rate | Requests with no answer in 48 hours ÷ all requests | Under 5% |

Worked example: you run 1,000 work orders a year. With a $250 limit, suppose 40% need approval: 400 calls. At a $500 limit, suppose 20% need approval: 200 calls. If each approval takes 15 minutes of staff time, the higher limit saves 50 hours a year. The tenants in 200 jobs also wait less. Use your own work order data for the real shares.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## How to write the approval clause

Write the approval clause as a short, exact rule. Vague words like "major repairs" cause disputes.

Use this checklist:

- [ ] **Limit**: one dollar amount per repair, for example $500.
- [ ] **Related repairs**: one job is one job. Do not split it to stay under the limit.
- [ ] **Above the limit**: written owner approval, by email or the owner portal.
- [ ] **Emergency**: definition, contact attempt, scope, and a report within 24 hours.
- [ ] **No answer**: what you may do after a set time, and up to what amount.
- [ ] **Vendor choice**: owner's preferred vendors, if given, for each trade.
- [ ] **Markups and fees**: any coordination fee in writing, as a percent or amount.
- [ ] **Changes**: how the owner can raise or lower the limit, in writing.

Show the limit on the owner's first statement and in the portal. Owners forget what they signed. A reminder prevents the surprise that leads to a complaint.
