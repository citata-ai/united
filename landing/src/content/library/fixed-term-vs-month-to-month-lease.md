## Fixed-term vs month-to-month: what is the difference?

A fixed-term lease runs for a set time, often 12 months. Rent and terms stay the same until the end date, unless the lease says otherwise. A month-to-month agreement renews each month. Either side can end it with written notice.

Both are normal. The right choice depends on your plans for the next year, not on which one is "better".

| Factor | 12-month fixed term | Month-to-month |
|---|---|---|
| Rent change | At renewal | With notice, any month (local limits apply) |
| How it ends | At the end date, or by breach | Written notice, often 30 days |
| Turnover risk | Lower | Higher |
| Your flexibility to sell or move in | Lower | Higher |
| Tenant flexibility | Lower | Higher |
| Best fit | Long holds, stable areas | Short holds, planned sale or rehab |

## What are the pros of a month-to-month lease?

A month-to-month lease gives you room to change plans. These are the main reasons owners choose it:

1. **Planned sale.** A buyer who wants to move in can get the unit sooner.
2. **Planned renovation.** You can end the tenancy before the work starts, with notice.
3. **Owner move-in.** You or a family member can take the unit back sooner.
4. **A trial period.** A tenant who passed screening but has a thin rental history can start on a short term.
5. **A good tenant with an uncertain job.** Flexibility can keep a reliable payer who would not sign for 12 months.

Watch for: month-to-month also gives the tenant freedom to leave. A tenant can give notice in a slow rental month, and you carry the vacancy.

## How much notice ends each type of lease?

State law sets the notice for a month-to-month tenancy. A fixed-term lease usually ends on its end date, but some states still require notice before it ends. Here are 4 examples.

| State | Month-to-month notice | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Florida | At least 30 days before the end of a monthly period | Fla. Stat. 83.57 |
| Texas | At least one month, unless a signed lease sets a different period | Tex. Prop. Code 91.001 |
| California | Owner: 60 days if the tenant lived there 1 year or more; 30 days if less | Cal. Civ. Code 1946.1 |
| New York | 30, 60 or 90 days, based on how long the tenant has lived there | N.Y. Real Prop. Law 226-c |

New York's rule also covers a fixed-term lease. Under Real Property Law 226-c, a landlord who will not renew, or who will raise rent by 5% or more, gives notice. The notice is 30 days if the tenant lived there under 1 year. It is 60 days for 1 to 2 years, and 90 days for 2 years or more.

California adds a second limit. Civil Code 1946.2 says that after a tenant has lived in the unit for 12 months, a covered owner cannot end the tenancy without a just cause stated in the notice. For a no-fault cause, the owner pays relocation help equal to 1 month of rent, or waives the last month. Some housing is exempt, so check the section.

So in California, "month-to-month" does not mean "end any time for any reason". Other states and cities have similar just-cause rules.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## Should month-to-month rent be higher?

Many owners charge more for month-to-month, because the turnover risk is higher. A premium is legal in most places if you set it up front and apply it to all tenants the same way. Rent control and local rules can limit it.

Use a simple test. Compare the yearly premium to the cost of one extra vacant month. The numbers below are an example, not market data.

| Line | Example |
|---|---|
| Base rent, 12-month lease | $1,800/mo |
| Month-to-month premium | $75/mo |
| Premium over 12 months | $75 × 12 = $900 |
| Cost of 1 vacant month | $1,800 |
| Turnover cost (clean, paint touch-up, listing) | $600 |
| Total cost of one extra turnover | $2,400 |

In this example, one extra turnover costs about 2.7 times the yearly premium ($2,400 ÷ $900). The premium helps, but it does not pay for frequent turnover. Use your own rent and turnover costs. You can model management costs with our [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator).

What to do: set the premium so a tenant sees a clear reason to sign 12 months. The goal is a choice, not a penalty.

## What happens when a fixed-term lease ends?

A fixed-term lease has 3 common outcomes at the end date:

1. **Renewal.** You and the tenant sign a new term.
2. **Move-out.** One side gave the required notice, and the tenant leaves.
3. **Conversion to month-to-month.** The tenant stays and keeps paying, and the tenancy continues month to month.

The law often decides outcome 3 when nobody acts. In New York, Real Property Law 232-c says that if a landlord accepts rent for a period after the lease ends, the tenancy becomes month-to-month. Many lease forms also say the lease converts to month-to-month at the end.

Your lease should name the outcome. Add a clause that says what happens at the end date, what notice each side gives, and what rent applies.

## Checklist: pick the right lease type

Use this list before you send a lease or a renewal.

- [ ] Do I plan to sell, renovate or move in within 12 months? If yes, consider month-to-month.
- [ ] Do I want stable income and low turnover? If yes, offer 12 months.
- [ ] What notice does my state require to end a month-to-month tenancy?
- [ ] Does my state or city limit ending a tenancy without cause?
- [ ] Does local rent control limit a month-to-month premium?
- [ ] Does my lease say what happens at the end date?
- [ ] Do I offer the same options and prices to every applicant?

The last point protects you under fair housing rules. Offer the same lease terms to every qualified applicant for the same unit.
