## Can a tenant paint the walls?

A tenant painting request should receive a clear written decision before work starts. Review the surface, product and proposed method. A promise to paint neatly does not address lead, ventilation or damage to nearby finishes.

Ask which rooms and surfaces the tenant wants to paint. Distinguish walls from cabinets, trim, tile and doors. Request the product details and planned preparation. Record whether the tenant will sand, scrape, drill or remove old coatings. That step can change the safety review.

The EPA warns that renovation, repair or painting in pre-1978 homes with lead paint can create dangerous dust. Its guidance recommends lead-safe certified contractors and explains that rental work can fall under the RRP rule. Do not approve sanding based only on a tenant's willingness to do the work free.

If you approve the job, attach a scope and color record to the lease file. Specify protection for floors and fixtures. Explain the cleanup and inspection process. Make restoration terms clear before the tenant buys materials. Do not rely on a verbal promise that everyone remembers differently at move-out.

## What changes need written consent?

Use written consent for optional changes that affect finishes, fixtures, building systems or future maintenance. Keep a route for maintenance reports so tenants do not mistake a repair need for an upgrade request.

| Proposed change | Suggested review | What to record |
|---|---|---|
| Painting | Surface and lead-risk review | Product, color, preparation and restoration |
| Shelves or wall mounts | Substrate and hidden-service review | Location, fasteners and removal method |
| Faucet or appliance | Plumbing, power and compatibility review | Installer, model and service responsibility |
| Lock or access device | Safety and access review | Approved hardware and key arrangements |
| Accessibility change | Separate Fair Housing review | Request, necessary scope and lawful conditions |

This table is an operating proposal, not a national permit rule. Ask the local building authority which permits or licenses a job needs. Keep that answer with the scope. A tenant's trade experience does not replace required approvals.

Review reasonable modification requests separately. The Fair Housing Act, in 42 USC 3604(f)(3)(A), protects necessary reasonable modifications in covered housing under its terms. Do not reject a disability-related request merely because your standard form says no alterations. Confirm any lawful restoration conditions through the proper review.

## Who owns fixtures the tenant installs?

Fixture ownership needs a written answer tied to local law and the actual item. Resolve the issue before installation. Do not assume that paying for an item always determines who owns it after attachment.

Identify the item by make, model and location when useful. State whether it stays at the end of the lease. Record who maintains it during the tenancy. Explain what happens if it breaks, leaks or needs a part. Keep manufacturer instructions with the work record.

If removal is allowed, describe how the tenant must restore the affected area. A wall mount can leave holes. A changed faucet can leave missing owner parts. Record where any removed owner fixture will be stored and who must keep it safe.

For a landlord-funded upgrade, create a normal work order. Do not make the tenant's rent ledger carry an informal barter deal. If you agree to a lawful rent credit, record the amount, completed work and approval separately. Get advice before trading required repairs for tenant labor.

Schedule a closeout inspection after the work. Compare the completed job with the approved scope. Fix unfinished details while the installer and materials are still available.

## Can I charge to undo changes?

Restoration charges need an enforceable basis, documented condition and a lawful amount. Written consent helps explain expectations, but it does not override deposit law. Check the local rules before posting a charge.

California Courts explains that deposit deductions for damage cannot cover normal wear and tear. Its guide also limits cleaning charges to restoring the move-in level of cleanliness. Use that California example to understand the distinction. Check the rule and required evidence in your own state.

Keep photographs from before and after the change. Save the approval and any restoration terms. Match each proposed charge to a specific unfinished or damaged area. Do not treat a whole-room repaint as automatic when only a small area needs work.

Washington RCW 59.18.180 sets a notice and remedy process for specified tenant noncompliance affecting health and safety. That is not general permission to enter and charge for every unwanted alteration. Ask counsel which local process applies to the actual condition.

Give the tenant a clear explanation of any proposed charge. Include the relevant agreement, supported work and required records. Remove charges that you cannot explain or document.

## How should I handle tenant repair requests?

Keep urgent repair reports separate from optional DIY requests. A tenant reporting a leak needs a maintenance response. Do not send only an alteration form and wait for the tenant to arrange the job.

State repair-and-deduct rights differ, so do not use a blanket instruction that tenants can never arrange repairs. Have counsel review the local remedy rules. Give staff a short routing note for requests that mention unsafe conditions, prior notices or withheld rent.

Use a simple approval process for optional work. Ask for the scope, check hazards and permits, set ownership terms, approve in writing and inspect completion. Keep the decision with the lease. Tell vendors which parts of the home the tenant may change.

Review recurring requests when planning upgrades. If tenants keep asking for shelves or better fixtures, consider an owner-installed standard. Compare the work and ongoing service costs with your wider budget using the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator).

Start with the next request in your inbox. Write down the exact change, who will perform it and what must happen at move-out. Resolve those details before anyone opens a paint can or removes a fixture.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.
