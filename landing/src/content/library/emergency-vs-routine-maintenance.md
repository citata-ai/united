## What counts as an emergency?

An emergency is a problem that threatens health, safety or the building right now. If waiting until the next business day could hurt someone or cause major damage, treat it as an emergency.

Use three tiers. The response times are an example policy, not a legal standard. Set your own, write them in the lease or move-in packet, and follow them.

| Tier | Examples | Response (example policy) |
|---|---|---|
| Emergency | Fire or smoke; gas smell; active flooding or a burst pipe; sewage backup; no heat in freezing weather; total power loss; exterior door or window that will not lock; carbon monoxide alarm | Call back within 1 hour; someone on site the same day or night |
| Urgent | No hot water; only toilet not working; refrigerator not cooling; slow leak under a sink; one room without power; pests in the unit | Fix within 24–72 hours |
| Routine | Dripping faucet; sticky door; broken blind; cabinet hinge; running toilet; small cosmetic damage | Schedule within 7 days, or at the next visit |

Some urgent items have a legal edge. Texas Property Code 92.052 covers a landlord's failure to keep a device that supplies hot water of at least 120 degrees Fahrenheit. It also covers any condition that "materially affects the physical health or safety of an ordinary tenant."

## What is urgent but not an emergency?

An urgent problem makes the home hard to live in but does not threaten safety tonight. No hot water, a broken refrigerator, or the only toilet failing are the common cases.

Urgent problems often become legal problems if they wait. State law sets the outer limit:

| State | Repair timing rule | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Texas | Rebuttable presumption that 7 days is a reasonable time to repair a condition that affects health or safety, after notice | Property Code 92.056(d) |
| California | A tenant who repairs and deducts after 30 days from notice is presumed to have waited a reasonable time; shorter notice can apply | Civil Code 1942(b) |
| Florida | Tenant may withhold rent as a defense 7 days after written notice of a material failure to maintain the unit | Statutes 83.60(1)(b) |

In Texas, the remedies for a landlord who does not repair include a civil penalty of one month's rent plus $500 (Property Code 92.0563). These are deadlines for the worst case. Your policy should be much faster.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## How do I set after-hours rules?

After-hours rules tell tenants when to call at night and what happens next. Write them down and give them to every tenant at move-in.

A simple after-hours policy:

1. **One emergency number.** Answered 24 hours a day by you, a manager or an answering service.
2. **A short list of what counts.** Print the emergency tier from the table above.
3. **Call 911 first** for fire, gas, carbon monoxide, or any risk to life.
4. **Approved vendors.** A plumber, an electrician, a heating tech and a locksmith who answer at night.
5. **A spending limit.** For example, vendors may spend up to $500 without a call to the owner.
6. **A log.** Record the time of the call, the response and the fix.

Texas makes the phone number a legal rule. Property Code 92.020 requires a landlord with an on-site management office to give a number "answered 24 hours a day" for emergencies that affect health or safety. Landlords without an on-site office must also give tenants an emergency number.

## Can I enter the unit in an emergency?

Yes, in many states you can enter without the usual notice in a true emergency. Normal repairs still need notice.

- **California:** Civil Code 1954 lets a landlord enter "in case of emergency." For other entry, 24 hours' written notice is presumed reasonable, during normal business hours.
- **Florida:** Statutes 83.53(2) lets a landlord enter "at any time for the protection or preservation of the premises." For repairs, the law requires at least 24 hours' notice and entry between 7:30 a.m. and 8:00 p.m.
- **New York:** The Attorney General's guide says that in an emergency, "such as a fire or water leak," a landlord may enter without consent or notice.

After an emergency entry, leave a note that says when you entered and why.

## What should the tenant do first in a leak or no-heat case?

Give tenants a first-steps card at move-in. The first 10 minutes decide how much damage a leak does.

For a leak:

- [ ] Shut off the water at the fixture valve, or at the main shut-off.
- [ ] If water reaches outlets or lights, stay away and turn off the breaker only if safe.
- [ ] If the ceiling sags, leave the room.
- [ ] Move valuables and electronics away.
- [ ] Take photos and video.
- [ ] Call the emergency number.

For no heat:

- [ ] Check the thermostat setting and batteries.
- [ ] Check the breaker and the furnace switch.
- [ ] Replace a clogged filter if you know how.
- [ ] If you smell gas, leave and call the gas company or 911.
- [ ] Call the emergency number if freezing weather is expected.

Show tenants where the main water shut-off and the breaker panel are on move-in day.

## How do I log and follow up on requests?

Log every request with a time, a tier and a result. The log shows you responded and helps you spot repeat problems.

Record these fields: date and time received, tier, who responded, when, what was done, cost, and whether the tenant confirmed the fix. Ask tenants to send routine requests in writing. In Texas, written notice is required only if the lease is written and requires it (Property Code 92.052(d)).

If round-the-clock calls are more than you want to handle, a manager can run the emergency line. Our guide to [property management](/property-management) explains what is usually included.
