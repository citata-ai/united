## What does HUD guidance say about criminal records now?

HUD's 2016 guidance on criminal records is withdrawn. A Federal Register notice of 2026-07-17 lists the April 4, 2016 guidance on criminal records as withdrawn. The withdrawal took effect on September 25, 2025. The same notice says the documents "should not be relied upon as authoritative".

The 2016 guidance was not the law. The Fair Housing Act is the law, and it did not change. HUD's discriminatory effect rule, 24 CFR 100.500, is still in the eCFR as of this writing. It says a practice can be unlawful if it "actually or predictably results in a disparate impact" on a protected group, even without intent. A practice can still be lawful if it is necessary for a "substantial, legitimate, nondiscriminatory interest".

HUD proposed in January 2026 to remove that rule. It published a supplemental proposal on 2026-08-10. We found no final rule. Courts can also apply disparate impact under the Act itself.

What this means for you: the federal floor is less clear, but it is not gone. State and local law is now the main thing to check.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## Can I use arrest records?

Do not use an arrest without a conviction to deny an applicant. An arrest shows that police suspected a person, not that a court found the person guilty.

Two rules back this up:

- **Cook County, Illinois:** The county's FAQ says "An arrest without a conviction cannot be considered when evaluating rental applications."
- **Federal FCRA:** 15 U.S.C. 1681c(a)(2) bars a consumer reporting agency from reporting "records of arrest" older than 7 years, or until the statute of limitations ends, whichever is longer. Convictions are not under this 7-year limit.

Many screening reports still show old charges or dismissed cases. Read each line and ignore anything that is not a conviction.

## Which states and cities limit criminal history checks?

Many places limit criminal checks, and the rules differ a lot. The table compares three.

| Place | When you may check | What you may consider | Before a denial |
|---|---|---|---|
| New Jersey (Fair Chance in Housing Act) | Only after a conditional offer, with a disclosure statement first | Murder, kidnapping, arson, human trafficking and other listed crimes; lifetime sex offender registration; 1st degree offense in past 6 years; 2nd or 3rd degree in past 4 years; 4th degree in past 1 year | Individualized assessment; withdraw only for a substantial, legitimate, non-discriminatory interest |
| Cook County, Illinois (Just Housing Amendment) | Only after a prequalification step on income, rental history, credit and other criteria | Convictions in the past 3 years; current registered sex offender; current child sex offender under residency restriction | Individualized assessment; show a clear risk to personal safety or property |
| California | State fair housing law applies | Civil Rights Department lists "refusing to rent to anyone with a criminal history" as discrimination | Check state rules and city ordinances |
| Texas | State law does not set a fair chance process | Texas Property Code 92.3515 lets criminal history be a listed ground in your printed criteria | Fair Housing Act still applies |

Cities can add more rules on top of state law. Check your city and county before you write a policy.

## What is an individualized assessment?

An individualized assessment is a review of one applicant's record before you deny. You look at facts, not a category.

New Jersey's guidance lists factors such as the nature and severity of the offense and the applicant's age at the time. Use a short form so each review is the same:

1. **Confirm the record.** Is it a conviction? Is it the right person? Check the full name and date of birth.
2. **Check the date.** Is it inside your look-back period and the local limit?
3. **Link it to a risk.** Does the offense relate to the safety of other residents or the property?
4. **Ask for context.** Give the applicant a chance to show errors or rehabilitation: work history, a program, a reference.
5. **Decide and record why.** Write the reason in one or two sentences. Keep the form with the file.
6. **Send notices.** If a screening report played any part, send the FCRA adverse action notice.

The form takes 10 minutes. It also gives you a record if someone files a complaint.

## How do I write a criminal history policy?

Write a policy that names the records you consider and the time limits. A clear policy is easier to apply the same way to everyone.

A sample structure:

| Policy part | Example text (adapt to your local law) |
|---|---|
| Records not considered | Arrests without conviction; sealed, expunged or juvenile records; dismissed charges |
| Records considered | Convictions for offenses that relate to the safety of residents or property |
| Look-back period | A set number of years from conviction or release, no longer than local law allows |
| Timing | Criminal check after the applicant meets income, credit and rental history rules |
| Review | Individualized assessment before any denial |
| Notice | FCRA adverse action notice when a consumer report was used |

Put the policy in your written screening criteria and give it to every applicant.

## Do I need an adverse action notice after a criminal check?

Yes, if the record came from a tenant screening report. 15 U.S.C. 1681m(a) applies to any adverse action "based in whole or in part on any information contained in a consumer report". A screening report is a consumer report.

The notice must name the screening company and say the company did not make the decision. It must also tell the applicant of the right to a free report within 60 days and to dispute errors.

If a manager screens for you, ask how they apply local fair chance rules. Our guide to [property management](/property-management) lists questions to ask.
