## When did the FARE Act take effect?

The NYC FARE Act took effect on June 11, 2025. Its full name is the Fairness in Apartment Rental Expenses Act. It is Local Law 119 of 2024, and the NYC Department of Consumer and Worker Protection (DCWP) enforces it.

The law adds a new Subchapter 15, "Rental Real Estate Agreements", to Title 20 of the NYC Administrative Code. The key sections are 20-699.21 (fees and agents) and 20-699.22 (disclosure).

Two dates matter for a landlord:

- **June 11, 2025:** the fee ban and the disclosure rules start (DCWP FAQ).
- **July 10, 2025:** the DCWP penalty schedule, 6 RCNY 6-89, takes effect (NYC Rules).

DCWP also says the ban covers a lease signed before June 11, 2025, if the broker fee was not yet paid. A contract made before that date may be an exception "depending on the particular circumstances" (DCWP FAQ). Ask a lawyer about an old deal.

A federal court denied a request to block the law on June 10, 2025. DCWP said the lawsuit "remains pending" on that date. Check the case status before you rely on any outcome.

## Can a NYC landlord still charge a tenant a broker fee?

A NYC landlord cannot pass the fee of the landlord's own broker to the tenant. The DCWP FAQ lists what the FARE Act bans and what it still allows.

| Situation | Allowed under the FARE Act? | Code section |
|---|---|---|
| Your broker charges the tenant a fee | No | 20-699.21(a), (b) |
| A listing agent who posts with your permission charges the tenant | No | 20-699.21(a), (b) |
| You require the tenant to use a specific agent or a dual agent | No | 20-699.21(c) |
| Anyone requires the tenant to hire a broker to see or rent the unit | No | 20-699.21(c) |
| A listing shows a broker fee for the tenant | No | 20-699.21(d) |
| The tenant chooses to hire their own broker and pays that broker | Yes | DCWP FAQ, question 5 |
| You charge for a background check and credit check | Yes, with a cap | RPL 238-a |

Watch for: the law presumes that an agent who posts a listing has your permission. So you are liable for that agent's fee unless you can prove the opposite (Admin. Code 20-699.21(e), per DCWP). Keep a written list of the agents you authorize. Report copies of your listing that you did not approve.

## What fees must a NYC listing disclose?

Every NYC rental listing must show all fees the tenant must pay, "in a clear and conspicuous manner" (Admin. Code 20-699.22(a), DCWP FAQ). You must also give the tenant an itemized written list of those fees.

Use this checklist for each new lease:

- [ ] The listing shows every tenant fee and no broker fee.
- [ ] Each fee on the written list has a short description.
- [ ] The tenant signs the fee list before the tenant signs the lease.
- [ ] You give the tenant a copy of the signed list.
- [ ] You keep the signed list for 3 years.
- [ ] The background and credit check fee is no more than the actual cost or $20, whichever is less.

The $20 cap comes from New York Real Property Law 238-a. The same section bans a fee to "process, review or accept" an application. It also limits a late fee to $50 or 5% of the monthly rent, whichever is less. And a late fee can apply only when rent is more than 5 days late.

## What fines does a landlord face?

DCWP issues a summons, and the case goes to the NYC Office of Administrative Trials and Hearings (OATH). If OATH sustains the charge, you pay a civil penalty. You may also have to repay the tenant the illegal fee (DCWP FAQ).

Here are the fines in 6 RCNY 6-89, the schedule DCWP adopted in 2025:

| Violation | 1st | 2nd | 3rd and later |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agent or landlord collects an unlawful fee | $750 | $1,800 | $2,000 |
| Rental depends on hiring an agent | $750 | $1,800 | $2,000 |
| Unlawful fee shown in a listing | $750 | $1,800 | $2,000 |
| Listing does not show all tenant fees | $375 | $900 | $1,000 |
| No itemized fee list given to the tenant | $375 | $900 | $1,000 |

Default amounts are higher for a first and second violation, for example $1,000 instead of $750. A "second" violation means the same respondent broke the same rule within 2 years. Each subdivision charged counts as a separate violation.

The tenant has a second path. The FARE Act creates a private right to sue in civil court (Admin. Code 20-699.24, DCWP FAQ).

## How do NYC landlords change their leasing budget?

NYC landlords now pay their own broker, so the leasing fee is an owner cost. You have three main choices: pay a listing broker, lease the unit with your own staff, or use a manager whose fee includes leasing.

Here is a worked example. The numbers are a sample, not a market rate. Use the quote you get.

| Line | Example |
|---|---|
| Monthly rent, 2-bedroom | $2,971 (HUD FY2027 fair market rent, New York metro) |
| Broker fee you agree to | 1 month of rent: $2,971 |
| Lease term | 12 months |
| Broker cost per month of the lease | $2,971 ÷ 12 = $247.58 |
| Cost if the unit turns over each year | $2,971 a year |
| Cost if the tenant stays 3 years | $2,971 ÷ 36 = $82.53 a month |

The table shows one clear fact: longer tenancies cut the broker cost per month. So renewals are worth more to you now. Each renewal you keep saves one full leasing fee.

What to do:

1. **Get the broker fee in writing.** Put the amount and who pays it in your listing agreement.
2. **Control your listings.** Name the agents who may post your unit. The presumption of permission makes this your risk.
3. **Update your fee list.** Remove any broker fee and keep the $20 check cap.
4. **Plan for renewals.** Start renewal talks early, since a turnover now costs you the broker fee.
5. **Compare total cost.** Some managers include leasing in the monthly fee. Check the math with our [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator).

If you rent to a voucher holder, see the [New York fair market rent 2027 page](/guides/section-8-rent-limits/new-york-fair-market-rent-2027) for the HUD limits by bedroom count.

## Which questions should you ask your broker now?

Your broker contract decides how the FARE Act hits your budget. Ask these 5 questions before you sign:

1. **Who pays you, and how much?** Get one number or one percent, in writing.
2. **Where will you post the listing?** Each post is in your name under the law.
3. **Who checks the fee disclosure?** One person must own the listing text and the signed form.
4. **Do you also work for tenants?** A dual agent cannot be a condition of the rental.
5. **What happens if DCWP sends a summons?** Agree who pays a fine that the broker causes.

Questions from tenants or brokers can go to the DCWP Business Compliance Counsel at BCC@dcwp.nyc.gov (DCWP, 2025-06-11).
