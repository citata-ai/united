## What goes into basis at purchase?

Basis at purchase is the price you paid plus the settlement costs of buying the property. IRS Publication 551 says your basis includes the settlement fees and closing costs for buying property. A cost for buying property is one you would pay even if you bought with cash.

What goes in and what stays out:

| Closing cost | In basis? | IRS source |
|--------------|-----------|------------|
| Purchase price | Yes | Publication 551 |
| Abstract of title fees | Yes | Publication 551 |
| Charges to install utility services | Yes | Publication 551 |
| Legal fees, including title search and preparing the contract and deed | Yes | Publication 551 |
| Recording fees | Yes | Publication 551 |
| Surveys | Yes | Publication 551 |
| Transfer taxes | Yes | Publication 551 |
| Owner's title insurance | Yes | Publication 551 |
| Seller's costs you agree to pay, such as back taxes or commissions | Yes | Publication 551 |
| Seller's real estate taxes you pay without reimbursement | Yes | Publication 551 |
| Amounts put in escrow for future taxes and insurance | No | Publication 551 |
| Costs of getting the loan | No, not in property basis | Publications 551 and 527 |
| Points | No, generally deducted over the loan term | Publication 527 |

Worked example, with example numbers:

| Line | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Price | $320,000 |
| Title and legal fees | $2,700 |
| Recording fees | $250 |
| Transfer taxes | $1,600 |
| Survey | $450 |
| Total cost basis | $325,000 |

Keep the settlement statement. It is the proof for each line, and you need it until at least 3 years after you sell, per IRS record rules.

## How do I split land and building value?

You split the basis because land is not depreciable. Only the building, and later improvements, can be depreciated.

IRS Publication 551 gives the method. Multiply the total cost by a fraction: the fair market value of each part over the fair market value of the whole. If you are not sure of the values, you can allocate by the assessed values for real estate tax.

Example using the county assessment:

| Item | Assessor's value | Share | Your basis |
|------|------------------|-------|------------|
| Land | $60,000 | 25% | $81,250 |
| Building | $180,000 | 75% | $243,750 |
| Total | $240,000 | 100% | $325,000 |

The assessor's dollar values do not need to match your price. You use only the ratio.

Yearly depreciation: IRS Publication 527 says residential rental property is depreciated with the straight line method over 27.5 years under GDS. $243,750 ÷ 27.5 is about $8,864 a full year. The first year is less, because of the mid-month convention. Publication 527's own example: a $185,000 house with a $160,000 building, placed in service in February, gets a first-year deduction of $5,091 at 3.182%.

## How do improvements change basis?

Improvements raise your basis. Depreciation and some losses lower it. The result is your adjusted basis.

IRS Publication 527 says an expense is an improvement if it betters the property, restores it, or adapts it to a new or different use. Its Table 1-1 lists examples. They include a new roof, a bedroom or bathroom addition, central air, a furnace, a water heater, a fence and wiring upgrades. You depreciate each improvement as its own item, starting when you place it in service.

Common changes to basis, from IRS Publication 551, Table 1:

| Increases basis | Decreases basis |
|-----------------|-----------------|
| Improvements (roof, addition, HVAC system) | Depreciation allowed or allowable |
| Local assessments for improvements such as streets or sewers | Casualty losses you deducted |
| Legal fees to defend or perfect title | Casualty insurance reimbursements |
| Restoring property after a casualty | Residential energy credits you claimed |

Publication 527 also covers local benefits that raise the property's value, such as new streets, sidewalks or sewers. You add those charges to basis instead of deducting them.

Worked example after 5 years, with example numbers:

| Line | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Cost basis at purchase | $325,000 |
| Plus: new roof in year 3 | +$14,000 |
| Minus: depreciation claimed on building and roof | −$46,000 |
| Adjusted basis | $293,000 |

If you sell for $400,000 with $24,000 of selling costs, your gain is $400,000 − $24,000 − $293,000 = $83,000. Of that, the $46,000 that came from depreciation is unrecaptured section 1250 gain. IRS Topic 409 says it is taxed at a maximum rate of 25%.

## What is the basis of a home converted to a rental?

For a former home you now rent, the basis for depreciation is the lower of 2 numbers on the date you convert it. IRS Publication 527 sets the rule:

1. **Fair market value (FMV)** on the date you change it to rental use, or
2. **Adjusted basis** on that date: your original cost, plus permanent improvements, minus casualty losses and other decreases.

Publication 527's example: an owner built a house for $140,000 on a $14,000 lot, added $28,000 of improvements, claimed a $3,500 casualty loss and a $500 energy credit. The house's adjusted basis was $164,000. On the conversion date, the FMV was $168,000: $21,000 for the land and $147,000 for the house. The basis for depreciation is $147,000, because it is less than $164,000.

Why the rule matters: if your home fell in value before you rented it, you cannot depreciate the lost value. If it rose, you depreciate only your actual cost.

How to document FMV on the conversion date:

- [ ] Get an appraisal dated near the first day the home is available for rent.
- [ ] Or save 3 sales of similar homes from the same month.
- [ ] Save the county assessment for the land and building split.
- [ ] List every improvement you made while you lived there, with receipts.

Publication 527 says to treat a converted home as placed in service on the date you convert it.

## A cost basis worksheet

```text
COST BASIS WORKSHEET: [address]
A. Purchase price                            $______
B. Settlement costs that go into basis       $______
C. Cost basis (A + B)                        $______
D. Land share ____%  (assessor ratio or appraisal)
E. Building basis (C x (1 - D))              $______
F. Improvements to date (list each)          $______
G. Depreciation allowed to date              $______
H. Adjusted basis (C + F - G)                $______
Converted home only:
I. FMV of building on conversion date        $______
J. Depreciable basis (lower of E+F or I)     $______
```

Update lines F and G each year. A manager's statements can show improvement invoices, which helps. For more owner guides, see [/guides](/guides).
