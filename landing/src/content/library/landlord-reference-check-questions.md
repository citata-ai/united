## How do I know a landlord reference is real?

Check that the reference owns or manages the rental before you ask any question. A fake reference, often a friend or a relative of the applicant, is a known screening trick. Ten minutes of checking stops it.

Verification steps:

1. **Get the full rental address** from the application, with unit number and dates.
2. **Look up the owner** in public property records for that county. In New York City, the Department of Finance runs ACRIS, which lets you "search property records and view document images" by address in four boroughs. Most counties have an assessor or recorder search.
3. **Match the name.** The owner may be an LLC. Look up the LLC on the state business registry, or call the management company named on the building.
4. **Find the phone number yourself.** Use the management company website or a business listing. Do not rely only on the number the applicant gave.
5. **Ask an opening check question.** "Can you confirm the address and the unit you rent to this person?" A real landlord answers at once.

Red flags:

- The "landlord" is a mobile number with no company name.
- The owner name in public records is different, and the reference cannot explain why.
- The reference cannot state the rent amount or the lease dates.
- The reference praises the tenant before you ask anything.

## Which questions get honest answers?

Fact questions get more honest answers than opinion questions. A past landlord may hesitate to say "bad tenant." The same landlord will often confirm "paid late 4 times." Ask the same 12 questions for every applicant, in the same order.

| # | Question | What it checks |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What address and unit did this person rent from you? | Reference is real |
| 2 | What were the move-in and move-out dates? | Matches the application |
| 3 | What was the monthly rent? | Matches the application; rent-to-income history |
| 4 | Whose name was on the lease? | Who was the tenant |
| 5 | How many times was rent late in the last 12 months? | Payment history |
| 6 | Do they owe you any money now? | Unpaid balance |
| 7 | Did they give proper notice before moving out? | Respect for lease terms |
| 8 | Did you keep any of the deposit? Why? | Damage beyond normal wear |
| 9 | Did you send any written lease violation notices? | Lease compliance |
| 10 | Did you file or start an eviction? | Eviction history |
| 11 | Did they report repairs on time? | Care of the unit |
| 12 | Would you rent to them again? Why or why not? | Overall view |

Two tips:

- **Stay quiet after question 12.** Let the reference fill the silence. That is where useful detail comes.
- **Do not ask about protected traits.** The Fair Housing Act, 42 U.S.C. 3604, bars decisions based on race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status or national origin. Do not ask "Do they have kids?" or "Were they disabled?" Ask about rent, notice and the unit only.

## What can a past landlord legally say?

No single federal law limits what a past landlord may say in a reference. The main risk for the past landlord is a false statement. So many landlords give only dates and rent to stay safe.

What this means for you:

- **A "dates and rent only" answer is not a red flag by itself.** It is often company policy.
- **Use yes-or-no fact questions** to get more. "Was rent ever more than 30 days late?" is easy to answer truthfully.
- **Write down what was said, not what you think.** "Owner said 4 late payments in 2025" is a fact. "Owner sounded unhappy" is not.

## Do references fall under the FCRA?

A screening report from a consumer reporting agency falls under the Fair Credit Reporting Act. The FTC page "Using Consumer Reports: What Landlords Need to Know" says landlords need a permissible purpose, and that "Landlords may get consumer reports on applicants and tenants who apply to rent housing or renew a lease."

If you deny or change terms based on such a report, the FTC says you must give an adverse action notice. The notice includes the name, address and phone number of the agency, a statement that the agency did not make the decision, and the applicant's right to a free report "within 60 days."

A reference you collect yourself is a different case. A separate FCRA section, 15 U.S.C. 1681m(b), covers adverse action based on third-party information that is not from an agency. It speaks of credit, and how it applies to a lease is a question for a lawyer. The safe habit: tell each denied applicant the main reason in writing.

The FTC also says that when you are done with a consumer report, "you must securely dispose of the report and any information you gathered from it."

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## Should I call the current or the prior landlord?

Call both, and give more weight to the prior landlord. The current landlord may want the tenant to leave and may give a too-positive reference. The prior landlord has no such reason.

| Reference | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Current landlord | Knows the most recent behavior | May give a too-positive reference to get the tenant out |
| Prior landlord | No reason to shade the answer | Information is older |
| No landlord history | Common for first-time renters | Use other criteria, such as income and a co-signer, by your written policy |

## How do I log reference checks?

Log every reference check in the same form, kept with the application. A standard form shows that each applicant got the same process.

Reference log fields:

- Applicant name and application date
- Reference name, company, phone and how you verified it
- Date and time of the call
- Answers to questions 1 to 12
- Your decision note, based on your written criteria
