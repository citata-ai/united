## What is a property management chart of accounts?

A property management chart of accounts is the numbered list of every account in your books. Each rent payment, vendor bill, deposit and owner draw posts to one account. The chart decides what your reports can show.

A chart has 5 groups. Most charts give each group its own number range:

| Range | Group | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1000s | Assets | Operating bank account, trust bank account, tenant receivables |
| 2000s | Liabilities | Security deposits held, prepaid rent, vendor bills owed |
| 3000s | Equity | Owner contributions, owner draws |
| 4000s | Income | Rent, late fees, pet rent, other income |
| 5000s–6000s | Expenses | Repairs, management fees, insurance, taxes, utilities |

A property manager often runs 2 sets of books. One is for the management company itself. The other is the trust books for owners' money: rent, deposits and owner payouts. The chart below is for the owners' side.

## Which income and expense accounts does a rental need?

A rental needs income accounts for each kind of money it earns, and expense accounts that match the tax lines the owner files. Start with the IRS Schedule E form. Schedule E (Form 1040), the 2025 form, lists line 3 "Rents received" and expense lines 5 to 19.

The IRS Schedule E instructions say line 19 is for "any ordinary and necessary expenses not listed on lines 5 through 18".

Two rules from IRS Publication 527 (2025) shape the income side:

1. **Advance rent is income when received.** Pub 527 says: "Include advance rent in your rental income in the year you receive it".
2. **Tenant-paid expenses are rent.** Pub 527 says: "If your tenant pays any of your expenses, those payments are rental income."

This is general information, not tax advice. Check the current law or ask a tax professional.

## How do you match the chart of accounts to Schedule E?

You match the chart to Schedule E by giving each income and expense account exactly one Schedule E line. Then a year-end report sums the accounts by line. The owner's tax preparer copies the totals with no re-sorting.

Use these steps:

1. **List the lines.** Copy Schedule E lines 3 to 19 into a sheet.
2. **Map each account.** Next to each account, write its line number.
3. **Find the orphans.** Any account with no line needs a decision. Most go to line 19.
4. **Check repairs vs improvements.** The Schedule E instructions say repairs are deductible but improvements are not. An improvement goes to a fixed asset account, not line 14.
5. **Set the tag in software.** If your software lets you set a tax line on each account, set it there.
6. **Test with last year.** Run last year's data through the map. Compare it with the owner's filed return.

A worked example of a year-end report for one rental:

| Schedule E line | Accounts | Total |
|---|---|---|
| 3 Rents received | 4000, 4010, 4020 | $22,140 |
| 9 Insurance | 5400 | $1,380 |
| 11 Management fees | 5600 | $1,771 |
| 14 Repairs | 5800–5830 | $2,240 |
| 16 Taxes | 6000 | $3,900 |
| 17 Utilities | 6100–6120 | $960 |
| 19 Other | 6300, 6320 | $420 |
| Expenses before interest and depreciation | | $10,671 |

The math: $1,380 + $1,771 + $2,240 + $3,900 + $960 + $420 = $10,671. Rent less these expenses is $22,140 − $10,671 = $11,469. The tax preparer adds interest and depreciation.

## Where do security deposits and improvements go?

Security deposits go in a liability account, and improvements go in an asset account. Neither belongs in income or repairs while they keep that status.

**Security deposits.** IRS Publication 527 says: "Don't include a security deposit in your income when you receive it" if you plan to return it. So post deposits to account 2100, Security deposits held. If you keep part of a deposit at move-out for damage or unpaid rent, move that part to income (4030 in the sample).

**Improvements.** Pub 527 says an expense is for an improvement if it results in a betterment, restoration or adaptation of the property. Improvements are capitalized and depreciated. Pub 527 gives residential rental property a 27.5-year recovery period under the general depreciation system. So a new roof goes to a fixed asset account, not account 5800.

Sample balance sheet accounts for the trust books:

| Account | Name | Group |
|---|---|---|
| 1000 | Trust bank account | Asset |
| 1100 | Tenant receivables | Asset |
| 1500 | Building improvements | Asset |
| 2000 | Vendor bills owed | Liability |
| 2100 | Security deposits held | Liability |
| 2200 | Prepaid rent | Liability (books); income for tax when received |
| 3000 | Owner contributions | Equity |
| 3100 | Owner draws | Equity |

Account 2200 shows a common gap. Your books may hold prepaid rent as a liability until it is earned. For tax, Pub 527 counts advance rent as income in the year received. Ask the owner's tax adviser how to report it.

## Should every property use the same chart of accounts?

Yes. Every property should use the same chart of accounts. One chart lets you compare repair costs per unit across buildings, spot an outlier, and run one report for an owner with 10 properties.

Do not create new accounts for each building, like "Repairs, Oak Street". Use the property as a tag or class in your software instead. The account says what the money was for. The tag says where.

Keep the chart short. A rental with 80 accounts produces reports nobody reads. Add an account only when an owner or the tax return needs that split.
