New Jersey has no statewide rent cap. The Department of Community Affairs (DCA) says so in its Rent Increase Bulletin and its Truth in Renting guide. The limits come from 3 places: the Anti-Eviction Act, case law on unconscionable increases, and local ordinances. I read the DCA statute texts and guides on 2026-10-19.

## Does New Jersey have a cap on rent increases or rent control?

New Jersey has no state cap on rent increases. The DCA Truth in Renting guide says the State "has no laws that establish, govern or control rents". Municipalities may pass rent control or rent leveling ordinances, and local boards enforce them.

A statewide limit still applies. Under N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.1(f), you can evict for nonpayment of a rent increase only if the increase "is not unconscionable". It must also comply with any municipal ordinance.

The statute does not define "unconscionable". The DCA bulletin lists the factors from Fromet Properties v. Buel:

1. **The size** of the increase.
2. **Your expenses and profit.**
3. **Comparable rents** in the same area.
4. **The bargaining position** of each side.
5. **Whether the increase would shock the conscience** of a reasonable person.

The DCA bulletin says the landlord carries the burden to prove the increase is fair. A tenant who thinks an increase is unconscionable may withhold only the difference and argue it in court.

## How many days notice does a New Jersey landlord need to raise the rent?

A New Jersey landlord needs at least 30 days of written notice to raise the rent, according to the DCA. The statute does not set a number of days for this cause. N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.1(f) asks for "a valid notice to quit and notice of increase".

The DCA Rent Increase Bulletin gives the practice:

- **Lease tenancy.** Give notice within the time frame in the lease, at least 30 days.
- **Month-to-month tenancy.** Give a 30-day notice to quit, served on the first day the rent is due.
- **Rent-controlled unit.** Follow the time frame in the local ordinance.

The notice to quit ends the old tenancy. It does not make the tenant move. If the tenant stays after a valid notice, the DCA bulletin says a new tenancy starts at the higher rent.

A worked example for a month-to-month unit with rent due on the 1st:

1. **Serve on November 1:** notice to quit and notice of increase, from $1,600 to $1,700.
2. **New rent starts December 1.**
3. **If the tenant pays only $1,600,** you may file for nonpayment of the increase. The court decides if $100 is unconscionable.

The DCA bulletin also says you may raise the security deposit with the rent. The deposit still may not pass 1.5 months' rent.

## Can a New Jersey landlord raise the rent during a fixed-term lease?

No, a New Jersey landlord cannot raise the rent during a fixed-term lease. The DCA Rent Increase Bulletin says you may raise rent "only at the beginning of the term of the lease". You offer the tenant a new lease at the new rent after the old lease ends.

Some tenants do not sign but stay after a valid notice. The DCA says a new tenancy at the higher rent then starts on its own.

## How much notice is needed to end a month-to-month tenancy in New Jersey?

In most New Jersey rentals, notice alone cannot end a month-to-month tenancy. N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.3 says no landlord may evict or fail to renew a lease except for good cause. The notice period then depends on the cause, under N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.2.

The Anti-Eviction Act does not cover owner-occupied premises with 2 or fewer rental units (N.J.S.A. 2A:18-61.1). For those units, N.J.S.A. 2A:18-56(b) ends a month-to-month tenancy with 1 month's notice to quit. A tenancy from year to year needs 3 months.

Watch for: the reprisal law. N.J.S.A. 2A:42-10.12 creates a rebuttable presumption of reprisal in some cases. It applies to a notice to quit or a big change in terms after the tenant enforces a legal right. It also applies after a good-faith complaint to a code office. A court enters judgment for the tenant if it finds reprisal (N.J.S.A. 2A:42-10.11). This law also exempts owner-occupied premises with 2 or fewer rental units.

## Do cities in New Jersey add their own rent or notice rules?

Yes, many New Jersey municipalities add their own rent rules. The DCA bulletin says an ordinance may limit the amount and the frequency of increases. An ordinance may not cover every unit, and it may set its own notice period.

To check a unit, the DCA tells you to call the municipal clerk or the local rent board. I did not list city caps here, because I could not read each ordinance on an official site.

Two State exemptions limit local rules:

- **New construction.** N.J.S.A. 2A:42-84.2 exempts new multiple dwellings of 4 or more units from local limits on rent increases. The exemption lasts for the initial mortgage term or 30 years, whichever is less. With no mortgage, it lasts 30 years. You must tell each prospective tenant in writing, and the lease must say so (N.J.S.A. 2A:42-84.3).
- **Subsidized housing.** The DCA guide says HUD-owned or HUD-subsidized buildings and NJHMFA-financed projects are not subject to local rent control.

The DCA texts I used date from 2008 and later. I did not confirm any 2024–2026 change to these rules, so check the current law and your town's ordinance.
