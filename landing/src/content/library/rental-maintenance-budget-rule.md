## What is the 1% rule for maintenance?

The 1% rule says you should budget 1% of the property value each year for maintenance and repairs. On a $300,000 home, that is $3,000 a year, or $250 a month.

The rule is popular because it is easy. It is also a rule of thumb, not a measured average. It ignores the two things that drive maintenance cost most: the age of the home and the age of its major parts.

Use the 1% rule as a first guess for a home you do not know yet. Replace it with a real budget after your first year of records.

## What is the 50% rule?

The 50% rule says that operating costs take about half of gross rent. Operating costs here mean taxes, insurance, repairs, vacancy, management and capex. The mortgage payment is not included.

For example, a home rents for $2,000 a month. The 50% rule guesses $1,000 a month of costs before the mortgage.

The 50% rule is a screening tool for a purchase. It helps you reject a bad deal in 2 minutes. It is not a maintenance budget, because it mixes many costs into one number. Taxes and insurance are known numbers. Look them up instead of guessing.

## How do age and type of home change the budget?

Age changes the budget most, because big parts wear out on a schedule. A new home has few replacements due for 10 years. A 25-year-old home may need a roof, a furnace and a water heater in the same 3 years.

InterNACHI publishes life spans for home parts. Some examples from its chart:

| Component | Typical life (InterNACHI) |
|---|---|
| Water heater | 10 years |
| Dishwasher | 9 years |
| Refrigerator | 9 to 13 years |
| Furnace | 15 to 25 years |
| Heat pump | 10 to 15 years |
| Asphalt shingles, 3-tab | 20 years |
| Asphalt shingles, architectural | 30 years |
| Carpet | 8 to 10 years |
| Vinyl flooring | 25 years |

InterNACHI says these are estimates, not guarantees. Climate, use and maintenance change actual life.

Type of home matters too. A single-family home puts the roof, yard and exterior on you. A condo moves some of those costs to the HOA, but you pay HOA dues and special assessments instead. A multifamily building shares one roof across many units, so the cost per unit is often lower.

## What is a capex reserve?

A capex reserve is a savings account for big replacements. Capex means capital expense: a cost that replaces or improves a major part of the home. You add a fixed amount each month so the money is there when the furnace fails.

Build the reserve from your own home's parts. The method is simple:

1. **List each major component.** Roof, HVAC, water heater, appliances, flooring, paint.
2. **Write its age** from records, labels or an inspection report.
3. **Write its typical life** from a source such as InterNACHI.
4. **Get a replacement cost** from a local quote.
5. **Divide cost by remaining years.** That is the yearly reserve for that item.
6. **Add the items.** Divide by 12 for the monthly amount.

## Worked example: monthly capex reserve

The home below is a single-family rental. The costs are example quotes, not national averages. The paint life of 5 years is an example too: InterNACHI says paint life depends most on surface preparation and paint quality. Use your own local quotes.

| Item | Age | Life | Years left | Example cost | Per year |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Roof (3-tab) | 12 | 20 | 8 | $9,600 | $1,200 |
| Furnace | 15 | 20 | 5 | $5,000 | $1,000 |
| Water heater | 6 | 10 | 4 | $1,600 | $400 |
| Fridge, range, dishwasher | 5 | 10 | 5 | $3,000 | $600 |
| Carpet, 3 bedrooms | 4 | 8 | 4 | $2,400 | $600 |
| Interior paint | 2 | 5 | 3 | $2,700 | $900 |
| **Total** | | | | | **$4,700** |

The capex reserve is $4,700 a year, or about $392 a month. Add routine repairs on top: clogs, faucets, small electrical fixes, pest calls. Track those for 12 months to get your own number.

Compare the result with the 1% rule. If the home is worth $300,000, the 1% rule gives $3,000 a year for everything. The component method gives $4,700 for capex alone. In this example, the 1% rule is too low because three big items are near the end of their life.

## How do taxes treat repairs and capex?

Repairs and capex are not taxed the same way. IRS Publication 527 says you must capitalize an expense that improves the property. An improvement is a betterment, a restoration, or an adaptation to a new use. Publication 527 also lists appliances and carpeting used in residential rental real estate as 5-year property for depreciation.

A routine repair, such as fixing a leak, is usually deductible in the year you pay it. A new roof is usually an improvement that you depreciate. The line between them depends on facts.

This is general information, not tax advice. Check the current rules or ask a tax professional.

## How do I keep the budget accurate?

Keep the budget accurate with records. Each repair and replacement goes into one log with the date, the item, the cost and the vendor.

- **Review the log once a year.** Compare actual spend with the budget.
- **Update ages** after each replacement.
- **Raise the reserve** when an item passes its typical life.
- **Keep the reserve separate** from your operating account, so you do not spend it on other bills.

After 2 or 3 years, your log is better than any rule of thumb. It shows what this home costs you.
