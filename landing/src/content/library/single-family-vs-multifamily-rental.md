## How do price per unit and cash flow compare?

Compare the full property budget before using price per unit. A duplex may cost less per rentable home while requiring expensive shared work. A house may cost more per unit but have fewer separate kitchens or leases.

Here, small multifamily means a duplex, triplex, or fourplex. Verify the permitted unit count before comparing rents. A listing description does not establish that every advertised space can lawfully be rented.

| Question | Single-family house | Small multifamily building |
|---|---|---|
| Rent sources | Often a single household lease | Separate leases may spread receipts |
| Vacancy | Whole-home vacancy can remove rent | Other occupied units may still pay |
| Systems | Check the home's actual equipment | Identify shared and unit systems |
| Utilities | Check who pays each bill | Verify meters and lawful allocation |
| Work | Fewer separate resident contacts | More leases and shared-space decisions |
| Financing | Depends on occupancy and program | Unit count and occupancy affect review |

Use actual tax, insurance, utility, and repair information. Ask for records that support each claimed expense. A low price per unit means little when the seller's cost sheet omits building repairs.

Keep your cash investment separate from purchase price. Down payment, closing costs, repairs, and reserve cash affect what you must contribute. Compare properties using the same cost categories.

## How do vacancy risk and turnover differ?

A house under a single lease concentrates rent in that lease. Separate apartments can retain some rent while another unit is empty. That arithmetic does not guarantee a safer building or lower overall vacancy.

For a hypothetical illustration, assume a house rents for $2,400 per month. A full empty month loses $2,400 of scheduled rent. Now assume a duplex has separate units renting for $1,300 each.

If a single duplex unit is vacant for that month, scheduled rent from the occupied unit remains $1,300. The missing $1,300 still matters. These invented figures show concentration, not typical local rents or a forecast.

Shared events can affect the entire building. A major water or electrical problem can disrupt multiple units. Budget the building as well as each apartment so shared risk stays visible.

Turnover also has a workload. Separate leases can end at different times, creating repeated inspections, repairs, listings, and showings. Compare that work with the longer gap that a whole-home vacancy might create.

Ask a local manager to explain the leasing market for each actual unit. Do not assume every household wants the same layout or lease term. Use property features and consistent criteria when advertising.

## How do financing rules differ for small multifamily?

Loan terms depend on the property, occupancy, program, and borrower. Get a written quote for the exact legal unit count. A quote for an owner-occupied house does not establish terms for a non-owner-occupied fourplex.

Fannie Mae's general rental income guide describes how lenders assess rental income. It says income must be likely to continue and that management experience can restrict its use. The guide includes a worksheet for owner-occupied properties with 2 to 4 units.

Ask how the lender will document rent and which amounts can count toward qualification. Do not assume all advertised rent becomes qualifying income. The underwriting calculation serves a different purpose from your cash budget.

Confirm required cash, reserve documentation, appraisal, and insurance with the lender. Keep these requirements outside a simple price-per-unit comparison. They can change which purchase you can support.

If you intend to live in a unit, describe that plan accurately. Ask what happens if your occupancy later changes. Do not treat the other units' rent as proof that your own housing cost disappears.

## How does management work differ?

Management depends on the building layout and services, not just the unit count. A house can require yard care and exterior upkeep. Small multifamily can add shared access, common spaces, parking disputes, and utility questions.

Inspect which systems serve which units. Record who controls thermostats, meters, shutoffs, and storage areas. Shared equipment requires a clear access and repair process before a problem occurs.

Ask for a management proposal based on the property. Confirm whether the quoted charge includes shared-space checks, separate lease work, and project oversight. Minimum charges and extra tasks may change the result.

Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) with the written proposal. Then add the costs that belong to the building rather than the manager.

Keep a ledger that can show unit performance without losing shared expenses. IRS Publication 527 discusses rental costs and mixed-use allocations. If you occupy part of the property, ask a tax professional how to split the relevant expenses.

## What should a first-time buyer inspect before choosing?

Inspect permission, systems, cash needs, and the operating process before making the choice. A manageable building is one whose work you understand and can fund. A unit count alone cannot establish that.

- **Verify legal use.** Confirm the dwelling count and required local approvals.
- **Check leases.** Match actual rent and deposits to the seller's records.
- **Inspect systems.** Identify shared equipment and obtain repair estimates.
- **Price financing.** Use the lender's terms for this occupancy and unit count.
- **Price management.** Confirm local response and shared-space duties.
- **Stress the budget.** Test vacancy and major work without assuming higher future rent.

Compare a normal case and a difficult case for each property. Keep the same holding period and sale assumptions. Count your time and need for liquid cash alongside the financial totals.
