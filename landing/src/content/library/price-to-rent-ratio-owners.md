## What is the price-to-rent ratio formula?

The price-to-rent ratio compares a home's value with its annual gross rent. Divide a realistic price by monthly rent multiplied by the months in a year. Keep both inputs tied to the same property and condition.

This is a comparison method, not a forecast of investment returns. The ratio does not deduct expenses. It does not show how much cash you can spend after the mortgage payment.

Here is an assumed example, not market data:

| Input | Assumed value |
| --- | ---: |
| Current sale value | $300,000 |
| Monthly gross rent | $2,000 |
| Annual gross rent | $24,000 |
| Price-to-rent ratio | 12.5 |

The calculation is $300,000 divided by $24,000. You could say the price equals 12.5 years of gross rent. You cannot say the property pays for itself in that time. Taxes, insurance, vacancy, debt, and repairs still need cash.

Use current value when deciding whether to keep a home you already own. Use the proposed purchase price when comparing a new purchase. Label the value basis so you do not mix those decisions.

## What ratio favors renting out a house?

No single ratio proves that renting out your house beats selling it. A lower ratio signals a stronger gross rent relationship. It does not tell you whether your own property has enough cash left after costs.

Compare homes with similar condition, property type, and likely lease terms. A low ratio can reflect deferred work or costs that the rent does not reveal. A high ratio can leave little operating margin even when the house looks easy to rent.

Do not use broad buy-versus-rent rules for a tenant as an owner investment rule. A tenant compares housing costs and flexibility. An owner compares tied-up equity, operating risk, cash needs, and sale proceeds.

Write your decision criteria before you see the ratio. State the minimum cash you need, the work you can accept, and your plan for major repairs. This keeps an attractive ratio from replacing the full review.

If the property fails your cash test, find the cause. Better management may help with operations. It cannot remove a large tax bill or make an unaffordable purchase price disappear. Ask for evidence behind each proposed improvement.

## How do you find your home's ratio?

Use a supported sale estimate and rent estimate for the same home. Ask a local agent for relevant sales and a rental manager for comparable leases. Keep the dates, conditions, and included services beside each estimate.

Separate active listing prices from completed transactions. A home listed at a price has not necessarily sold for that price. A rental advertised at a rent has not necessarily signed a lease at that rent.

Adjust for parking, utility charges, furnishings, and property condition. Compare a long-term rental with long-term rent evidence. Do not use peak nightly revenue as the rent for a year-round lease.

Create a range instead of one precise answer. Use the lower supported rent with the higher plausible value for a cautious ratio. Then reverse the inputs for a more favorable case. If your choice changes inside that range, gather better evidence.

Keep your existing lease terms in the review when the home is occupied. A possible future asking rent does not describe current collections. Your first-year cash plan should follow the actual rent you expect to receive, with clear assumptions for any change.

## What does the ratio leave out?

The ratio leaves out almost every cost that determines owner cash flow. IRS Topic 414 lists common rental expenses such as maintenance, insurance, taxes, and interest. These costs need a separate budget.

Build that budget from actual bills where possible. Get a rental-use insurance quote. Review the property tax estimate, association charges, and upcoming replacements. Add management and leasing costs if you will hire help.

The [management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) can help compare fee structures. Enter the same rent and vacancy assumptions in each option. Keep repair reserves outside the management fee so you do not miss them.

Also compare net sale proceeds. Deduct the loan payoff, selling costs, and a tax estimate prepared for your facts. The equity available after a sale may differ from the number on a property website.

When debt matters, compare the full loan terms. The CFPB's loan comparison guide stresses consistent offers and total costs. A small monthly payment can reflect a longer term or upfront fees. The ratio cannot detect either feature.

## How should you use the result?

Use the ratio to choose where to spend research time. Then make a separate hold-versus-sell worksheet. Include the cash you keep each year and the cash you could receive from a sale today.

Write down the risks you would accept to keep the house. Consider a vacancy during a repair, a higher insurance premium, and a sale that takes longer than expected. Do not add appreciation just to make a weak rental work.

Check the result with someone who can question your inputs. Ask which assumption changes the decision most. Replace that assumption with stronger evidence before you choose.

Your next step is a complete operating budget with a documented rent range. Keep the ratio at the top of the page as a quick reference. Make the final choice from the budget, your cash needs, and the work the property requires.
