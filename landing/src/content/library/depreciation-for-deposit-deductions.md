## Why prorate a damage charge at all?

You prorate because the tenant owes you for the value lost, not for a new item. A carpet that was already 4 years old was worth less than new carpet. Charging the full new price would make the tenant pay for normal aging.

State law backs this up. California Civil Code 1950.5(e)(2)(B) limits charges to "a reasonable amount necessary to restore the premises back to the condition it was in at the inception of the tenancy, exclusive of ordinary wear and tear." Texas Property Code 92.104(b) says you "may not retain any portion of a security deposit to cover normal wear and tear."

Texas defines normal wear and tear in 92.001. It is "deterioration that results from the intended use of a dwelling." It does not include damage from "negligence, carelessness, accident, or abuse" by the tenant, the household or guests.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## What is useful life for carpet and paint?

Useful life is the number of years you expect an item to last in normal use. No deposit statute we read sets a number for carpet or paint. You set a reasonable schedule, write it down, and use it for every tenant.

One public reference is the IRS. IRS Publication 527, Table 2-1, gives these recovery periods for rental property:

| Item | IRS recovery period (GDS) | IRS recovery period (ADS) |
|---|---|---|
| Carpets | 5 years | 9 years |
| Appliances, such as stoves and refrigerators | 5 years | 9 years |
| Furniture used in rental property | 5 years | 9 years |
| Residential rental buildings and structural parts | 27.5 years | 30 years |

These are tax periods for your own return. They are not a rule for deposits. A cheap carpet can wear out faster. A good one can last longer. Paint is not listed. Many landlords set a repaint cycle in years and use that.

This is general tax information. Ask a tax professional about your return.

A simple written schedule might look like this. The years are an example; set your own and keep the receipts that show install dates.

| Item | Your schedule (example) | Proof to keep |
|---|---|---|
| Carpet, builder grade | 5 years | Invoice with install date |
| Carpet, mid grade | 8 years | Invoice with install date |
| Interior paint | 3 years | Paint invoice or work order |
| Vinyl plank flooring | 10 years | Invoice |
| Refrigerator | 10 years | Purchase receipt, model plate |

## How do I prorate a replacement charge?

Use one formula for every item:

1. **Find the replacement cost.** Use a real bid or invoice for the same grade.
2. **Divide by useful life.** This gives the value per year.
3. **Find the years left.** Useful life minus the item's age at move-out.
4. **Multiply.** Value per year times years left is the maximum charge.
5. **Limit to the damaged area.** Charge only for the room or part that was damaged.

Formula: maximum charge = replacement cost × (years left ÷ useful life).

If the result is zero or below, the item had no value left. A full replacement charge is then hard to defend.

## Worked example: 4-year-old carpet

The schedule you choose changes the charge a lot. This example uses one carpet and two schedules.

Facts: the tenant's pet ruined carpet in the living room. The carpet was installed 4 years ago. A bid to replace the living room carpet is $2,000.

| Step | 5-year schedule | 8-year schedule |
|---|---|---|
| Value per year | $2,000 ÷ 5 = $400 | $2,000 ÷ 8 = $250 |
| Years left | 5 − 4 = 1 | 8 − 4 = 4 |
| Maximum charge | $400 × 1 = $400 | $250 × 4 = $1,000 |
| Tenant pays | $400 | $1,000 |
| You pay | $1,600 | $1,000 |

Both results can be fair if your schedule fits the carpet grade and you use it for everyone. A $1,000 charge for builder-grade carpet that is past its prime is not. Write the math into the itemized statement so the tenant can see it.

The same method works for paint. Example: a room cost $450 to paint, your schedule is 3 years, and the paint is 2 years old. The maximum charge for a full repaint is $450 × (1 ÷ 3) = $150. Check first whether the marks are normal wear and tear under your state's law.

## Can I charge labor at my own rate?

You can charge for your own labor in many states, but you must show it is reasonable. California sets the clearest rule. Civil Code 1950.5(h)(2)(A) says that if you or your employee did the work, the itemized statement must "reasonably describe the work performed" and include "the time spent and the reasonable hourly rate charged."

Use this checklist for each labor line:

- [ ] What you did, in one line.
- [ ] Hours spent, to the half hour.
- [ ] Hourly rate, close to what a local handyman charges.
- [ ] Materials listed separately, with receipts.
- [ ] Before and after photos.

A rate far above local vendor prices invites a dispute. In Texas, a landlord who keeps a deposit in bad faith owes $100 plus three times the amount wrongly withheld plus attorney's fees (92.109). The landlord also has the burden of proving the deduction was reasonable.
