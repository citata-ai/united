## Why does a free rental analysis bring owner leads?

A free rental analysis brings owner leads because it answers the owner's first question: what rent can my home get? An owner who asks that question is close to a decision. They may rent the home, sell it, or hire a manager. The form also gives you an address, a home type and a phone number, which is more than a contact form gives.

All 3 direct competitors we studied build a rental analysis for their clients (our competitor study, 2026-10-19). It is a base part of owner marketing in this market. The difference is in how fast it gives value and how well the follow-up works.

## How many leads can a rental analysis tool generate?

No public source we found gives a lead count for rental analysis tools. Property Manager Websites, which sells one, says a client "added over 100 new doors" with it, but gives no lead count, sample or time frame (Property Manager Websites). So estimate your own number.

The best public baseline is Goodjuju's 2026 study. Across 35 companies, 141,908 visits in the fall window gave 1,262 owner leads, a 0.9% average rate (Goodjuju, 2026). That rate is for all site visits and all owner forms, not rental analysis forms only. Phone calls were not counted.

Worked estimate (sample numbers, not a benchmark):

| Step | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly site visits | from GA4 | 3,000 |
| Share that reach the rental analysis page | 3,000 × 10% | 300 |
| Form rate on that page | 300 × 5% | 15 requests |
| Requests that are real owners with a rental | 15 × 70% | 10–11 leads |
| Leads that sign | 10 × 20% | 2 new owners |

Replace each rate with your own data after 90 days. The 10%, 5%, 70% and 20% here are placeholders. The point is the chain: more visits to the page, a higher form rate, and fast follow-up each move the result.

## Should the analysis be instant or manual?

Use both: an instant range on the page, then a manual report. Each has a job.

| | Instant estimate | Manual report | Instant range + manual report |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owner gets value | At once | In 1–2 days | At once, then more |
| Accuracy | Rough | Good | Rough, then good |
| Lead quality | Mixed, some curious visitors | Higher, owner waited | Higher, owner gets a reason to talk |
| Your work per lead | None | 20–40 minutes | 20–40 minutes, only for real leads |
| Starts a sales talk | Rarely | Yes | Yes |
| Risk | Owner takes the number and leaves | Owner waits and asks another manager | Lowest |

The work time is our estimate, not measured data. The pick for most companies is the third column. Show a range such as "$1,850–$2,100 for a 3-bedroom in this ZIP" right away. Then say: "We will check this against our own leases and send a full report within 1 business day."

## How do I follow up after the report?

Follow up fast, with a person, and with a reason to talk. The owner often asks several managers at once.

1. **Same hour:** send an automatic email that confirms the request and names the person who will check it.
2. **Same day:** call. Ask 3 questions: is the home empty now, when do you want a tenant, have you rented it before?
3. **Within 1 business day:** send the report. Put the rent range, 3–5 comparable homes, and your fee for that rent.
4. **Day 3:** call or text to walk through the report.
5. **Day 7 and day 21:** send one short note each, with one useful fact: a local rule, a vacancy cost, a repair to do before listing.
6. **Day 45:** if no answer, ask if the owner still plans to rent. Then stop.

Track each request in your CRM: date, source, report sent, first call, result. Mark the form submit as a key event in GA4 so you can see which pages and channels bring requests.

## Which data sources set the rent number?

Set the rent number from 3 sources, in this order:

1. **Your own recent leases.** Use leases signed in the last 6–12 months for similar homes nearby. This is the best data, because it shows what tenants actually paid.
2. **Current listings.** Look at similar homes listed now on the major listing sites. Note the asking rent and the days on market. Asking rent is not signed rent.
3. **HUD Fair Market Rents.** HUD sets Fair Market Rents at the 40th percentile of rents paid by recent movers. They include utilities except phone (24 CFR 888.113). So for a home where the tenant pays utilities, the market rent can be lower or higher than the FMR. Use the FMR as a check, not as the answer.

Example: the FY2027 three-bedroom Fair Market Rent for the Chicago-Joliet-Naperville metro is $2,586, up from $2,294 in FY2026 (HUD FY2027 data). If your leases and listings point to $2,100 for a 3-bedroom in one ZIP, the FMR tells you that home is below the 40th percentile for the metro. Explain that in the report.

For homes where the owner wants to rent to a voucher holder, the local housing authority's payment standard sets what the voucher can pay. See the [Section 8 rent limits by metro](/guides/section-8-rent-limits) and the [Section 8 rent calculator](/tools/section-8-rent-calculator).

## What should the rental analysis form ask?

Ask only for what you need to price the home and call the owner. Every extra field lowers the form rate.

Checklist:

- [ ] Address or ZIP
- [ ] Home type and bedrooms and bathrooms
- [ ] Is the home empty now? (yes / no / date)
- [ ] Name, email, phone
- [ ] Optional: "Anything we should know?" text box
- [ ] One line on how you use the data and how fast you reply

Do not ask about the tenants an owner wants by race, religion, family status, disability or any other protected class. A rental analysis is about the home and the market.

1. **Check your current form** on a phone. Count the fields and time how long it takes.
2. **Add an instant range** by ZIP and bedroom count, built from your own leases.
3. **Write the follow-up steps** above into your CRM.
4. **Measure for 90 days:** page visits, requests, real owner leads, signed owners.
