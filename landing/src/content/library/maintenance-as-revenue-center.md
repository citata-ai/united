## Which maintenance revenue models exist?

A property management company can earn maintenance revenue in five common ways. Each one pays you for real work. Each one also needs the owner's written consent.

| Model | How you earn | Fits best when | Main trust risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Markup on vendor invoice | A percent added to the vendor's bill | You use outside vendors for most jobs | Owner sees a higher price than the vendor quote |
| Coordination fee | A flat or percent fee per work order | You want a clear, separate charge | Owner feels charged twice for "management" |
| In-house labor | Your tech bills at a set hourly rate | You have steady job volume | Owner doubts the rate is fair |
| Add-on services | Seasonal and recurring services sold to owners | Single-family and small multifamily | Upsell feels pushy |
| Preventive plans | A monthly or yearly plan for checks and filters | Owners who want fewer surprises | Plan sold but work not done |

Property Meld, a maintenance software vendor, names snow removal and salting, gutter cleaning, lawn care and preventive maintenance as services managers can offer. It says these can "drive profit gains while keeping your property owners' assets in better condition." Its post also describes a customer, RGroup, that sells an "Age-in-Place" plan: homeowners pay a monthly fee for upkeep, vendor coordination and preventive work.

## How much margin is fair?

A fair margin covers the real cost of the work you do on the job and leaves a profit you can explain in one sentence. No public primary source sets a standard markup percent. So test your number against your own costs.

Here is a worked example. All inputs are example inputs, not benchmarks.

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| Work orders per month | 120 |
| Staff time per work order (intake, dispatch, follow-up, bill) | 45 minutes |
| Loaded staff cost per hour | $40 |
| Average vendor invoice | $350 |

1. Staff hours: 120 × 0.75 = 90 hours a month.
2. Staff cost: 90 × $40 = $3,600 a month.
3. Cost per work order: $3,600 ÷ 120 = $30.
4. As a percent of the average invoice: $30 ÷ $350 = 8.6%.

In this example, a markup under about 9% loses money on staff time alone. A markup well above it needs a clear reason: after-hours dispatch, a warranty on the work, or faster response. If you cannot name the reason, the owner will ask.

A flat coordination fee is simpler to defend. In the example, a $30 to $40 fee per work order covers the cost and is the same on a $90 job and a $2,000 job. A percent markup grows with the bill, which owners notice on large repairs.

## How do I disclose maintenance revenue to owners?

Disclose every maintenance fee in the management agreement and again on each owner statement. The owner must never learn about it from a vendor.

The NARPM Code of Ethics, Standard 3-4, says: "The Property Manager shall accept no commissions, rebates, profits, discounts, or any other benefit which has not been fully disclosed to and approved by the Client." Standard 3-6 adds that the manager "shall disclose to the Property Manager's Client all pertinent facts relating to any transaction." These rules bind NARPM members. Many state license laws set similar duties for brokers.

Use this checklist:

- [ ] The agreement names each fee: markup percent, coordination fee, in-house hourly rate, trip charge.
- [ ] The agreement says if you own or share profit with any vendor, including a maintenance LLC you own.
- [ ] Vendor rebates and volume discounts are disclosed, passed to the owner, or both.
- [ ] Each statement shows the vendor cost and your fee on separate lines.
- [ ] The owner can ask for the original vendor invoice and get it within a set number of days.
- [ ] Rate changes go to owners in writing before they take effect.

Software helps here. Rentvine describes a "Price Book" for in-house jobs: labor and material rates are set once, technicians do not see owner-facing rates, and coordinators apply owner pricing or markups before the invoice. It also supports a separate maintenance LLC as the payee. A setup like that makes the markup a visible, deliberate step, not a hidden one.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## When does maintenance revenue hurt owner trust?

Maintenance revenue hurts trust when the owner thinks you choose repairs for your profit, not for the property. Watch for these signals:

1. **The owner finds a lower quote.** If a vendor quote is far below your bill, explain your fee line by line.
2. **Repairs grow faster than rents.** If maintenance spend per door climbs each quarter, show the owner why with photos and work orders.
3. **Only your in-house team gets jobs.** Offer an outside bid on large jobs. It shows your rate holds up.
4. **The fee is on every small task.** A markup on a $15 part looks petty. Set a minimum invoice size for the fee.
5. **Preventive plans with no proof.** Send a dated photo report after each visit.

## A simple way to start

Start with one model, not five. For most firms with outside vendors, a flat coordination fee is the easiest to explain. Add seasonal services next, because owners see a clear service for the money.

Put the full fee list on your public pricing page too. Owners compare managers before they call. You can also show the total cost of your fees with the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator), so an owner sees maintenance fees next to the monthly fee.
