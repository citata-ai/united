## What should a written screening policy include?

A written screening policy includes every rule you use to decide on an application, in the order you check them. If a rule is not in the policy, do not use it.

The core sections are:

| Section | What it says |
|---|---|
| Application steps | Who must apply, fees, documents, how you treat incomplete files |
| Identity | Accepted ID types |
| Income | The ratio, what income counts, how you verify it |
| Credit | What you look at and what leads to denial or conditions |
| Rental history | References, unpaid balances to past landlords |
| Eviction history | Time window, how you treat dismissed or settled cases |
| Criminal history | Narrow categories, time windows, local law limits |
| Occupancy | Your occupancy standard, consistent with local code |
| Decision and notice | Approve, approve with conditions, deny; adverse action steps |
| Accommodations | How an applicant asks for a reasonable accommodation |

Order matters. Write down the order you use, for example the first complete application is reviewed first.

## What income ratio should you require?

No federal rule sets a required income ratio, so you choose one and apply it to everyone. For example, you can require gross monthly income of 2.5 or 3 times the monthly rent. Write down what you count and how.

Worked example with a 3x rule: rent is $1,600, so the applicant needs $4,800 a month in gross income. One applicant earns $3,900 from a job and gets $1,100 a month in benefits. Total income is $5,000, so the applicant meets the rule.

Decide these points in advance:

- **What counts:** wages, self-employment, benefits, child support, retirement income, savings.
- **Housing vouchers:** whether you apply the ratio to the full rent or only the tenant's share. Check state and city law, which may set the rule for you.
- **Proof:** pay stubs, offer letters, bank statements or benefit letters, and how many months.
- **Co-signers:** whether you accept them and what they must meet.

Watch for: a ratio that is too high can screen out applicants for reasons tied to a protected class. Pick a ratio you can explain with your own payment data.

## How do you treat credit, eviction and criminal history fairly?

You treat these histories fairly by using narrow, written rules tied to the tenancy, with time limits, and by using them the same way for everyone. The federal Fair Housing Act bars refusing to rent because of race, color, religion, sex, national origin, familial status or disability (42 U.S.C. 3604). It also bans ads and notices that show a preference based on these classes.

**Credit.** Look at rent-related debt and payment patterns, not only a score. For example, deny only for unpaid balances to past landlords or utilities over a set amount in a set number of years. Say whether medical debt counts.

**Eviction history.** Set a time window, such as 3 or 5 years. Say how you treat cases that were dismissed, sealed or settled. A filing is not a judgment.

**Criminal history.** This area changed in 2025. In a letter dated November 25, 2025, the HUD Secretary rescinded 3 guidance documents, including the 2016 Office of General Counsel guidance on the use of criminal records under the Fair Housing Act. The letter focuses on HUD-assisted housing. It lists mandatory denials under HUD rules, for example a person subject to a lifetime sex offender registration, and says owners have broad discretion to screen for criminal activity that would affect health and safety.

The Fair Housing Act itself did not change. State and city laws may still limit what criminal history you can consider and when. So keep criminal criteria narrow: name the offense types, set a lookback period, and allow the applicant to give context.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## What must you do after a denial?

After a denial, or any worse terms, you must give an adverse action notice when a consumer report played a part. The FTC says adverse action includes denying the application, requiring a co-signer, or charging a larger deposit or higher rent than to another applicant. The notice is required "even if the information in the report played only a small part."

The FTC lists what the notice must include:

1. **The name, address and phone number** of the consumer reporting company.
2. **A statement** that the company did not make the decision and cannot explain it.
3. **The applicant's right** to a free copy of the report if asked for within 60 days, and the right to dispute wrong or incomplete data.

The FTC also says to dispose of reports securely when you are done, for example by shredding paper.

## Why publish the criteria before applications?

You publish the criteria before applications so that every applicant sees the same rules before they pay a fee. It is the simplest proof that you treat people the same.

- **For applicants:** they can check whether they qualify before paying.
- **For your team:** no one makes up a rule on the spot.
- **For disputes:** a dated policy shows what rules were in force on the day.

Post the policy on each listing, link it in the application, and give a copy on request. Keep each old version with its dates.

## Tenant screening criteria template

Copy this text, fill in the brackets, and check it against your state and city law before use.

> **Rental Screening Criteria**
>
> Company: [name]. Effective date: [date]. This policy applies to every applicant for every unit we manage.
>
> **Fair housing.** We do not discriminate based on race, color, religion, sex, national origin, familial status, disability, or any other class protected by [state and city law]. To ask for a reasonable accommodation, contact [name, phone, email].
>
> **1. Application.** Every adult [18+] who will live in the unit must apply. Fee: $[amount] per adult [state limit, if any]. We process complete applications in the order we receive them. An application is complete when we have [list documents].
>
> **2. Identity.** A valid government-issued photo ID, or [other accepted documents].
>
> **3. Income.** Combined gross monthly income of [X] times the monthly rent [or the tenant's share of rent with a housing voucher]. We count: [wages, benefits, support, other]. Proof: [documents for (X) months].
>
> **4. Credit.** We review credit for rent-related debt. We may deny or require [an added deposit / a co-signer] for: [unpaid balances to a landlord or utility over $(amount) in the past (X) years]. [We do not count medical debt.]
>
> **5. Rental history.** We contact up to [X] past landlords for the past [X] years. We may deny for [unpaid rent or damage owed to a past landlord over $(amount)].
>
> **6. Evictions.** We may deny for an eviction judgment in the past [X] years. We do not count cases that were dismissed or sealed.
>
> **7. Criminal history.** [Write narrow categories allowed by local law, with lookback periods. Describe how the applicant can give context.]
>
> **8. Occupancy.** Up to [X] people per [bedroom], under [local code].
>
> **9. Decision.** We decide within [X] business days of a complete application. If a consumer report affects our decision, we will send an adverse action notice with the reporting company's contact details and your rights.
>
> **10. False information.** We may deny an application that has false or misleading information.

## How do you keep the policy current?

You keep the policy current with a set review date and a log of changes. Review it each year and when a law changes, as the 2025 HUD letter shows. Note the date on each version.

Screening rules also come up when owners pick a manager. See our [guide to Section 8 rent limits](/guides/section-8-rent-limits) if you manage voucher units and need to set the income rule on the tenant's share.
