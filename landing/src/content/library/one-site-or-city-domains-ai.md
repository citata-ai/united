## One website or many city domains: the short comparison

One website with real city pages wins for most property managers. Here is how the two setups compare on the points that matter for search and AI answers.

| Point | One domain, city pages | Many city domains |
|---|---|---|
| Links and mentions | Add up on one site | Split across sites |
| Brand entity | One name, one set of facts | Many names or URLs for one company |
| Google spam risk | Low, if pages are real | Google names it as doorway abuse when sites funnel to one page |
| Google Business Profile | Each office links to its city page | Each domain needs a real location |
| Work per update | One fee page, one about page | The same edit on each site |
| Reviews | One review page | Reviews split or copied |
| Local relevance | City pages with local facts | Domain name only, unless each site has real content |

The last row is the one people overrate. A city name in the domain does not make the content local. Local facts, rents, rules and reviews do.

## What happens to authority on many domains?

Authority splits on many domains. Every link, mention and review points to one site or another. None of them adds up.

The Ahrefs study of 75,000 brands shows why that matters (Ahrefs, 2025-05-26). Branded web mentions had the strongest correlation with AI Overview visibility, at 0.664. Referring domains scored 0.295 and Domain Rating 0.326. All three grow when people point to one brand and one site.

Worked example. Say a manager earns 40 referring domains in total:

| Setup | Referring domains per site |
|---|---|
| 1 domain | 40 |
| 4 city domains, even split | 10 each |
| 4 city domains, typical split | 25, 8, 5, 2 |

Each small site now competes alone. The fourth site, with 2 links, may never reach the pages an AI answer reads. Ahrefs warns that correlation is not cause. But no study shows that splitting helps.

## How does AI treat duplicate sites?

No AI engine publishes a rule for duplicate sites. Google's AI guide, last updated 2026-07-10, does speak about duplicates. It says duplicate content "can be a bad user experience" and that engines "might waste crawling resources" on it.

City sites built from one template tend to share most of their text. We saw the same problem with city pages on one site. On the old Fridman Properties site, two city pages (Palatine and Skokie) were 96% identical (our data, 2026-10). Fridman Properties' owner is CEO of MyRemotely, our partner.

An AI answer quotes facts. If 4 sites say the same thing, the engine has 1 fact, not 4. It may also pick a different version each time, or merge your sites with other companies. Google's spam policies add a second risk: "multiple domain names or pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page" is doorway abuse.

## When does a second domain make sense?

A second domain makes sense when it serves a separate business, not a separate keyword. Use this checklist. If you tick 3 or more, a second site can be right.

- **Different brand name** on the sign, the lease and the invoices.
- **Different legal entity** or a different broker license.
- **Different office** with its own staff and phone.
- **Different service,** such as HOA management or commercial leasing.
- **Different audience** that would be confused by the main site.
- **Recent purchase** of a company with its own name, reviews and links. Keep it until you merge it.

Google Business Profile follows the same logic. Google allows one profile per location. A service-area business gets one profile for its central office, with a service area. Each profile should link to a website that represents that location. A city domain with no office behind it has no profile to support it.

## How do you merge sites safely?

You merge sites safely with a URL map, permanent redirects and a slow watch on the results. Google's site move guide, last updated 2026-08-20, gives the method. Here are the steps for a property manager.

1. **List every URL** on each old site, from the sitemap and Search Console.
2. **Map each old URL** to the closest new page. Each city domain maps to its city page.
3. **Write the city page first.** Move the useful local facts from the old site into it.
4. **Set 301 or 308 redirects** from each old URL straight to its final page. Avoid chains.
5. **Keep the redirects** "generally at least 1 year", as Google advises. Keep the old domains renewed longer.
6. **Use the Change of Address tool** in Search Console for each old domain.
7. **Submit the new sitemap** in Search Console.
8. **Update every profile:** Google Business Profile, Yelp, Expertise.com, BBB and your ILS accounts.
9. **Watch both sites** in Search Console. Old traffic should fall as new traffic rises.

Expect a dip. Google says a medium site can take "a few weeks or more" before the new URLs show, and rankings may move during the change.

Watch for: a redirect to the home page loses the match between the old page and the new one. Map city to city, fee page to fee page.

## What should the city pages on one site contain?

City pages on one site should hold facts that only fit that city. Without them, the merged site has the same duplicate problem inside one domain. Good city pages include:

- **Local rents** by home type, with the source and date.
- **Local rules:** rental license, registration or inspection rules, with a link to the city.
- **Your proof there:** doors you manage, reviews from local owners, office address if one exists.
- **Fees** that apply in that area, if they differ.
- **Neighborhoods** you serve, named, not a list of every ZIP code.
