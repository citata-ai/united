A Texas property manager can have a great website and still break the rules with two missing links in the footer. A California manager can run a Facebook ad without an 8-digit license number and get a complaint. License law treats a property management website as an ad. We read the rule text for three large states and the federal Fair Housing ad rule on 2026-10-20. This page shows what each requires.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## Must ads show the broker name?

In the three states we checked, ads must show the brokerage or broker name. The details differ.

| Rule | Texas | California | Florida |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brokerage or broker name in ads | Yes, license holder name and broker's name | Yes, responsible broker's name | Yes, licensed name of the brokerage firm |
| Size rule | Broker name at least half the size of the largest agent or team contact info | Not in 10 CCR 2773 | Not in 61J2-10.025 |
| License number in ads | Not in 535.155 | Yes, 8-digit license number | Not in 61J2-10.025 |
| Website placement | Homepage links to 2 TREC notices | Number on websites the licensee controls | Firm name next to contact info |
| Social media | Details may sit on a linked profile | Covers electronic ads the licensee controls | Internet rules apply |
| Rule | 22 TAC 535.155, 531.18, 531.20 | 10 CCR 2773 | Fla. Admin. Code 61J2-10.025 |

These rules apply when your state requires a real estate license for the property management work you do. Check your state's license board for that first.

**Texas.** 22 TAC 535.155(a) says each ad must show, in a readily noticeable place, the name of the license holder or team, and "the broker's name in at least half the size of the largest contact information for any sales agent, associated broker, or team name." "Advertisement" covers websites, email, text messages, social media, business cards, signs and billboards. It does not cover a message to a current client.

**Florida.** Rule 61J2-10.025(1) says ads must be made "in a manner in which reasonable persons would know they are dealing with a real estate licensee," and "must include the licensed name of the brokerage firm." Online, the firm name goes "adjacent to or immediately above or below the point of contact information."

## Do I need a license number on the site?

In California, yes. 10 CCR 2773(a) requires a broker or salesperson to show its "eight (8) digit real estate license identification number and responsible broker's name" on solicitation materials that are the first point of contact with consumers. The list includes:

- business cards and stationery;
- websites the licensee owns, controls or maintains;
- flyers, postal mail and promotional materials;
- electronic ads, including internet, email and the opening section of streaming video;
- print ads;
- "for rent" and "for lease" signs that show the licensee's name.

Texas does not require a license number in its ad rule, but it requires two homepage links:

1. **Consumer Protection Notice** (22 TAC 531.18): labeled "Texas Real Estate Commission Consumer Protection Notice" in at least 10-point font, or "TREC Consumer Protection Notice" in at least 12-point font.
2. **Information About Brokerage Services** (22 TAC 531.20): a completed IABS notice, labeled "Texas Real Estate Commission Information About Brokerage Services" in at least 10-point font, or "TREC Information About Brokerage Services" in at least 12-point font.

On social media, both links may sit on the account profile or a page reached by a direct link.

## Which claims can get me in trouble?

Misleading claims, unsupported rankings and Fair Housing violations cause the most trouble. Texas lists 20 types of misleading ads in 22 TAC 535.155(d). Those most relevant to property managers:

- an ad that "is inaccurate in any material fact or representation";
- an ad that "ranks the license holder or another service provider unless the ranking is based on objective criteria disclosed in the advertisement";
- an ad about property value, unless based on a disclosed appraisal or allowed under 535.17;
- an ad that promotes another service provider when you expect pay from it, without disclosing that you may be paid;
- an ad that implies a sales agent runs the brokerage, for example a "CEO" title for an agent.

So "#1 property manager in Austin" needs a disclosed, objective basis. "Best rated" needs the source and date. A rent estimate tool on your site should say it is an estimate.

Fair Housing applies in every state. 24 CFR 100.75(a) makes it unlawful to publish any notice, statement or ad for the rental of a dwelling "which indicates any preference, limitation or discrimination because of race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin." It covers flyers, signs, banners, applications and other documents. A listing that says "no kids" shows a familial status preference and breaks it.

### Ad and website audit checklist

- [ ] Brokerage name on every ad, page and profile, in the size your state requires
- [ ] License number where required (California: 8 digits plus responsible broker)
- [ ] Texas: both TREC links on the homepage, right label, right font size
- [ ] Brokerage name next to phone and email online (Florida)
- [ ] No ranking or "best" claim without a disclosed, objective source
- [ ] Referral fees from vendors disclosed where required
- [ ] Rental listings free of words that show a protected-class preference
- [ ] Social profiles carry the same required details

## How do rules differ by state?

Each state writes its own advertising rule, so the same website can pass in one state and fail in another. The three states we read show the range: Florida centers on the firm name, California on the license number, and Texas on name size and homepage notices.

If you manage in more than one state, build to the strictest rule in each area:

1. List every state where you hold a license or manage homes.
2. Pull each state's ad rule from the license board or the official code.
3. Build a footer that meets all of them: brokerage name, license numbers, required links.
4. Put the same details in each social profile.
5. Re-check once a year; boards amend these rules.
