## Which tax forms go to owners and vendors?

Tax-form work starts with classifying payments and payees correctly. Ask your tax professional to review current IRS instructions before staff generate forms. Do not let a software default decide the reporting treatment.

The IRS instructions for Forms 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC address rent reporting by property managers and applicable nonemployee compensation. The instructions also describe exceptions and special payment cases. Use the current rules for the relevant reporting year rather than an old threshold.

Check each payee's legal name, tax classification and taxpayer information through the approved process. Keep sensitive records in controlled storage. Do not ask staff to send tax identifiers through a broad team chat.

Reconcile reportable rent with property records. Owner payouts can differ from rental receipts because of fees, bills and other activity. Have the tax professional explain those differences before the owner asks why the totals do not match.

Review vendor payments by payment method and type. Avoid duplicate reporting when another reporting rule applies. Ask the preparer to document the treatment of uncertain cases.

This is general information, not tax advice. Check the current law or ask a tax professional.

Put preparation, review, recipient delivery and filing steps into the tax calendar after confirming current deadlines. This guide does not set a universal filing date.

## Which owner reviews should I hold?

Owner reviews should connect the year's property records with the owner's next decisions. Start with owners who have unresolved repairs, unclear cash plans or a likely change in use. Give every owner a clear route to request a review.

Prepare the account statement, lease position, known repair work and pending approvals. Ask whether the owner's goals have changed. A home held for a temporary move may need a different plan from a long-term rental.

Separate service issues from investment choices. Resolve unexplained charges or missed updates before proposing a new purchase. Do not use an annual meeting as a reason to sell more services before fixing current work.

Use this generic year-end checklist and adapt it to your calendar:

- **Check company books.** Review reconciliations, unpaid bills and earned fees.
- **Check client records.** Resolve beneficiary differences and unexplained balances.
- **Confirm payee details.** Route tax information through controlled storage.
- **Review tax-form scope.** Obtain the preparer's current reporting and filing instructions.
- **Prepare owner reports.** Explain receipts, expenses and distributions clearly.
- **List property decisions.** Assign repair, lease and funding questions.
- **Review agreements.** Identify upcoming terms, notice needs and scope gaps.
- **Review service results.** Read complaints and missed promises alongside metrics.
- **Build the cash plan.** Use actual company receipts and cost dates.
- **Assign follow-up.** Give every open item a lead and completion condition.

This checklist proposes a workflow. It is not a legal retention schedule or a substitute for professional review.

## When should I review fees and agreements?

Review fees and agreements before planning the next service year, with enough time for any required process. Check the signed terms and local rules before changing charges. A year-end announcement does not itself authorize a new fee.

Build an inventory of current agreements and amendments. Identify which service scope and price apply to each owner. Keep legacy terms visible rather than assuming every account uses the latest template.

Compare billed fees with the work performed. Review staff time, field travel, reporting demands and unusual owner requests. A weak margin may call for a process change rather than a price increase alone.

Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to compare proposed fee structures. Keep the assumptions and effective dates separate from current billed income. Have accounting staff test a sample statement.

Ask counsel to review unclear scope, termination, disclosures and authority. Give the lawyer actual examples of the work that created questions. A practical example can reveal a gap that a broad request to review the contract misses.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

Update sales and onboarding materials only after the revised offer is approved. Staff should not promise a new service while the contract still describes the old scope.

## Which numbers belong in next year's plan?

Next year's plan should use actual company results, expected workload and cash timing. Separate owner property results from the management company's income. Door growth alone does not show whether the company can fund the service.

Buildium's accounting guide recommends aligning measures with business goals and using consistent records. Apply that approach to recurring fees, leasing income, service cost and owner losses. Use definitions that staff can reproduce.

Review why owners left. A property sale, owner move-back and service complaint need different planning responses. Keep stated reasons separate from your team's assumptions.

Check capacity by role. Ask where onboarding, account review, field work and urgent coverage already strain staff. Include training and supervision when estimating the cost of new work.

IRS Publication 583 explains the importance of business records and accounting methods. Use those records as the planning base. Keep management estimates distinct from the booked result.

Stress the cash plan with delayed receipts, fewer placements and an owner exit. Record what would change before payroll or service coverage becomes a problem. Give optional spending a clear approval route.

Use the plan to choose work the team can deliver, rather than setting a growth number and hoping capacity follows.

## What should be complete before closing the checklist?

Close the checklist when the assigned reviewer can see the evidence for each completed task. A checked box without a report or decision is not enough. Keep open items visible after the calendar changes.

Ask another staff member to locate a signed agreement, a year-end statement and a tax work record. That test checks whether the archive is usable. Repair missing links and permissions before staff leave.

Keep unresolved matters with a named lead. Record what remains unknown, who will obtain the answer and what can proceed meanwhile. Do not bury outstanding account differences inside next year's opening figures.

Review the checklist after the close. Remove duplicate tasks and add steps that the team missed. Preserve the completed record so the next review starts from evidence.
