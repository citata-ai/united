## How long can you rent and keep the home sale exclusion?

The home sale exclusion depends on your actual ownership, residence, and sale dates. IRS Publication 523 generally tests ownership and residence over the 5 years before sale. Each test normally requires at least 2 years, with other conditions and exceptions.

Do not convert that rule into a promise that every owner can rent for a fixed period tax-free. The date you last lived there, prior use, another excluded sale, and other facts can change the result. Ask your tax professional to calculate your own deadline.

Renting also creates tax records for the property. Publication 523 says the exclusion does not cover gain attributable to certain depreciation after May 6, 1997. A later sale can therefore have taxable gain even if you meet the main-home tests.

Get the timeline before signing a lease that could overlap your planned sale. Have counsel check the lease and sale plan under local law. A tax target does not give you a right to remove a tenant on demand.

Keep a dated file with move-in, move-out, rental availability, lease, and proposed closing dates. Review the file when the plan changes. Do not rely on an old calendar reminder after extending the rental period.

## What if rents also fall?

A slow sale market does not guarantee strong rent. Estimate rent from local comparable leases and test a lower collection case. Keep asking rent separate from rent actually signed and received.

Budget the mortgage, taxes, insurance, utilities you pay, management, maintenance, and replacements. Add leasing and turnover costs. Use the [management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) when paid management forms part of the plan.

Here is an assumed monthly budget, not local market data:

| Item | Assumed cash |
| --- | ---: |
| Collected rent | $2,000 |
| Debt and operating cash costs | -$1,700 |
| Replacement reserve contribution | -$200 |
| Cash left before personal tax | $100 |

If collected rent falls to $1,800 with the same costs, cash becomes negative $100. The small starting margin matters more than a confident story about future prices.

Ask whether your personal budget can carry both your next home and the rental during a gap in rent. Keep enough accessible cash for repair and leasing delays. A property that only works with continuous full rent needs a different purchase, lease, or sale plan.

## How do you set a break-even date?

Set a review date and calculate the future sale proceeds needed to justify waiting. Compare net proceeds after debt, costs, and estimated tax. Do not compare a future gross asking price with today's net offer.

Start with the net amount you could receive from a sale now. Then add the cash you expect to contribute during the hold. Subtract cash you expect to keep from operations. Add later selling costs and the opportunity cost you choose to use.

For an assumed example, waiting costs $6,000 in net cash and adds $4,000 in sale preparation. You need $10,000 more net proceeds merely to cover those added cash costs. That is a math example, not an expected price rise. It leaves out time value, tax changes, and risk.

Keep principal reduction separate from interest and other spending. A mortgage payment can affect both cash flow and future payoff. Your worksheet should reflect the ending loan balance rather than count the benefit twice.

Write the review trigger in advance. It can depend on reserve cash, lease timing, or revised sale evidence. A clear trigger helps you stop extending the plan only because the hoped-for sale price has not appeared.

## What are the risks of waiting?

Waiting exposes you to both rental operations and a later sale market. Price, repairs, insurance, taxes, and your own cash needs can change. A rental lease also adds terms you must account for in the sale plan.

Inspect the house before deciding. Identify near-term work and get quotes. A quiet home sale period can become an expensive holding period if a major system fails.

Confirm insurance for the planned rental use and any vacancy. Check local rental registration, inspection, and lease requirements with the relevant office. Ask counsel about sale access and notice rules for an occupied home.

Tax deductions do not remove the cash cost. IRS Topic 414 explains common rental expenses. IRS Publication 527 covers conversion and rental records. Have your preparer distinguish operating costs, improvements, and depreciation before treating the budget as an after-tax result.

Keep a case with no price recovery. If you can fund and accept that result, renting may remain a reasonable choice. If the plan needs a fast rebound to avoid financial strain, selling at a supported price now deserves a serious comparison.

## What should you decide before leasing?

Decide whether you want to operate a rental or only delay an uncomfortable sale. The first choice needs a service plan and reserves. The second still creates the same duties once you accept a tenant.

Ask a local agent for a supported net sale estimate. Ask a manager for supported rent and a full cost quote. Give both estimates to your tax professional for the timing review.

Then choose a lease strategy that fits the lawful exit plan. Keep the review date, cash limit, and decision criteria with your property records. Share the plan with anyone who relies on the household budget.

Your next step is a side-by-side worksheet for selling now and renting under a difficult case. Choose from the supported numbers and your ability to carry risk. A possible future recovery belongs in a scenario, not in the cash you rely on today.
