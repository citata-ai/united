## Can a landlord change a lease after it is signed?

A landlord usually cannot change a signed fixed-term lease alone. A lease is a contract. Both you and the tenant agreed to its terms for a set period. To change a term, you need the tenant's consent, or a clause in the lease that already allows the change.

The answer depends on 3 things:

| Question | If yes | If no |
|---|---|---|
| Is it a fixed-term lease? | Most changes need the tenant's written consent | Month-to-month: you can change terms with notice |
| Does the lease already allow the change? | Follow the lease clause and the notice it sets | Treat it as a new term |
| Does a state law force the change? | Some states let you amend at once | Follow the normal rules |

Arizona gives an example of the last row. Under A.R.S. 33-1342(C), if a government body changes a law that affects existing leases, the landlord may amend the lease right away. The landlord must give the tenant written notice with a short description and the effective date.

## Can I add a fee mid-lease?

You cannot add a new fee in the middle of a fixed-term lease without the tenant's agreement, unless the lease already allows that fee. A new fee changes the money terms the tenant agreed to.

Common fees owners want to add, and what to do:

- **Pet fee for a new pet.** The tenant asks for a change, so offer a pet addendum with the fee. The tenant can accept or decline.
- **Utility charge for a service you paid before.** Wait for the renewal, or get a signed addendum.
- **Parking or storage fee.** Charge it only for a new space the tenant chooses to rent.
- **Late fee or a higher late fee.** Wait for the renewal. Check your state's limits on late fees first.

Rent works the same way. A fixed-term lease sets the rent for the term. Some states add more limits. Oregon's ORS 90.323 applies to any tenancy other than week-to-week. It says the landlord may not raise rent in the first year of the tenancy, and may not raise it more than once in any 12-month period. The landlord must give at least 90 days' written notice.

## How do I add a new rule?

You add a new rule by giving written notice, and the rule must be fair and must not change the core deal. Oregon and Arizona show how this works.

| Rule | Oregon ORS 90.262 | Arizona A.R.S. 33-1342 |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Convenience, safety or welfare of tenants; protect the property; fair use of shared services | Must be reasonably related to its purpose |
| Applies to all tenants | Yes, in a fair manner | Required condition |
| Written notice | At signing or when the rule is adopted | At signing, or 30 days' notice for a new rule |
| Rule that substantially changes the lease | Not valid without the tenant's written consent | Not enforceable |

So a new rule like "no grills on wood balconies" may be fine with notice. A new rule like "no pets" for a tenant whose lease allows a pet is a substantial change. It needs the tenant's consent.

Steps to add a rule the right way:

1. **Write the rule** in plain words. Say what the tenant must or must not do.
2. **State the reason,** such as safety or protecting the building.
3. **Check the test:** does it change the deal the tenant signed? If yes, get consent.
4. **Apply it to every tenant** in the building in the same way.
5. **Send written notice** to each tenant with the effective date.
6. **Wait the notice period** your state sets, for example 30 days in Arizona.
7. **Keep a copy** of each notice and the delivery date.

## Does the tenant have to agree?

For most changes to a fixed-term lease, yes, the tenant has to agree. Oregon says it in its statute: a rule adopted after the tenant moves in that "works a substantial modification of the bargain" is not valid unless the tenant consents in writing (ORS 90.262).

When the tenant agrees, use a lease addendum. A good addendum has:

- [ ] The names of all tenants and the unit address.
- [ ] The date and name of the original lease.
- [ ] The exact term that changes, with the old and the new wording.
- [ ] The effective date.
- [ ] A line that says all other terms stay the same.
- [ ] The signature and date of every tenant and the landlord.

Give the tenant a real choice. If the tenant says no, keep the current terms until the lease ends. Then offer the new terms in the renewal.

## When do changes take effect on month-to-month leases?

On a month-to-month tenancy, changes take effect after the notice period your state sets. The tenant then accepts the new terms by staying, or ends the tenancy.

| State | Change of terms | Rent increase | Statute |
|---|---|---|---|
| California | Written notice; effective after at least 30 days | 30 days for 10% or less; 90 days for over 10% in 12 months | Civil Code 827 |
| Oregon | Rules: see ORS 90.262 | At least 90 days; not in the first year; once per 12 months | ORS 90.323 |
| Florida | No change-of-terms rule in 83.57; either side can end with at least 30 days' notice before the end of a monthly period | Not stated in 83.57 | Fla. Stat. 83.57 |
| New York | Notice for a renewal with a rent increase of 5% or more: 30, 60 or 90 days by length of occupancy | Same | RPL 226-c |

California's rule is the clearest. Civil Code 827(a) says the new terms become part of the lease if the tenant stays after the notice takes effect. Civil Code 827(b) measures the 10% against the rent at any time in the prior 12 months.

New York's notice depends on how long the tenant has lived there. Under RPL 226-c, the landlord gives at least 30 days' notice for under 1 year, 60 days for 1 to 2 years, and 90 days for over 2 years.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## A short decision path

Use this before you change any lease term:

1. **Read the lease.** Does it already allow the change?
2. **Check the lease type.** Fixed-term or month-to-month?
3. **Check your state law** for notice periods and limits.
4. **Fixed-term:** get a signed addendum or wait for renewal.
5. **Month-to-month:** send written notice for the full period.
6. **Record everything** in the tenant file.
