## What response time do owners expect?

Owners need a clear next step more than an empty promise of fast service. Ask each owner how they want routine updates and urgent decisions handled. Record those choices during onboarding. Do not assume a national response benchmark fits your market or team.

Property Meld's communication guide treats communication as a core part of maintenance service. That supports a useful distinction: sending a receipt and solving a problem are separate events. Neither event should hide the other in your reports.

Define a useful reply as a message that names the person responsible, states the current status, and gives the next update point. If you need owner approval, state the decision and the effect of a delay. Keep the request short enough to answer.

Put service hours beside every routine contact channel. Explain how weekends, holidays and staff leave affect that channel. Give owners an urgent route that does not depend on finding their usual manager.

Ask a new owner to repeat the contact process back to you. If they cannot explain it, simplify the process. A welcome packet that nobody can use will not prevent a missed message.

## How fast should maintenance requests get a reply?

Maintenance reports should reach a person who can assess risk before routine scheduling begins. Separate safety triage from the scheduling queue. Give residents emergency-service instructions where immediate danger exists, then explain how to reach your team.

Use the following as a proposed service design, not a legal deadline or industry benchmark.

| Request class | First action | Useful reply includes |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate danger | Direct caller to emergency services and alert the on-call lead | Safety instructions and the responsible contact |
| Urgent property risk | Assess the report and seek safe access | Dispatch status and next update |
| Routine repair | Check scope, access and approval needs | Assigned coordinator and scheduling step |
| Owner account question | Route to the right staff member | What was checked and what remains open |

Do not let an incomplete web form block urgent help. Staff should be able to create the record after the call. Ask for enough detail to act without asking residents to diagnose equipment.

Ready.gov's business guidance includes communications planning and continuity planning. Use that approach to define a backup when phones or software fail. Print the emergency contact route for the staff who take calls.

After dispatch, keep ownership of the update. A vendor appointment does not close the request. Confirm whether the resident can use the affected service and whether follow-up work remains.

## How do I track response time?

Track each stage with its own timestamp and use a consistent clock. Record when a request arrives, when a person reviews it, and when staff give a useful reply. Keep dispatch and completion times separate.

Choose whether routine reports use elapsed time or service hours. Publish that choice internally. Urgent safety reports need their own process and should not disappear from view when the office closes.

Review the slowest open requests as well as the average. An average can look healthy while a resident waits without an update. Check unanswered requests, repeated contacts and cases that staff closed too early.

Use a small audit sample to test reply quality. Read the actual message. Did staff answer the question, name the next action and set an update point? A blank status change should not count as service.

Record the reason for missed targets. Useful categories include wrong routing, missing coverage, unclear approval and vendor delay. Those causes need different fixes. A reminder to work faster does not solve a routing fault.

## Should response times be in the agreement?

Put tested response standards in the agreement or a clearly referenced service schedule. Define the channel, service hours, urgency rules and meaning of a reply. Avoid a broad promise that every issue will be resolved immediately.

Have local counsel review the language against your duties and local repair rules. A service target must not replace a legal deadline. This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

Explain the owner's part in the process. Who can approve work when the owner is away? What happens when staff cannot reach them? Which spending authority already applies? Align those answers with the signed agreement.

Do not use exclusions to remove all responsibility. Instead, explain the action you will take when access, parts or approvals delay work. A useful promise covers updates during a delay as well as the first reply.

Check the same wording across your proposal, website, welcome packet and phone script. An owner may rely on the sales promise even when operations staff have never seen it. Remove claims the team cannot measure.

Before launch, ask staff to handle a mock urgent report during a shift change. Check who receives it, who takes ownership and who sends the update.

## How should I introduce the standard?

Introduce response standards as a trial with a named service lead. Start with current request records and find where useful replies stop. Repair that handoff before buying another tool.

Give staff examples of an acceptable reply for an account question, repair and complaint. Let them adapt the wording while keeping the required information. Avoid scripts that repeat the ticket number without helping the reader.

Review missed promises with the person who owns the process. Change coverage or routing when the evidence supports it. If demand exceeds capacity, narrow the promise before staff start hiding late work.

Tell owners where to see progress and how to challenge an unresolved issue. Keep the escalation route easy to find. Use the [guide library](/guides) for related operating decisions, then write the service standard your own team can deliver.
