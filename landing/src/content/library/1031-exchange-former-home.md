## When does a former home become investment property?

A former home needs genuine investment or business use to support Section 1031 treatment. Merely deciding to sell your residence does not establish that use. IRS Publication 544 explains that personal-use property does not qualify for a like-kind exchange.

A real rental conversion should have records that match the claimed use. Keep market rent evidence, advertisements, leases, payment records, expense records, and personal-use dates. A short lease created only to support a desired tax label needs careful professional review.

Ask a tax professional to assess your plan before you list for sale. Explain whether you intend to rent, sell, return to the home, or acquire another investment. Your conduct and documents should accurately reflect that intent.

| Issue | Primary IRS source | What to confirm |
|---|---|---|
| Investment property status | Publication 544 | Qualifying business or investment use |
| Home sale exclusion | Publication 523 | Ownership, residence, and other limits |
| Combined treatment | Revenue Procedure 2005-14 | Order of exclusion and exchange rules |
| Dwelling safe harbor | Revenue Procedure 2008-16 | Ownership, fair rent, and personal use |
| Deferred exchange timing | Publication 544 | Identification, receipt, and funds handling |

Keep these as separate tests. Meeting a residence requirement does not prove investment use. Meeting exchange timing does not establish the home sale exclusion.

## How do Section 121 and Section 1031 combine?

IRS Revenue Procedure 2005-14 applies Section 121 before Section 1031 when both apply to a qualifying transaction. The home sale exclusion can remove eligible gain first. Exchange rules then address qualifying gain that remains.

IRS Publication 523 describes a potential exclusion up to $250,000, or $500,000 for qualifying joint filers. Its general ownership and residence tests use 2 years within the 5-year period before sale. Other conditions, exceptions, and limits matter.

Depreciation needs separate attention. Publication 523 says gain tied to depreciation allowed or allowable after May 6, 1997, cannot receive the home sale exclusion. Revenue Procedure 2005-14 explains that Section 1031 may apply to that gain in a qualifying exchange.

Cash or other property received can affect the calculation. The revenue procedure contains specific coordination rules for such amounts and replacement basis. Do not simplify the result to all gain disappears.

Also review nonqualified-use rules under Publication 523. A property's full residence and rental history matters. Bring a dated timeline instead of relying on a rough statement that you once lived there.

## How long should you rent the former home first?

No single conversion period resolves every investment-use question. IRS Revenue Procedure 2008-16 offers a safe harbor for qualifying dwelling units. A safe harbor is a defined route to protection, not a promise that every other case fails.

For relinquished property, the revenue procedure requires ownership for at least 24 months immediately before the exchange. During each of the 2 relevant 12-month periods, it requires at least 14 days rented at fair rent.

It also limits personal use in each period to no more than the greater of 14 days or 10% of fair-rental days. These are the safe harbor's own tests. Do not substitute the separate vacation-home residence test without checking the wording.

The revenue procedure has corresponding ownership and use conditions for replacement property after the exchange. Ask your adviser to review both sides. A qualifying sale plan can still fail if the replacement property's use does not fit.

The Section 121 timeline also continues to matter while you rent. Do not extend rental use to meet a desired exchange plan without checking how it affects the exclusion. Model the options before committing to dates.

## Which exchange deadlines must you plan around?

IRS Publication 544 generally requires written identification of replacement property within 45 days after transferring the relinquished property. It describes delivery and description requirements. Naming a property informally in your own notes is not enough.

The exchange period generally ends at the earlier of the 180th day after transfer or your return due date, including extensions. Publication 544 explains these rules and possible special relief. Have your adviser calculate the actual dates for your transaction.

Plan funds handling before closing. The IRS exchange FAQ warns about actual or constructive receipt of proceeds. It describes qualified intermediary and other arrangements designed to meet the applicable rules.

Do not send proceeds to your personal account and assume you can later label the purchase an exchange. Ask the intermediary and tax adviser to review closing instructions in advance. Confirm everyone understands the planned transaction.

## What records support investment intent and reporting?

Records should show the actual property use, cash, basis, and exchange process. Keep original documents rather than only a summary spreadsheet. Accurate records help your adviser apply rules that depend on dates and use.

- **Build the timeline.** Record purchase, residence, rental, personal-use, and sale dates.
- **Save rental evidence.** Keep advertisements, fair-rent support, leases, and received payments.
- **Preserve basis records.** Include purchase costs, improvements, and depreciation schedules.
- **Document the exchange.** Keep intermediary agreements, identification notices, and closing instructions.
- **Track replacement use.** Record occupancy and rental activity after acquisition.
- **Review reporting.** Ask the preparer which forms and calculations support the result.

Compare the exchange with a sale before paying transaction costs. Consider liquidity, the next property's risks, and the tax result. Deferral is not the same as an investment that fits your needs.
