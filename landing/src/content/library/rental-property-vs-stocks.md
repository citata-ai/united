## How do returns compare with the same cash?

Compare both investments using the same starting cash and the same holding period. For a rental, include the down payment, closing costs, initial work, and cash reserve. For a fund, include invested cash and any cash you keep aside.

Use this comparison before you enter a return forecast:

| Item | Direct rental | Index fund |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial costs | Purchase, financing, repairs | Trading and account costs where applicable |
| Ongoing costs | Repairs, tax, insurance, management | Fund and account fees |
| Debt | Include mortgage terms | State whether the purchase uses debt |
| Cash received | Rent less cash costs | Distributions or share sales |
| Exit costs | Selling costs and debt payoff | Trading costs and taxes where applicable |

Do not compare leveraged property gains with unleveraged fund gains without showing the debt. Borrowing changes the effect of both gains and losses on your own cash.

As an assumed example, $60,000 invested and $3,600 annual cash received equals a 6% cash yield. That is not the total return. Include changes in value, additional cash paid, and selling costs separately. Use the same method for the fund.

## How much time does each need?

A direct rental needs an operating plan for leasing, repairs, money, and tenant communication. An index fund needs investment selection and account review. Neither choice makes your decisions disappear.

For the rental, write a task list before you assign a time value. Include calls, vendor selection, records, inspections, and changes between tenants. Then decide which tasks you will do and which you will pay someone to do.

A manager can reduce your work, but you still review results and supply cash when needed. Include that service cost in the comparison. Use the [management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to keep the fee assumptions visible.

For a fund, read the prospectus and understand the index. Investor.gov notes that index funds track a market index and still carry investment risk. A narrow fund can expose you to a limited area of the market.

Do not make unpaid owner labor vanish from the rental case. Record it even if you choose not to subtract a wage. This makes the choice useful if your health, job, or interest in direct management later changes.

## How do taxes differ?

Rental and fund taxes depend on the income, transaction, and account involved. IRS Topic 414 covers rental income, expenses, and depreciation. IRS Publication 925 explains limits that can affect rental losses.

Ask a tax professional to compare the actual ownership and account choices. A rental expense deduction does not guarantee a current tax saving. Likewise, a fund inside a retirement account can have different tax treatment from a taxable account.

For the rental, keep a separate schedule for operating cash, debt reduction, depreciation, and sale proceeds. Those figures describe different parts of the investment. Do not treat tax depreciation as cash you received.

For the fund, separate distributions, price changes, and share sales. Ask your preparer which events affect your return. Do not assume a quoted total return equals cash available after tax.

Compare after-tax scenarios only after the rules fit your facts. Keep a pre-tax comparison as a clear reference. If the decision works only because of a complex tax benefit, ask what happens when that benefit is delayed, limited, or unavailable. Do not buy a weak property only to create deductions.

## How does access to your money differ?

A property sale and a fund share sale require different exit plans. Investor.gov defines liquidity as how easily or quickly a security can trade. Market access and price risk are separate questions.

A rental owner usually needs a buyer for the property or a lender willing to refinance it. Plan for the work, transaction costs, and time that process may take. Equity is not the same as cash in your bank account.

A traded fund may offer a simpler sale process, but you still accept the market price and settlement timing. Check the product's terms. Do not assume every fund has the same trading rules or access.

Consider a personal emergency during a bad market. How would you raise cash without a forced property sale or unwanted share sale? Keep that question outside optimistic return forecasts.

Also test concentration. Investor.gov explains diversification as spreading money across investments to reduce risk. Owning several homes on the same street does not remove every shared local risk. A fund focused on one sector can also leave a concentrated exposure. Review what you actually own.

## How should you make the choice?

Choose the investment that fits your cash needs, skill, time, and willingness to accept losses. Start with the work you can sustain rather than the return you hope to earn.

For each option, write a base case and a difficult case. In the rental case, reduce collections and add a large repair. In the fund case, test a lower market value when you need cash. These are planning scenarios, not predictions.

Ask an adviser to challenge the assumptions you control least. For a property, that may be rent growth or repair costs. For a fund, it may be the time you can remain invested without selling.

Your next step is a side-by-side cash worksheet. Keep the same starting amount, time horizon, and tax basis. Make the choice only after you can explain both the potential reward and how you would handle an unfavorable result.

This is general information, not tax advice. Check the current law or ask a tax professional.
