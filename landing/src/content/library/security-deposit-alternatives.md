## How does deposit insurance work?

Deposit insurance works according to the actual policy, not the label on a leasing page. Identify who buys coverage, who receives payment and which losses qualify. Ask for the policy form before you agree to use the product.

A surety bond can involve a tenant, an owner and a surety that backs covered obligations. An insurance product can use a different structure. A landlord fee program may fund coverage without making the tenant an insured person. Do not combine those arrangements in your tenant notice.

| Arrangement | What to review | Main question |
|---|---|---|
| Cash deposit | Lease and deposit law | Which deductions and refunds are lawful? |
| Surety bond | Bond and tenant agreement | Can the surety seek repayment from the tenant? |
| Insurance product | Policy and endorsements | Who has coverage, and for which losses? |
| Fee in lieu | Signed fee agreement and state law | Can the tenant switch to a deposit? |
| Deposit installments | Payment agreement | Does the money remain a refundable deposit? |

This table is a review framework. It does not claim that every product in a category works alike. Ask the provider to show the contract provision behind each answer. Keep those answers with your leasing policy.

## What does the tenant pay?

The tenant pays the fees and other charges stated in the applicable agreement. Ask whether those fees are refundable, recurring or subject to change. Do not advertise a fee as the same thing as a refundable deposit.

Use a plain comparison sheet. Show the cash due at signing, later fees and possible amounts owed after damage. Explain whether a payment reduces a damage balance. If the answer depends on an insurer's later decision, say so.

Texas Property Code 92.111 requires a deposit option when a landlord offers its statutory fee alternative. It also requires written disclosures and a signed agreement. The tenant's choice cannot determine application approval. Read the full section before designing an offer.

Florida Statute 83.491 also sets written agreement and disclosure rules for its fee-in-lieu option. The statute distinguishes that arrangement from certain insurance or surety products. A provider's national sales page does not settle which rules apply to your offer.

Avoid adding a sample price without a quote. Request a quote for the actual property and proposed lease. Give the tenant a copy of the comparison before selection. Save the version the tenant received.

## How do claims work?

Claims follow the provider's contract and applicable law, so confirm the process before the first lease begins. Find the event that starts the claim window. Ask whether that event is move-out, lease termination, discovery or another date.

Prepare a claim file that ties each amount to a supported loss. Include the signed lease, condition records, payment ledger and repair evidence. Separate unpaid rent from physical damage. Do not send an unexplained total from the tenant ledger.

Ask the provider these questions in writing:

- **Confirm coverage.** Which rent, damage and collection costs does the contract cover?
- **Check exclusions.** What circumstances can cause denial or reduce payment?
- **Identify deadlines.** Which notices and documents must arrive before each cutoff?
- **Track recovery.** Can the provider pursue the tenant after paying the owner?
- **Resolve disputes.** How can the owner and tenant contest a claim?

Keep ordinary tenant notices separate from the insurer submission. Filing a claim does not prove that you met lease or state notice duties. Have counsel check both processes before you start.

Track each amount received after submission. If a tenant pays later, notify the provider as the contract requires. Keep the account clear enough to show what remains unpaid. Do not let software charge the tenant again for a loss already resolved.

## What are the risks for the landlord?

The main risks are a gap in coverage, a missed deadline and a tenant notice that overstates protection. A program can be easy to enroll in but hard to claim against. Test the claim process using a sample file before rollout.

Ask how coverage continues after renewal or a manager change. Confirm what happens if a fee payment fails. Check whether the owner must pay any amount before coverage responds. Ask whether the provider can cancel the program and how existing leases are handled.

Review the owner's management contract as well. Who chooses the product? Who files claims? Who bears a denied claim if the manager missed a deadline? Put those duties in writing before staff start selecting alternatives for owners.

Check accounting treatment with your bookkeeper. Keep refundable tenant funds separate from program fees in the records. Do not give staff a generic deposit code for every payment. A wrong code can create a false refund balance at move-out.

## How should I decide whether to offer an alternative?

Offer an alternative only when you can explain the tenant cost and the owner's protection in plain language. Start with a limited internal review. Ask a leasing employee, a claims employee and counsel to read the same contract. Compare their answers.

Build a written approval checklist. Confirm the product's availability, the issuer, the policy form, the claim process and the local rules. Record who approved it. Set a review date so a changed contract does not pass unnoticed into later leases.

Keep the choice voluntary where the applicable rules require it. Never suggest that a tenant must buy insurance merely to avoid rejection without checking the law. Train staff to explain the actual options rather than steer everyone toward the easiest enrollment screen.

Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to review the wider owner budget. Then ask a provider for the full contract and a written claim example. Decide from those records, rather than a promise of deposit-free leasing.
