## Why is doors per manager a weak metric?

Doors per manager is a weak metric because it counts only the people with the "property manager" title. The rest of the team stays out of the number.

Ben White, co-founder of Ailo, gives a clear example in Elite Agent (2025-04-14). He visited an agency that managed 1,600 properties "with just four property managers." When the owner drew the org chart, the real team was 18 people:

- 1 team leader
- 4 portfolio managers, each with a junior
- 1 remote professional
- 1 leasing agent
- shared administrators and a trust accountant

So is the ratio 400 doors per manager, or about 89 doors per person? White's point: the metric stuck because it is easy to measure, not because it is useful. He argues for "dollars, not doors": staff cost per property, or staff cost as a percent of revenue.

Doors per manager also ignores rent levels. A manager with 150 doors at $3,000 rent brings in far more fee revenue than a manager with 150 doors at $1,000 rent. The workload can be close. The economics are not.

## How do I calculate payroll percent of revenue?

Payroll percent is total staff cost divided by total revenue for the same period. Use 12 months, so seasonal leasing does not skew it.

**Payroll percent = (wages + bonuses + payroll taxes + benefits) ÷ total revenue × 100**

Decide these points once and write them down:

1. **Include every person.** Managers, leasing agents, accountants, admins, maintenance coordinators and in-house techs.
2. **Include your own pay.** If you do manager work, add a market salary for that role.
3. **Include contractors who do staff work.** A virtual assistant paid by the hour is staff cost, even with a 1099.
4. **Use all revenue.** Management fees, leasing fees, renewal fees and maintenance income.
5. **Keep pass-through money out.** Rent you collect for owners is not your revenue.

Benefits and payroll taxes matter a lot. In June 2026, US private industry employers paid $46.89 per hour worked in total compensation. Of that, $32.82 was wages and $14.07 was benefits, a 30.0% benefit share (BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation). If you count wages only, you can understate staff cost by a large amount.

## A worked example

This example uses stated inputs. Only the salary figure comes from BLS.

| Input | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Doors | 300 | example input |
| Total revenue, 12 months | $650,000 | example input |
| Property managers | 3 at $69,990 | BLS median, May 2025 |
| Admin | 1 at $45,000 | example input |
| Benefit share of total cost | 30% | BLS ECEC, June 2026 |

The steps:

1. Wages: 3 × $69,990 + $45,000 = $254,970.
2. Total staff cost: $254,970 ÷ 0.70 = $364,243 (wages are 70% of total cost when benefits are 30%).
3. Payroll percent with benefits: $364,243 ÷ $650,000 = 56.0%.
4. Payroll percent with wages only: $254,970 ÷ $650,000 = 39.2%.

The same firm reads 39% or 56%, based only on the definition. That is why you must fix the definition first. In this example, the owner's pay is not in the number yet. Add it and the ratio goes higher.

## What payroll percent is healthy?

A healthy range is 40% to 50% of potential revenue or lower, in Ben White's view. He writes that "the average staff cost as a percentage of potential revenue sits between 40% and 50%," and that "in well-run businesses, that figure can be much lower."

Read that benchmark with care:

- **It is one expert's view.** White runs Ailo, an Australian property management software company. The US market has different fee levels and laws.
- **It uses "potential revenue".** That means revenue at full occupancy and full fees, not what you collected.
- **It does not say what is in "staff cost".** Check if your own number includes benefits and owner pay before you compare.

We found no US public benchmark for this ratio from a primary source. So the best benchmark is your own trend. Track the number each month on a rolling 12-month basis. A rise of a few points over two quarters is a signal to look at pricing, staffing or process.

To check if your fees cover staff cost, run your numbers through the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator).

## How does payroll percent change as I grow?

Payroll percent moves in steps, not in a smooth line. Each new hire adds a full salary on day 1. Revenue from new doors comes in over months.

| Stage | What happens to payroll percent |
|---|---|
| Just before a hire | Lowest point. Staff carry the most doors. Service risk is highest. |
| Just after a hire | Jumps up. New salary, no new revenue yet. |
| New doors fill the seat | Falls back as revenue grows into the new cost. |
| Next hire | The cycle repeats. |

White gives a rule from growth planning. If you add 100 properties a quarter, plan to add 2 junior team members each quarter. He says your structure should follow your growth goals and culture, "not the other way around."

Use this to plan hires:

- **Set a ceiling.** For example: "We hire when rolling payroll percent falls below our target and the team carries more doors than our service standard allows."
- **Hire juniors into a ladder.** White says a pyramid of junior roles lets you promote from inside, so you recruit from outside less.
- **Tie pay to portfolio revenue.** White describes firms that publish salary scales linked to the revenue a manager runs. The manager picks the level they want to reach.

## How to turn the metric into a pay conversation

White makes one more point that helps with staff. The goal "each manager handles 180 properties" and the goal "I want to pay my managers $150,000" can mean the same thing for the business. The second one sounds like a career, not a load.

For US context, the BLS median wage for property, real estate and community association managers was $69,990 in May 2025. The lowest 10% earned less than $41,010. The highest 10% earned more than $139,680. A revenue-based pay ladder lets your best managers move toward the top of that range as the revenue they run grows.
