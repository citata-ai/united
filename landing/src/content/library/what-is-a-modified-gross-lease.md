## What is a modified gross lease?

A modified gross lease is a lease where the landlord and the tenant split the property's operating costs. PropertyMetrics describes it as a structure where "both the landlord and the tenant are responsible for paying a property's operating expenses." The lease lists the split.

Picture lease types on a line. At one end, the landlord pays all costs (absolute gross). At the other end, the tenant pays all costs (absolute net). A modified gross lease sits in the middle. PropertyMetrics notes that most leases fall in the middle, so this type is common.

| Lease type | Tenant pays | Landlord pays |
|-----------|-------------|---------------|
| Full-service gross | Base rent only | Taxes, insurance, CAM, utilities |
| Modified gross | Base rent plus some costs | The rest, as the lease says |
| Triple net (NNN) | Base rent, taxes, insurance, CAM | Roof and structure (usually) |

Source: Tyler Cauble's lease type table. CAM means common area maintenance: parking, lobbies, landscaping and cleaning of shared space.

## Which costs does the tenant pay in a modified gross lease?

The tenant pays the costs the lease names. No standard list exists. PropertyMetrics lists property taxes, property insurance and CAM as commonly negotiated costs, and adds utilities and structural repairs.

Common splits:

- **Tenant pays its own utilities.** Electric and gas for its own space. The landlord pays the rest.
- **Tenant pays increases only.** The landlord pays costs up to a base year or a stop. The tenant pays its share of anything above that.
- **Tenant pays some items in full.** For example, the tenant pays its own janitorial service, and the landlord pays taxes and insurance.

The tenant's share is usually pro rata, which means its part of the building. PropertyMetrics gives this example: a 10,000-square-foot tenant in a 100,000-square-foot building has a pro rata share of 10%. If shared costs are $1,000,000, the tenant's share is $100,000.

## How a base year works: worked example

A base year sets the landlord's share of costs. In later years, the tenant pays its share of the increase above the base year. PropertyMetrics notes the base year usually matches the calendar year the lease starts.

Example with made-up numbers. The tenant has 10% of the building. The lease uses 2026 as the base year.

| Year | Building operating costs | Increase over base year | Tenant pays (10%) |
|------|-------------------------|------------------------|-------------------|
| 2026 (base) | $500,000 | $0 | $0 |
| 2027 | $530,000 | $30,000 | $3,000 |
| 2028 | $560,000 | $60,000 | $6,000 |

The tenant pays base rent each year. It also pays $3,000 extra in 2027 and $6,000 extra in 2028. The landlord keeps paying the first $500,000 each year.

## How an expense stop works: worked example

An expense stop is a fixed dollar amount per square foot. The landlord pays up to the stop. The tenant pays the costs above it. PropertyMetrics shows why the lease wording matters.

Building: 100,000 square feet. Taxes are $100,000 ($1.00 per sq ft). Insurance is $25,000 ($0.25 per sq ft). The stop is $1.00 per sq ft.

| Stop applies to | Cost per sq ft | Above the stop | Tenants pay in total |
|-----------------|----------------|----------------|----------------------|
| Taxes and insurance as one group | $1.25 | $0.25 | $25,000 |
| Each item on its own | $1.00 and $0.25 | $0 and $0 | $0 |

Same building, same costs, same stop. One clause changes the tenants' bill from $25,000 to $0. Read that clause before you sign.

## Modified gross vs full-service gross lease

In a full-service gross lease, the tenant pays one flat rent and the landlord pays all operating costs. In a modified gross lease, the tenant pays some of those costs. The difference is who carries the risk of rising costs.

| Point | Full-service gross | Modified gross |
|-------|-------------------|----------------|
| Tenant's monthly bill | One flat rent | Rent plus listed costs |
| Who pays cost increases | Landlord | Shared, as the lease says |
| Tenant budget | Very easy to plan | Mostly easy, with a yearly true-up |
| Landlord risk | High if costs rise | Lower |
| Admin work | Low | Medium: tracking and yearly statements |

A landlord who fears rising taxes or insurance often moves from full-service gross to modified gross. A tenant who wants one fixed number often asks for full-service gross and accepts a higher rent.

## When do property managers use a modified gross lease?

Property managers use a modified gross lease most often in multi-tenant office and small commercial buildings. PropertyMetrics notes that landlords in multi-tenant buildings often use calendar-year base years because they are easier to track. It also says the choice between gross and modified gross "will largely depend on market conditions."

Common uses:

- **Small office buildings.** Each tenant pays its own electric; the landlord pays the rest up to a base year.
- **Mixed-use buildings.** Ground-floor shops on modified gross leases, apartments on normal residential leases.
- **Flex and light industrial units.** Tenant pays utilities and interior repairs.

Residential leases follow their own rules. Most residential tenants pay one rent plus some utilities. That is not called a modified gross lease, even though utilities are split. Rules on passing costs to residential tenants differ by state and city. This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## Checklist before you sign a modified gross lease

- **List every cost and who pays it.** Taxes, insurance, CAM, utilities, janitorial, repairs, roof, HVAC.
- **Find the base year or the stop.** Write the dollar amount.
- **Check how the stop applies.** As one group, or item by item.
- **Check the pro rata share.** Confirm both square-foot numbers.
- **Check the yearly statement rules.** Due date, what proof comes with it, and the tenant's audit right.
- **Check caps.** A cap on yearly increases changes the risk split.
