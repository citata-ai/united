## Which lines hold rent and rental expenses?

Schedule E separates rents received from expense categories. Use the form and instructions for the year you are filing. This guide refers to the IRS instructions available when researched, not a substitute return.

Start with a reconciled ledger for each property. Match rent receipts to bank activity, then separate deposits, owner transfers, and loan funds. A bank deposit is not automatically rental income.

IRS Publication 527 generally excludes a refundable security deposit when received if you plan to return it. Advance rent is different and generally enters income when received. Keep the payment purpose in the records.

| Item | IRS Schedule E location or treatment |
|---|---|
| Rents received | Line 3 |
| Advertising | Line 5 |
| Insurance | Line 9 |
| Management fees | Line 11 |
| Mortgage interest paid to banks | Line 12, subject to instructions |
| Repairs | Line 14 |
| Taxes | Line 16 |
| Utilities | Line 17 |
| Depreciation and depletion | Line 18 |

The table follows the IRS Schedule E form categories and instructions. Keep invoices behind the totals. An account label chosen by software does not prove the expense belongs on that line.

## Why should you reconcile before entering totals?

Reconciliation checks that the ledger matches the underlying transactions. A manager statement may already subtract fees from rent. Report the underlying rent and expenses correctly rather than counting only the net owner distribution.

Check payment processor reports against the ledger. Include payments received outside the platform. Investigate refunds, reversals, and year-end timing before assuming a report total equals taxable rent.

Separate owner contributions and withdrawals. Moving your own money into a rental account is not rent. Moving rental cash to your personal account is not a property expense.

Use a separate list for capital purchases and improvements. IRS Publication 527 distinguishes repairs from improvements. A large payment should receive review based on the work performed, not just its amount.

Save property tax bills, insurance terms, loan statements, and paid invoices. When a charge covers both personal and rental use, preserve the allocation method. Clear supporting records help your preparer avoid guessing.

## Where does depreciation go?

The IRS instructions place depreciation and depletion on Schedule E line 18. Depreciation recovers eligible asset cost under tax rules. It does not equal the year's cash spent on the property.

IRS Publication 527 explains that land is not depreciable. It also covers the allocation between land and building. Keep purchase documents and the basis calculation with the asset record.

For a former home converted to rental use, Publication 527 generally uses the lower of adjusted basis or fair market value for depreciation basis. The conversion date and valuation therefore matter. Ask your preparer to record the method used.

Depreciation generally begins when the property is ready and available for rent. That may differ from the first rent receipt. Keep listing, repair completion, and availability records to support the date.

Form 4562 can be required in situations described by the IRS instructions. Provide the asset purchase date, service date, cost, use, and prior depreciation. Do not enter a round annual estimate because the software requests a figure.

## How do fair rental days and personal-use days work?

Fair rental and personal-use days help determine allocation and limits. Record actual use on a calendar. Time available but not rented is not always treated the same as days actually rented for these tests.

IRS Publication 527 explains personal-use rules, including some use by family members and below-market arrangements. A day can count as personal even when money changes hands. Give your preparer the facts rather than a preselected label.

A home used both personally and as a rental can require divided expenses. A converted main home can also need special treatment of the period before rental use. Do not apply a vacation-home fraction without checking the applicable rule.

Keep direct rental costs separate from shared home costs. For example, a rental advertisement and a whole-home utility bill may need different allocation treatment. Preserve the reason for each split.

If only part of the home is rented, document the space and use. Publication 527 discusses reasonable expense allocation. Have a professional review the method when areas or use change during the year.

## When does rental income go on Schedule C instead?

Substantial services can change ordinary rental reporting. IRS Publication 527 says services mainly for tenant convenience can require Schedule C. Examples include certain hotel-like services, rather than simply providing heat or cleaning common areas.

Stay length alone does not answer the reporting question. Describe the services, frequency, and who provides them. Ask your tax professional how those facts affect both the income schedule and any self-employment tax.

Do not confuse this issue with passive activity status. IRS Publication 925 applies separate rules to passive activities and losses. A reporting schedule does not by itself resolve every loss limitation.

## What should you review before filing?

Review the property calculation and loss limits before transferring the result to your return. IRS Publication 925 explains passive and at-risk restrictions. A calculated rental loss is not always currently usable.

- **Match rent totals** to the ledger and supporting payment records.
- **Remove owner transfers** from income and expense categories.
- **Separate loan parts** using the interest and escrow records.
- **Review improvements** with the asset schedule.
- **Confirm use days** against leases and your calendar.
- **Carry forward records** for depreciation and any suspended losses.
