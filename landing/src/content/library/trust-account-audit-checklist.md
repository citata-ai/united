## Which records will the auditor ask for?

A trust account audit needs records that show whose money you hold and why each payment occurred. Start with the regulator's request. Use the checklist below to find gaps before you send records.

| Record | What to check | Supporting file |
|---|---|---|
| Bank statement | Account and period match | Statement from the bank |
| Control record | Receipts, payments and running balance | Transaction export |
| Beneficiary ledger | Balance for each owner or transaction | Detailed ledger |
| Reconciliation | Differences have an explanation | Signed review record |
| Receipt or payment | Purpose and authority match | Invoice, receipt or agreement |
| Account access | Current people have approved rights | Bank mandate and access list |

California's Department of Real Estate explains these record groups in its trust fund audit guidance. The DRE cites Regulations 2831, 2831.1 and 2831.2 for control records, separate records and reconciliation. These are California rules. Do not treat them as rules for every state.

Build an index that links each export to its source system. Keep the period, account name, export date and person who made the file. Give the reviewer a clear route from a bank entry to an owner ledger and invoice.

Include records for closed accounts that fall within the review period. Ask your bank for missing statements early. A closed account can still contain transactions that the auditor must trace.

## How should the trust account be titled?

Trust account naming must follow your state's rules and the bank's actual account records. A label inside your software does not change the bank account title.

For California, the DRE cites Regulation 2832 and Business and Professions Code section 10145. Its guidance requires a trust designation in the broker's, corporation's or licensed fictitious business name. Check the bank statement and account agreement against that requirement.

Ask the bank to confirm the full title and authorized signers. Compare those names with your current staff and broker records. Remove former staff access through the proper bank process. Keep the change record with the audit file.

Review payment permissions separately from view access. A person may need statements without needing the power to release money. Check both the bank and the software because each may grant separate rights.

Do not change historical records to make them appear correct. Document any error, the date you found it and the approved correction. Preserve the original record so a reviewer can follow the change.

For other states, make a local rule sheet before you use this checklist. Ask your broker or attorney which account types, signers and deposit practices apply.

## Which errors should I check before the audit?

Check unexplained differences, missing source records and owner balances that do not match available cash. These are useful review targets, not a ranked national list of audit findings.

The DRE explains that separate beneficiary records should agree with the control record. It then calls for comparison with the adjusted bank balance. Outstanding checks and deposits in transit need support; they do not explain every difference by default.

Trace a receipt through the bank deposit, control record and owner ledger. Then trace a payment back through the invoice and approval. Use the same effective date when you compare balances. Different dates can create a false mismatch.

Review negative beneficiary balances, duplicate invoices, returned payments and transfers between accounts. Ask what created each entry. Check whether an owner distribution left enough cash for obligations already recorded.

Do not use another owner's funds to hide a shortage. Stop the affected payment process and involve the responsible broker when you find a possible shortage. Get state-specific advice on the correction and any report that may be required.

Keep an issue log with the amount, cause, correction and reviewer. An unresolved item should remain visible. A balancing entry without a source does not resolve the underlying question.

## How long must I keep trust account records?

Trust account retention depends on the state, record type and any active claim or audit. Do not adopt a retention period from another manager's checklist.

Create a retention schedule that names the rule, record owner, storage location and destruction approval. Ask local counsel to verify the deadline and the event that starts it. Keep the cited rule with the schedule.

Separate routine retention from a hold on records. A dispute, audit request or insurer instruction may require a different process. Ask counsel before you delete records tied to an open matter.

Check whether stored exports remain readable after a software change. Keep the ledger detail and transaction identifiers, not just a summary PDF. Test the backup by opening a sample file with a person who did not create it.

Restrict access to bank details and personal data. Share records through an approved route and keep a list of what you supplied. Confirm that any redaction still lets the auditor perform the requested review.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## How can I test the audit file before handoff?

Run a practice review with someone who did not prepare the records. Ask that person to trace a receipt and payment without help from the bookkeeper. This tests whether the file explains itself.

Use this suggested review order: confirm the account list, match balances, trace source records, then inspect unresolved items. Record questions that require a phone call. Add the missing explanation to the file rather than relying on memory.

Keep a cover sheet with the contact person and scope. State which periods and accounts the package covers. List missing records plainly and give the expected next action.

Set a recurring review task so the next audit begins with maintained records, not a search through old inboxes.
