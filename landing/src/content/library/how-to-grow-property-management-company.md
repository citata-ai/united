## How does a property management company grow its door count?

A property management company grows its door count in one way: it wins more doors than it loses. Every growth plan comes down to this line:

`Net door growth = doors won − doors lost`

Buildium's 2026 State of the Property Management Industry Report surveyed more than 1,000 property managers. 75% planned to grow their portfolio in 2026, but only 55% had grown in the past year (Buildium). The gap between plan and result is often the second term: doors lost.

## Which growth sources bring the most doors?

The best source is the one with the lowest cost per new door for your company. No public study we could verify ranks sources for all companies. So track your own.

Common sources, with what each needs from you:

| Source | What it needs | Speed | Cost per door |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owner referrals | Happy owners and a clear ask | Slow, steady | Low |
| Realtor and investor referrals | Relationships, often a referral fee | Medium | Medium |
| Your website and search | Clear service pages, reviews, fee info | Slow to start | Low after setup |
| AI answers (ChatGPT, Gemini) | Pages that AI tools can read and cite | Slow to start | Low after setup |
| Lead services | A budget per lead and fast follow-up | Fast | Medium to high |
| Buying a book of business | Cash and an onboarding plan | One step | Varies by deal |

Buildium's guide also points to current owners: help them buy more properties, and their new doors come to you. Its first-100-doors guide adds speed: reply to a new owner lead within 15 minutes.

More owners now ask AI tools to suggest a manager. Our [guide on how owners choose a property manager with AI](/guides/how-owners-choose-a-property-manager-with-ai) covers what those tools read.

How to track sources:

1. Add a "source" field to every new owner record.
2. Each month, count doors won per source.
3. Divide the money spent on each source by its doors won.
4. Each quarter, move budget toward the sources with the lowest cost per door.

## How much does churn slow growth?

Churn sets a ceiling on your size. Churn is a percent of your doors, so it grows as you grow. Lead flow does not grow by itself. At some size, doors lost each month equal doors won, and growth stops.

Peter Lohmann, who runs a 700+ door company, gives an example in his 2026-01-09 newsletter: 10 new doors a month with 25% annual unit churn caps a company at 400 doors. He also warns that growth slows a lot before the ceiling. He calls 75% of the ceiling the "growth wall".

The simple form of the math:

`Growth ceiling ≈ doors won per year ÷ annual unit churn`

| Doors won per month | Annual churn | Ceiling (simple formula) | 75% "growth wall" |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 25% | 480 | 360 |
| 10 | 15% | 800 | 600 |
| 10 | 10% | 1,200 | 900 |
| 15 | 25% | 720 | 540 |

Lohmann's own model gives 400 for the first row, a bit lower than the simple formula. Either way, compare row 1 with row 3. Cutting churn from 25% to 10% raises the ceiling more than 50% more leads does (row 4).

To cut churn, record why each owner leaves. Buildium's guide also suggests dropping owners who take too much of your time for the fee they pay. A company stretched thin by bad-fit clients has less time for good ones.

## Organic growth or acquisitions?

Organic growth is cheaper per door. Acquisitions are faster. Most companies that scale fast do both.

| Factor | Organic growth | Acquisition |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | A few doors a month | Dozens or hundreds of doors at once |
| Cash needed | Marketing budget | Purchase price, often paid over time |
| Owner fit | Owners chose you | Owners chose the old company |
| Main risk | Slow lead flow | Owners leave after closing |
| Team load | Steady | A spike at onboarding |

Books of business trade often. In one week of January 2026, Lohmann's newsletter listed a 36-door company in California with an asking price of $225,000. It also listed a 278-door company in Palm Beach County, Florida, at $1.2 million. Asking prices are not sale prices, but they show the market exists for small books.

Scale changes the playbook. In the same issue, Lohmann says Evernest grew to more than 20,000 doors and bought 17 companies in six months. Few companies need that pace. The lesson for a smaller company: plan onboarding before you buy, because the owners did not choose you.

## What growth rate is realistic?

A realistic growth rate is the one your own numbers support. We found no reliable public benchmark for annual door growth.

Build your target from four inputs:

1. **Leads per month** from all sources.
2. **Close rate:** leads that sign.
3. **Doors per new owner,** on average.
4. **Annual churn.**

Worked example for a 200-door company:

- 20 owner leads a month × 20% close rate = 4 new owners.
- 4 owners × 1.5 doors each = 6 doors won a month, 72 a year.
- 15% churn on 200 doors = 30 doors lost a year.
- Net growth: 72 − 30 = 42 doors, or 21% in a year.

Now test the levers. Raise the close rate to 25% and you win 90 doors a year: net growth becomes 60 doors, or 30%. Cut churn to 10% and you lose 20 doors: net growth becomes 52, or 26%. Do both and you get 70 doors, or 35%.

Growth also needs capacity. Buildium's report found the share of companies that use AI rose from 20% to 58% in one year. Software and staff must keep pace with the new doors, or service drops and churn rises.
