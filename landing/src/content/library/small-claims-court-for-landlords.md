## What can a landlord claim in small claims court?

A landlord can use small claims for eligible money claims within the court's authority. Check the claim type, amount, parties and location before filing.

California Courts lists limits of $12,500 for individuals and $6,250 for businesses. The ownership entity can therefore change the proper route. Do not assume that a personally managed property makes the plaintiff an individual. [Read the California process guide](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/small-claims).

Texas uses justice courts for small claims under its own rules. The Texas Justice Court Training Center explains the filing process and directs readers to court forms and requirements. Use the local court's current instructions. [Read the Texas filing guide](https://www.tjctc.org/srl/small-claim-plaintiff.html).

Separate recovery of money from recovery of possession. Ask the court or counsel which case handles each request. A money judgment is not a substitute for a lawful eviction process.

Review the deadline to bring the claim before spending time on forms. Different claim types can have different limits. Avoid changing the claim label merely to fit a deadline or court limit.

## What evidence should I bring?

Bring evidence that connects each requested amount to the agreement, event and actual loss. Build the case so the judge can follow it without reading your whole inbox.

Start with the lease and changes. Add the tenant ledger, payment records and relevant notices. For damages, show condition before and after, repair details and why you attribute the loss to the defendant.

| Claim item | Supporting proof | Common gap |
|---|---|---|
| Unpaid rent | Lease, ledger and receipts | Missing payment credit |
| Repair cost | Condition photos and invoice | No proof of cause |
| Cleaning | Condition comparison and scope | Charge based on routine turnover |
| Allowed fee | Lawful lease term and calculation | Unreviewed or duplicate fee |
| Filing expense | Court receipt and local authority | Assumed recovery |
| Deposit credit | Deposit statement and payment record | Same loss claimed twice |

The table is a preparation tool, not a list of automatically recoverable charges. Local law controls what you may claim.

Label each exhibit with a short title and date. Keep the original and a readable copy in the format the court accepts. A clear index helps you find proof during the hearing.

## How do I calculate the claim?

Calculate the net supported loss after payments and credits. Keep estimates, paid costs and disputed items clearly marked.

Reconcile the rent ledger first. Check concessions, returned payments, later receipts and any rent recovered from a replacement tenant. Ask counsel how the governing law treats those credits.

For property damage, distinguish restoration from improvement. Explain the repair scope and condition history. A receipt for a new item does not alone prove the tenant owes its full price.

Write a short claim schedule. Put the requested amount beside the supporting exhibit and legal basis. Remove any item you cannot explain from the file.

Review the deposit handling before filing. If you already retained money for a cost, show that credit. Do not claim the same cost again without accounting for funds held.

Consider settlement using the same schedule. A tenant can respond more usefully to a clear list than to a demand for an unexplained balance.

## How do I serve the tenant?

Serve the correct defendant through a method the court permits. Filing a form and sending a casual message are different steps.

San Mateo Superior Court explains that service gives formal notice and that each defendant must receive the claim through the required process. Its plaintiff guidance also stresses using the correct legal name. [Read the service guide](https://sanmateo.courts.ca.gov/self-help/small-claims-self-help/serve-your-claim).

Check the address and defendant identity before hiring a server. A former tenant's forwarding address may be old. Keep the source of the address and any returned service records.

Ask the clerk or self-help center about deadlines, authorized servers and proof of service. Follow your court's rule rather than copying another state's form.

If service fails, use the allowed next procedure. Do not invent a substitute method. A flawed service record can delay the case or undermine the judgment.

Keep service proof with the hearing papers. Confirm that the court has the required filing rather than assuming the server handled every step.

## How do I collect after winning?

Collection takes a separate review of payment, assets and lawful enforcement tools. A judgment confirms a legal result; it does not guarantee available funds.

California Courts separates the trial from the steps after judgment. Before you file, review the local collection process and costs as well as the hearing itself.

Ask whether voluntary payment or an agreed payment plan is practical. Put any agreement in writing and keep the ledger current. Follow local rules for acknowledging payment and satisfaction.

Do not seize belongings or withdraw money based on the judgment alone. Use authorized enforcement procedures and check exemptions. Ask counsel about bankruptcy or a contested enforcement step.

Use this recommended final review before filing:

- **Confirm the plaintiff.** Match ownership and the party with the claim.
- **Check the court.** Review amount, location and claim type.
- **Prove the loss.** Reconcile the ledger and attach support.
- **Plan service.** Verify the defendant and allowed method.
- **Assess collection.** Include time, costs and likely recovery in the decision.
