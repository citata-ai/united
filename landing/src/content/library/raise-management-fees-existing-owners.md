## Why raise fees for existing owners at all?

Raising fees on existing owners is the fastest way to raise profit. The new revenue needs no new staff, no new marketing and no new doors.

The 2022 NARPM Financial Performance Guide makes the case with data from 153 companies:

- **Price beats volume.** At the fastest-improving companies, 80.9% of the profit gain came from higher profit per unit. Only 15.8% came from more units.
- **Small price, big profit.** The study says a 10% rise in revenue per unit "can easily lead to a 100% increase in profitability".
- **No churn penalty seen.** Churn was 18.29% to 20.91% across all 4 pricing brackets in 2021. The study found no meaningful link between pricing and churn.

Buildium published a post by Loretta Morgan with the same view: compete on service, not on fees. She writes that her clients "don't shop on fees". They want a high level of service.

## How do I know the raise is worth the risk?

The raise is worth the risk when the doors you could lose are fewer than the break-even number. Do this math before you write a letter.

The break-even rule:

> Doors you can lose = raise ÷ (1 + raise)

| Fee raise | Revenue-neutral if you lose up to | On 200 doors |
|---|---|---|
| 5% | 4.8% of doors | 9 doors |
| 10% | 9.1% | 18 doors |
| 15% | 13.0% | 26 doors |
| 20% | 16.7% | 33 doors |

A worked example. You manage 200 doors at $150 a month in fees per door. That is $360,000 a year. You raise to $165, a 10% raise. If every owner stays, you earn $396,000, or $36,000 more. You must lose 19 doors before revenue falls below where you started: 181 × $165 × 12 = $358,380.

The real gain is larger. Lost doors also free staff time. Use Rentvine's value-of-a-door formula to see what each owner is worth: yearly revenue per unit × years the owner stays × units per owner.

## How much notice should I give?

Give the notice your management agreement requires, and then some. The agreement is the rulebook. It may let you change fees with written notice, only at renewal, or only with the owner's signature.

Check each agreement for these 4 clauses:

1. **Fee change clause.** Can you change fees by notice, or does the owner need to sign?
2. **Notice period.** How many days of written notice? How must you send it?
3. **Term and renewal date.** Does the term auto-renew? On what date?
4. **Termination clause.** How much notice must the owner give to leave? Is there a cancel fee?

A good rule: give at least as much notice as the owner needs to cancel. If owners need 30 days to leave, give 60 to 90 days on a fee change. That gives each owner time to ask questions and decide.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney. State license law can also set rules for agreement changes.

## Should I grandfather old owners?

Grandfather old owners for a set time only. A clear end date keeps goodwill and still moves every door to the new price.

| Option | Good for | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| No grandfathering, all at once | Small raises, simple fee lists | More calls in one month |
| Grandfather for 6 to 12 months | Bigger raises, long-time owners | You wait for the revenue |
| Grandfather until renewal date | Agreements with fixed terms | Rollout takes up to a year |
| Grandfather forever | Almost never | Two price lists and low-margin doors forever |

Raise prices for new owners first. That shows you the market accepts the new price. Then move existing owners.

## What should the letter say?

The letter should say what changes, when, and why, in under one page. Lead with the service, not the apology.

A template you can adapt:

> Subject: Changes to your management agreement on [date]
>
> Dear [owner name],
>
> Thank you for trusting us with [property address] since [year].
>
> On [effective date], our monthly management fee will change from [old fee] to [new fee]. All other fees stay the same.
>
> In the last 12 months we [one or two facts: average days to lease, rent collected on time, number of work orders closed, a new owner portal or inspection report].
>
> The new fee lets us keep that level of service as our costs rise. [Name one concrete cost or one new service.]
>
> Your current agreement lets either of us [make this change with X days of notice / end the agreement with X days of notice]. If you have any questions, call me at [phone]. I will be glad to walk you through your account.
>
> [Your name], [title]

Send it by the method your agreement names. Then call your top 20% of owners by revenue before they read it.

## Should I raise fees for all owners at once?

No. Raise fees in groups, and start with the owners least likely to leave. A staged rollout limits the damage if your price is wrong.

A 4-step rollout:

1. **New owners first.** Put the new fee in every new agreement. Run it for 30 to 60 days.
2. **Group by renewal date.** Send letters to each group 60 to 90 days before its renewal.
3. **Watch the first group.** Count calls, objections and cancel notices. Adjust the letter, not the price.
4. **Review your lowest-margin doors last.** Some owners will leave. If a door costs more to serve than it pays, that may be fine.

The NARPM study found the average company lost 19.5% of units in 2021. The top 25% lost 9.6%. Track your churn for 6 months after the change, and compare it with the 12 months before.
