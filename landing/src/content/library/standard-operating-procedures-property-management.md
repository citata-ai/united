## Why write SOPs at all?

SOPs let your team do the same task the same way, whoever is at the desk. That protects tenants, owners and your license.

Buildium's guide on written policies says good policies cover five areas:

| Area | What it holds | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Legislation | Legal steps written into the process | Written notices on the forms the law prescribes |
| Agency culture | Your own values and points of difference | How you answer an angry owner |
| Agency standards | Service and operating standards | Reply to owner email within 1 business day |
| Processes | How each task is done, so you can measure it | Move-in, arrears, renewals |
| Code of conduct | Codes you must follow | A franchise code or a licensing body code |

The author warns against copying a competitor's culture. Your SOPs should describe how your firm works, not how someone else's does.

## Which processes need an SOP first?

The first SOPs go to the processes where a mistake costs money, breaks a law or loses an owner. Rank your list by risk, not by how often the task happens.

A good first 10:

1. **Security deposit and move-out.** State deadlines and itemized-deduction rules apply.
2. **Rent arrears.** Notices must follow state forms and timelines.
3. **Applications and screening.** Same criteria for every applicant, in writing.
4. **Move-in and condition report.**
5. **Maintenance intake and emergencies.**
6. **Owner payouts and month-end close.**
7. **Lease renewals and rent changes.**
8. **New owner onboarding.**
9. **Vacancy marketing and showings.**
10. **Owner termination and file handover.**

Professional codes give you a floor for some of these. The NARPM Code of Ethics, for example, says members shall:

- offer all prospective tenants a written application (Standard 4-1);
- give all tenants a copy of the signed rental agreement with all addendums (Standard 4-2);
- give tenants a chance to complete a written condition report "within 7 days of moving in or longer if required by federal, state or local laws" (Standard 4-3).

Write each of those duties as a step in the right SOP, with the person who does it.

## What format works: text, video or checklist?

The best format is a one-page checklist as the master copy, with a short video for software steps. A long text manual is hard to scan and goes stale first.

| Format | Best for | Weak point |
|---|---|---|
| Checklist | Tasks with clear steps and deadlines | Does not show the software clicks |
| Screen video, 2 to 5 minutes | Software tasks: posting a payment, running a report | Goes stale when the software changes |
| Text page | Judgment calls and policy reasons | Staff skip long pages |
| Flowchart | Tasks with many branches, such as emergencies | Hard to edit |

Buildium's arrears example shows how a checklist reads: an SMS at day 3, another at day 5, a phone call at day 7, a breach step at day 8, then a notice to leave if rent is still unpaid. Each step has a day and an action. Your own timeline must follow your state's notice law.

## A one-page SOP template

Copy this template for each process. Keep it to one page.

```text
SOP: [name of process]
Owner: [role, not a person's name]
Trigger: [the event that starts it]
Deadline: [hard dates, with the law or contract that sets them]
Tools: [software, forms, templates]

Steps:
1. [Verb first. One action. Who does it.]
2. ...
3. ...

Records to keep: [what goes in the file, and where]
Done when: [the check that closes the task]
Escalate to: [role] if [condition]
Law or contract source: [statute, lease clause, agreement clause]
Last review: [YYYY-MM-DD]  Next review: [YYYY-MM-DD]
```

Write each step with a verb first: "Send", "Post", "Call", "Check". One action per step. If a step needs a judgment call, say who decides.

## Where should SOPs live?

SOPs should live in one searchable place that every staff member can open from a phone. Two copies of the same SOP will drift apart, and staff will follow the wrong one.

Options that teams commonly use:

- **Your property management software.** Some platforms hold task templates and checklists inside the workflow.
- **A shared drive.** Google Drive or SharePoint, with one folder per department.
- **A wiki tool.** Notion or Confluence, with links between related SOPs.
- **A training tool.** Trainual or similar, with sign-off when a staff member reads a new version.

Pick the place your team already opens every day. Link each SOP from the task it covers, such as the move-out task in your software.

## How do I keep SOPs current?

Keep SOPs current with a named owner, a review date and three event triggers. An SOP without an owner is out of date within a year.

Review an SOP at once when:

1. **A law changes.** For example, a new state deposit deadline or a new notice form.
2. **Your software changes.** New screens break the old video.
3. **A mistake happens.** Find the step that failed and fix the SOP the same week.

Then review every SOP on a fixed cycle. A 12-month cycle works for most. Use 6 months for deposits, arrears and screening, because those carry the most legal risk.

A quick health check each quarter:

- [ ] Every SOP has an owner and a review date in the future.
- [ ] No SOP has a step that names a person who left.
- [ ] Every legal deadline links to its source.
- [ ] New staff can do the task from the SOP alone.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.
