## What does an HOA manager do?

An HOA manager carries out the association work defined in the association's management contract. Your individual rental goals do not set that contract's scope. Ask for the association contact plan rather than assuming the manager serves as your leasing agent.

Start with this role map. The tasks are examples to check against the actual agreements, not a national rule for every building.

| Task | Association side | Rental management side |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shared grounds | Coordinate work within HOA scope | Report effects on the rental |
| Association notices | Send or administer board notices | Forward and track owner action |
| Unit rent | Usually outside HOA service scope | Collect if the rental agreement authorizes it |
| Tenant repair request | Address shared components within scope | Receive, assess and coordinate the request |
| Rental lease | Check association rental procedures | Prepare or administer within agreed authority |
| Owner reporting | Association budget and records | Rental income, bills and owner funds |

Confirm who makes decisions, not just who answers email. Ask which issues require board approval and who can authorize emergency work. Save that answer with your building records.

An association manager may help administer budgets, vendors and rules, depending on the contract. Read the scope before expecting direct help with your unit. The manager may need board approval for work that your rental manager cannot authorize.

## What does a rental property manager do?

A rental property manager performs the leasing, rent, repair and reporting services stated in your agreement. California DRE's Property Management chapter describes these core functions. It also recommends a written contract that sets out duties, fees and authority.

Use that guidance as a contract review prompt. Ask who advertises the home, handles applications and maintains the lease file. Confirm who receives rent, tracks unpaid balances and sends your statement. Then review maintenance approval and emergency response.

Ask the rental manager to identify the limits of its service. Does the manager attend HOA hearings, request project approval or only forward notices? Who pays association assessments? Does the manager monitor a separate HOA portal? Put each answer in writing.

Keep association funds and your rental figures distinct in your own review. An HOA budget does not show your unit's rent collection. A rental statement does not show whether the association has funds for shared work. Request the correct record for each question.

If one firm offers both services, request a named contact for each role. Ask how it handles a conflict between association instructions and an individual owner's request. Separate scope and clear records still matter when the company name is the same.

Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) for rental fees. Add association dues and special charges separately to your property budget. A management quote does not replace the association's own charges.

## Who handles a leak from a common area?

A common-area leak may require action from both managers while responsibility is reviewed. Stop active damage safely, report the facts and preserve evidence. Do not wait for a final cost decision before asking for urgent help.

California Civil Code section 4775 generally assigns common-area repair, replacement and maintenance to the association unless the declaration provides otherwise. It separately addresses an owner's separate interest and exclusive-use common areas. The component's classification matters.

Florida's condominium statute section 718.113 generally assigns common-element maintenance to the association, subject to the declaration's allocation for certain limited common elements. That example concerns condominiums. Do not apply it to every Florida HOA or to another state.

Collect the declaration and the relevant building plan. Ask a qualified contractor to identify the leak's source and affected parts. A stain inside the unit does not by itself establish which component failed.

Separate the repair questions. Who stops the leak? Who dries the unit? Who replaces interior finishes? Who handles the tenant's request while work continues? Assign a contact and written next action for each issue.

Insurance adds another review. California's Department of Insurance explains that association and unit-owner coverage can address different property. Ask both carriers how their actual policies apply. Do not assume the association policy pays every loss inside the unit.

Keep photos, dates, access records and invoices. Ask the managers to share findings with you, rather than sending conflicting instructions to contractors. Have counsel review any dispute about duties, reimbursement or tenant remedies.

## Who should tenants call?

Tenants should receive a contact plan that matches the building and your management contract. Make the rental manager the clear route for rental service requests where that is the agreed service. Identify the association route for shared facilities separately.

Give the tenant the normal maintenance channel and the after-hours contact. Explain what information to include: location, active damage, photos and safe access details. Tell the tenant how to report a failed response through the backup contact.

Do not ask a tenant to diagnose whether a pipe is a common element. The tenant reports the problem; the managers review the component and coordinate the response. Keep that division clear in the welcome materials.

Create a shared-area issue file when more than one party is involved. Include the initial report, HOA case reference, contractor appointment and next update. Ask the rental manager to keep the tenant informed even when the association controls the work.

Review the contact plan after an actual incident. Check whether the right person received the report and whether the next action was clear. Update any phone number, portal instruction or access detail that failed.

For more ownership questions, browse the [guides](/guides). Then ask both managers for their written service scopes and repair contacts. Keep those documents where you and your tenant can find them when a problem starts.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.
