## What is a vacancy rate?

A vacancy rate is the share of rental units that sit empty, or the share of rent lost to empty units, in a period. Owners use it to judge a market, set a budget and compare managers.

There are 2 common ways to measure it:

| Type | What it measures | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Physical vacancy | Units with no tenant | Vacant unit-days ÷ total unit-days × 100 |
| Economic vacancy | Rent you did not collect | (Potential rent − rent collected) ÷ potential rent × 100 |

Economic vacancy is usually higher, because it also counts unpaid rent, concessions and units used as models or offices. For an owner's budget, economic vacancy is the number that matters.

## How do you calculate vacancy rate for a portfolio?

You calculate vacancy rate for a portfolio with unit-days, so that a unit empty for 10 days counts less than one empty for 60.

Worked example: you manage 100 units for a full year.

- Total unit-days: 100 × 365 = **36,500**.
- Vacancies during the year: 30 turns averaging 21 days, plus 1 unit empty 120 days for a renovation. Vacant days: 30 × 21 + 120 = **750**.
- Physical vacancy rate: 750 ÷ 36,500 × 100 = **2.1%**.

Now economic vacancy. Average rent is $1,800, so potential rent is $1,800 × 12 × 100 = $2,160,000. You collected $2,101,600, after about $44,400 of rent lost to vacant days ($1,800 × 12 ÷ 365 × 750) and $14,000 of concessions and unpaid rent.

- Economic vacancy: ($2,160,000 − $2,101,600) ÷ $2,160,000 × 100 = **2.7%**.

Watch for: a point-in-time count. "3 of 100 units are vacant today" is a 3% snapshot, not a yearly rate. Use unit-days for reports.

## What is a normal vacancy rate?

The US rental vacancy rate was 7.3% in the second quarter of 2026. The Census Bureau publishes it in its Housing Vacancies and Homeownership survey (HVS), and FRED carries the series.

| Quarter | US rental vacancy rate |
|---|---|
| Q3 2009 (highest in the FRED series) | 11.1% |
| Q4 2019 | 6.4% |
| Q4 2021 | 5.6% |
| Q2 2024 | 6.6% |
| Q2 2025 | 7.0% |
| Q1 2026 | 7.3% |
| Q2 2026 | 7.3% |

The lowest value in the series is 5.0%, seen in several quarters from 1978 to 1981. The rate has risen from 7.0% to 7.3% over the year to Q2 2026.

Caution: the national rate is not your market. City and neighborhood rates can be far above or below it, so use local data for decisions.

For a professionally managed comparison: Camden Property Trust reported 95.2% occupancy in its same-property apartment portfolio in Q4 2025. That is a 4.8% vacancy rate.

## How does vacancy rate differ from occupancy rate?

Vacancy rate and occupancy rate are 2 sides of the same number. Occupancy is the share of units, or rent, that is filled. Vacancy is the share that is empty.

**Occupancy rate = 100% − vacancy rate**

So 95.2% occupancy is 4.8% vacancy. The 2 must use the same method. Do not compare a physical occupancy rate from one manager with an economic vacancy rate from another.

## How is vacancy rate used in a pro forma?

In a pro forma (a forward budget for a property), the vacancy rate cuts potential rent down to the rent you expect to collect. It is the first line under gross rent.

Worked example for one $1,800 a month unit:

| Line | Calculation | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Potential gross rent | $1,800 × 12 | $21,600 |
| Vacancy and credit loss at 5% | $21,600 × 5% | −$1,080 |
| Effective gross income | | $20,520 |
| Management fee at 8.49% of collected rent | $20,520 × 8.49% | −$1,742 |

The 8.49% fee is the average that iPropertyManagement found in its fee study, updated September 23, 2026.

Now test it. At 8% vacancy, effective income falls to $19,872, a loss of $648 a year against the 5% case. If the deal still works at the higher rate, it is more robust.

What to do: run every pro forma at 3 vacancy rates. Use your own history for the base case, the national Census rate as a check, and a stress case 3 to 5 points higher.

## How do property managers lower vacancy?

Property managers lower vacancy by cutting the number of turns and the days each turn takes. Both parts count: vacancy days = turns × days per turn.

1. **Keep tenants longer.** Fewer move-outs means fewer vacant days.
2. **Start renewals 90 to 120 days early.** If the tenant will leave, you know in time to market.
3. **Shorten make-ready.** Book vendors before move-out day and run trades in order.
4. **Price with data.** An overpriced unit sits; a $50 cut can cost less than 1 extra vacant week.
5. **Market before move-out,** where the lease allows showings with proper notice.
6. **Answer leads fast.** Reply to inquiries the same day and offer self-showing where it fits.

Worked check on point 4: on an $1,800 unit, 1 vacant week costs about $414 ($1,800 × 12 ÷ 365 × 7). A $50 a month price cut over a 12-month lease costs $600. If the cut fills the unit 2 weeks sooner, it saves about $828 in lost rent, for a net gain of about $228.
