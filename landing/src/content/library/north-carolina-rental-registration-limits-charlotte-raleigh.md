## What does NC G.S. 160D-1207 forbid cities to do?

N.C. G.S. 160D-1207(c) lists 5 things no North Carolina city or county may do to rental owners. Charlotte, Raleigh and every other local government fall under it. "In no event may a local government" do any of these:

| Clause | What a city may not do |
|---|---|
| (c)(i) | Require a permit or permission to lease or rent a home, or require owners to register rental property, except for problem properties |
| (c)(ii) | Make an owner join a government program to get a certificate of occupancy |
| (c)(iii) | Charge a special fee or tax on rental property that other property does not pay, with a narrow exception |
| (c)(iv) | Make a violation of a rental registration ordinance a crime |
| (c)(v) | Require an inspection before the owner gets city utility service |

The section cites session laws 2019-111, 2020-3 and 2020-25 as its sources.

What this means for you: in Charlotte or Raleigh, you can rent a house or apartment without a city rental permit or registry entry, as long as the property stays clear of the problem-property tests below.

## When can a North Carolina city make an owner register?

A North Carolina city can make an owner register only an individual problem property. G.S. 160D-1207(c)(i) allows registration for a property with:

1. **More than 4 verified violations** in a rolling 12-month period, or
2. **2 or more verified violations** in a rolling 30-day period, or
3. **A place in the top 10%** of properties with crime or disorder problems, under a local ordinance.

A "verified violation" has a specific meaning. All violations found in 1 unit during a 72-hour period count as 1. A violation counts only if the owner does not fix it within 21 days of written notice. If the same violation happens more than 2 times in 12 months, the owner loses the option to fix it. For a tenant-caused violation, filing a summary ejectment action within 30 days counts as a fix.

Fees for problem properties: a city may charge a special fee only for such a property, and the fee may not exceed $500 in any 12-month period with verified violations.

Worked example: an inspector finds 6 code items in 1 unit on Monday and 2 more on Wednesday. That is 1 verified violation (1 unit, 72 hours), and only if the owner does not fix it within 21 days. To reach registration, the property needs 5 such violations in 12 months, or 2 in 30 days.

If the city flags a property for crime, it must tell the landlord which incidents count, and police must help with an eviction of a tenant charged with a crime. Without that help, the tenant's conduct does not count. An owner can appeal any action under the section to the housing appeals board or another named board.

## When can a North Carolina city inspect a rental?

A North Carolina city can inspect a rental home only with reasonable cause, under G.S. 160D-1207(a). The section defines reasonable cause as any of these:

- **History.** The owner has more than 2 verified violations in 12 months.
- **Complaint.** Someone complains or asks for an inspection.
- **Knowledge.** The inspection department knows of an unsafe condition.
- **Visible problems.** Violations are visible from outside the property.

The city must not treat single-family and multifamily buildings differently, or owner-occupied and rented homes differently.

One exception is a targeted area. Under G.S. 160D-1207(b), a city may run periodic inspections in blighted areas it designates. All targeted areas together may not exceed 1 square mile or 5% of the city's area, whichever is greater. The city must notify owners and residents, hold a legislative hearing and plan help for low-income owners.

## Does Raleigh have a residential rental registration?

Raleigh cannot require registration of ordinary long-term rentals, because G.S. 160D-1207 bars it. Raleigh does regulate short-term rentals through zoning. The City of Raleigh says:

- **Zones.** Short-term rentals are a Limited Use in the R-1, R-2, R-4, R-6, R-10, RX, OX, NX, CX and DX districts.
- **Zoning permit.** Every operator must first get a zoning permit. The fee is in the city's Development Fee Guide.
- **Post the number.** Show the permit number on all ads and at the property.
- **Guest list.** Keep a list of all guests for 3 years.
- **Multi-unit cap.** In a multi-unit building, no more than 25% of units, or 2 units, whichever is greater, may be short-term rentals.
- **No events.** In residential zones, guests may not hold special events.

For long-term rentals in both cities, follow state law. HUD's FY2027 2-bedroom fair market rent is $1,785 in the Charlotte metro (up 5.9%) and $1,689 in the Raleigh metro (down 3.5%). See the [Charlotte](/guides/section-8-rent-limits/charlotte-fair-market-rent-2027) and [Raleigh](/guides/section-8-rent-limits/raleigh-fair-market-rent-2027) fair market rent pages.

## Can North Carolina cities cap rent?

No. N.C. G.S. 42-14.1(a) bars any county or city from an ordinance "which regulates the amount of rent to be charged" for privately owned residential or commercial rental property. A 2024 amendment (S.L. 2024-47) added subsection (b). It bars local rules that stop an owner from refusing to rent because the tenant's rent comes in part from a federal housing assistance program.

Cities keep some tools. They can regulate rent on their own property, sign rent agreements for subsidized homes, limit rent on homes built with Community Development Block Grant funds, and set terms for owners that take city money or incentives.

State rules that do apply to every owner:

| Rule | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Deposit, week-to-week | Up to 2 weeks' rent (G.S. 42-51) |
| Deposit, month-to-month | Up to 1.5 months' rent |
| Deposit, longer terms | Up to 2 months' rent |
| Inspections | Only with reasonable cause or in a targeted area (G.S. 160D-1207) |
