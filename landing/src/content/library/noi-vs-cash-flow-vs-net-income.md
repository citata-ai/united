## What goes into rental NOI?

NOI, or net operating income, measures property operations before financing and income tax. Start with rent and other operating income. Subtract operating costs such as insurance, property tax, management, utilities, and routine repairs.

Use a written definition in every spreadsheet. Reserve treatment can differ between reports and lender calculations. For this guide, NOI excludes reserve transfers, capital purchases, debt service, depreciation, and income tax. Show those items separately.

An asking rent is not collected income. A useful forecast accounts for vacancy and unpaid rent before calculating NOI. A report of actual results should identify what you received and what remains unpaid.

Do not confuse a tax expense list with an operating statement. IRS Publication 527 allows certain rental deductions, including interest and depreciation. Those deductions do not all belong in the operating measure defined here.

Keep the property income and expense period consistent. A monthly rent figure beside annual insurance produces a meaningless result. Convert each line to the same period and save the original bill.

## Why is the mortgage outside NOI?

NOI excludes financing so you can compare the property under different loan structures. The same home can have different cash flow for a cash buyer and a heavily financed buyer. Its operations do not change just because the loan changes.

Debt service means principal and interest payments. Both use cash. IRS Publication 527 discusses mortgage interest as a possible rental expense, but principal repayment is not the same kind of deduction.

Read the loan statement rather than treating the full payment as interest. If the payment includes escrow, separate taxes and insurance. Those expenses already belong in the operating costs when you calculate NOI.

This split also helps you evaluate refinancing. A lower payment can improve cash flow without improving rent collection or reducing repairs. Record the loan effect separately from the property effect.

If your manager reports a monthly owner distribution, ask how the statement treats loan payments and reserves. A distribution is a transfer to you. Its amount may reflect earlier cash, held funds, or unpaid bills.

## Can you show all the measures on one property?

A worked example shows why the same property can produce different results. Every amount below is hypothetical. The example is a teaching calculation, not a return estimate or a tax filing instruction.

Assume collected annual rent of $30,000. Assume operating expenses of $12,000, including property tax, insurance, management, and routine repairs. No personal use, purchase costs, or unusual transactions are included.

| Measure | Hypothetical calculation | Annual result |
|---|---|---:|
| NOI under this guide's definition | $30,000 rent minus $12,000 operating costs | $18,000 |
| Cash after loan payments | $18,000 minus $10,000 interest and $4,000 principal | $4,000 |
| Cash available after reserve transfer | $4,000 minus $2,000 moved to reserves | $2,000 |
| Tax rental result before loss limits | $30,000 minus $12,000 costs, $10,000 interest, $9,000 depreciation | -$1,000 |

The cash result includes the full $14,000 debt payment. The tax calculation includes interest but excludes principal. The assumed $9,000 depreciation reduces tax income without reducing current cash.

The reserve transfer keeps $2,000 available for future work. It does not automatically become a current tax deduction. A later repair or improvement needs its own tax classification under Publication 527.

## Why can taxable income be lower than cash flow?

Depreciation can lower tax income without a matching current cash payment. IRS Publication 527 explains how eligible rental costs are recovered over time. The land itself is not depreciable.

A loan principal payment has the opposite effect. It reduces cash but generally does not reduce rental tax income. That is why subtracting the whole mortgage payment on a tax worksheet can overstate deductions.

Capital work also creates a mismatch. Paying for an improvement uses cash now, while tax recovery may occur over time. Ask a tax professional to classify work rather than calling every contractor bill a repair.

A calculated rental loss is not always immediately deductible. IRS Publication 925 describes passive activity and at-risk limits. Keep the calculation of rental profit separate from the question of how much loss your return can use.

If you rent part of your home or use the property personally, expense allocation can change the result. Publication 527 covers mixed use. Do not apply the example as though every home were a full-time rental.

## How should an owner use each figure?

Use NOI to assess operations, cash flow to plan liquidity, and tax income to prepare a return. Each figure answers a different question. Keep them visible together rather than choosing the most favorable result.

A strong operating result can still leave little cash after debt payments. Positive cash can still conceal deferred repairs. A tax loss can still occur in a year when you transfer money to yourself.

Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) for quoted management costs, then place those costs in the correct worksheet line. Add leasing and other contract charges separately when needed.

Before you rely on a report, complete this review:

- **Match rent records** to bank receipts and tenant ledgers.
- **Separate loan parts** using the lender's statement.
- **List capital work** outside routine operating repairs.
- **Track reserve cash** without treating transfers as paid expenses.
- **Reconcile tax records** with your accountant's asset and loss schedules.
