## Which St. Paul rentals are under the 3 percent cap?

Most St. Paul rentals in buildings first occupied on or before December 31, 2004 are under the 3% cap. Section 193A.04 of the St. Paul Legislative Code says no landlord may charge a rent increase of more than 3% of the existing monthly rent within a 12-month period. The only ways around it are the exceptions in sections 193A.06 and 193A.08.

The cap follows the unit, not the tenant. Section 193A.05(a) says the limit still applies after a change of tenancy when the vacancy is not for just cause.

| Unit type | Under the 3% cap? | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Building with first certificate of occupancy on or before 2004-12-31 | Yes | 193A.04, 193A.08 |
| Building with first certificate of occupancy after 2004-12-31 | No | 193A.08(a)(3)(a) |
| Conversion from non-residential use, first residential certificate after 2004-12-31 | No | 193A.08(a)(3)(b) |
| Affordable housing restricted by deed or a government agreement | No | 193A.08(a)(2) |
| Amounts a provider is reimbursed under the Housing Support Act (Minn. Stats. ch. 256I) | No | 193A.08(a)(1) |

You cannot look up the certificate date yourself. The Department of Safety and Inspections (DSI) keeps those records, and you can ask DSI for them. DSI can also send a letter that confirms an exemption.

Watch for: an exempt unit still has one duty. Section 193A.08(b) says you must tell prospective tenants that the unit is not subject to the rent limit before the lease is final.

## How long is new construction exempt?

New construction in St. Paul is now exempt with no time limit. Before the May 2025 amendment, new construction was exempt for 20 years, per the Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis. The current text of section 193A.08(a)(3) exempts any residential rental property that got its first building certificate of occupancy after December 31, 2004.

The cut-off date is fixed. So a building first occupied in 2004 stays under the cap, and a building first occupied in 2005 does not. The amendment took effect 30 days after passage, approval and publication. DSI says the changes took effect on June 13, 2025.

This matters when you buy. Ask the seller for the DSI certificate of occupancy record before you close. The date decides whether your rent can follow the market.

## How does an owner ask for an exception above 3 percent?

A St. Paul owner asks DSI for an exception, and the size of the increase picks the path. DSI lists four levels.

| Path | Increase | City approval |
|---|---|---|
| Standard allowable increase | Up to 3% | Not needed |
| Self-certification exception | 3% to 8% (evaluated each year) | Application to DSI |
| Just cause vacancy exception | Up to CPI + 8% | Show DSI the vacancy qualifies |
| Staff determination exception | Above 8%, with justification | DSI review |

Steps for a request above 3%:

1. **Check habitability.** DSI considers an exception only for safe and habitable units.
2. **Fill the MNOI worksheet.** DSI strongly encourages it. MNOI (maintenance of net operating income) compares your net operating income with a base year, adjusted by 100% of the CPI change.
3. **Submit the form** "Request for Rent Increase Greater than 3% - Landlord Application" from the Rules and Processes (2025) page.
4. **Wait for the decision.** DSI notifies tenants when you apply and when it decides.
5. **Wait 45 days.** No increase over 3% may take effect until at least 45 days after the city's determination.

Either side can appeal in writing to the City Clerk within 45 calendar days of the written notice.

A worked example, at the HUD FY2027 2-bedroom fair market rent of $1,734 for the Minneapolis-St. Paul area:

- 3% standard increase: $1,734 x 0.03 = $52.02. New rent $1,786.02.
- 8% self-certification ceiling: $1,734 x 0.08 = $138.72. New rent $1,872.72.

For all bedroom sizes in the metro, see the [Minneapolis-St. Paul fair market rent 2027 page](/guides/section-8-rent-limits/minneapolis-st-paul-fair-market-rent-2027).

## What is a just cause vacancy in St. Paul?

A just cause vacancy lets a St. Paul owner reset rent by up to 8% plus CPI when a tenant leaves for a listed reason. Section 193A.05(b) lists the reasons. Examples:

- **Non-payment.** The tenant does not cure after a non-payment notice, and the landlord ends the tenancy at lease end instead of filing an eviction.
- **Repeated late payment.** At least 3 late payments in 12 months, after a written warning.
- **Material non-compliance.** The tenant keeps breaking a material lease term after a written notice to stop.
- **Substantial damage** that the tenant caused or allowed.

You must show DSI that the vacancy qualifies before you raise the rent. Keep the notices, dates and payment records.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## Does Minneapolis have rent control?

Minneapolis is a separate city, and St. Paul's ordinance does not apply there. Minnesota Statutes 471.9996 bars a city from controlling rents on private residential property. The one exception: the rule is approved in a general election.

St. Paul's Chapter 193A dates from an ordinance of November 2, 2021, the date of a general election. We did not find a Minneapolis rent cap in force on the city's website. Check with the City of Minneapolis before you set rents there.

Checklist for a St. Paul owner:

- [ ] Get the first certificate of occupancy date from DSI for each building.
- [ ] Mark exempt units and add the "not subject to rent limitations" notice to your lease packet.
- [ ] Track each tenant's last increase date. The 3% limit runs per 12 months.
- [ ] Keep move-out records that show a just cause vacancy.
- [ ] File an exception request early, and plan for the 45-day wait.
