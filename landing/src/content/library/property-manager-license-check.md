## Which states require a broker license for management?

State law and the manager's duties decide the license requirement. Do not use a broad online state list as your final check. Ask the regulator for the state where the property sits.

California DRE's landlord guide says the manager needs a valid, active license to collect rent and manage property. It notes an exception for resident managers. Texas TREC says paid showing or leasing needs a license. TREC also identifies control over acceptance or deposit of single-family rent as licensed work.

Those examples show why you must describe the actual service. A maintenance coordinator, leasing agent, resident employee, and full-service manager can have different roles. Job titles alone do not answer the legal question.

Send the regulator a short duty list if the answer remains unclear. State who owns the property, who employs the worker, and who receives rent. Ask which license or exception covers that arrangement.

Keep the regulator's answer with your contract review. Do not accept a claimed exception without a clear explanation. A manager who operates in another state must still explain authority for your property's location.

## Where is the official state license lookup?

The official state real estate regulator is the starting point for a license search. California DRE provides a public license lookup. In Texas, start with TREC and its license search tools.

Type the regulator's address yourself or follow a link from a state government page. Avoid treating a manager's screenshot as your own check. Records can change, and a screenshot may refer to another person.

Ask for the legal business name, individual license number, and supervising broker. Search by number where possible. Match the record to the name and address on your agreement. Resolve spelling or entity differences before you pay.

Use this verification log:

| Item | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Individual | Full name and license number |
| Firm | Legal name and any trade name |
| Status | Active status and listed expiration |
| Broker | Supervising person or firm |
| Discipline | Orders shown by the regulator |
| Review | Date you checked and unresolved points |

Read the regulator's notes about record scope. A search result may not include every past complaint. Keep a copy of the record you used for the hiring decision.

## What do NARPM designations mean?

NARPM designations show trade group qualifications, not state permission to perform licensed work. NARPM describes its designations as a mix of experience, education, and association service. Check the designation separately from the state license.

NARPM lists Residential Management Professional and Master Property Manager designations for people. It lists Certified Residential Management Company for firms. Ask who holds the credential and whether it remains current.

A company logo does not prove that the person assigned to your house holds the same credential. Ask who handles leasing, who approves repairs, and who reviews the accounts. Match the claimed training to the work you need.

Other credentials also need direct checks with the group that grants them. Save the full name of the credential rather than a string of initials. Ask what the program tests and what it does not cover.

Use credentials as part of a wider review. Request a sample owner statement and a sample repair record. Ask for references from owners with similar homes. The [property management page](/property-management) can help frame the service discussion, but the official records settle the license check.

## What does a license protect?

A license creates a regulatory check, but it does not guarantee good service or investment results. California DRE's hiring guide urges owners to check the license, agreement, and professional claims. Treat those as separate review steps.

Ask how the firm holds funds, reports transactions, and handles complaints. Check insurance with the insurer or agent. A license number does not prove that a policy covers a particular mistake.

Read any discipline order shown in the official record. Note what happened, what restrictions apply, and whether the manager has met them. Ask for a direct explanation. Do not assume a clean search proves the absence of every service problem.

Then inspect the contract. Find repair authority, fee terms, reporting duties, access to records, and termination steps. Use the [management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to compare costs after you confirm the service scope.

Keep identity and payment checks linked. Confirm bank instructions through a known contact method. A real license belonging to someone else does not protect a payment sent to an impostor.

## What should you do before signing?

Complete the record check while you still have time to choose another firm. Send a short written list of gaps. Ask the manager to resolve each item with a document or a direct explanation.

Compare the final agreement with the firm you verified. Check that the named broker, company, and payee fit the same arrangement. Keep the final contract and the license search in the same folder.

Set a future review point in your owner records. Repeat the check when the firm changes names, changes brokers, or assigns a new service provider. Ask how the firm tells owners about material changes.

If the regulator cannot confirm the claimed authority, pause the hiring decision. Get local legal help before you rely on an exception. The goal is a clear, verifiable service relationship before the manager receives control over your rental.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.
