## Which claims or disputes can come from tenants?

Review screening, property conditions, charges, access and communications as tenant dispute areas. This guide does not claim that these categories form a ranked national list of lawsuits.

The FTC explains that tenant screening reports are consumer reports covered by the Fair Credit Reporting Act. It says adverse action based partly or fully on such a report requires notice. Examples include a rejected application or a higher deposit.

Use a reviewed screening workflow and approved notices. Keep the decision record and the source information needed to explain it. Do not assume a screening vendor sends every notice your company needs.

The FTC also warns that a blanket refusal to rent to anyone with a criminal record may violate the Fair Housing Act. Have qualified counsel review screening criteria and exception handling. Avoid owner instructions that bypass the reviewed policy.

| Risk area | Suggested control | Evidence to preserve |
|---|---|---|
| Screening decision | Reviewed criteria and notice process | Application, decision and notice record |
| Repair complaint | Triage and assigned follow-up | Original report and work history |
| Disputed charge | Checked basis and clear explanation | Lease or agreement and ledger |
| Access dispute | Locally reviewed access process | Notice and visit record |
| Data disclosure | Limited access and approved sharing | Access and transfer record |

Check local tenant protections separately. A federal screening source does not answer every question about fees, notices, repairs or entry in your city.

## Which disputes can come from owners?

Owner disputes can involve service scope, authority, fees, funds or an alleged failure to act. Use the agreement and actual work record to inspect each issue.

A signed service promise should match your sales materials and staff process. If the offer says a service is included, staff need a way to deliver and record it. Review exceptions before they become an unexplained bill.

Keep spending authority clear. Record what the owner approved, the estimate presented and any scope change. A vague message can leave both parties with different views of what was authorized.

Client money needs separate attention. California DRE guidance describes control records, beneficiary records and reconciliation for trust funds. It also states that the broker or designated officer remains responsible for compliant records. Those are California references, not a national rule sheet.

Reconcile accounts before you explain a disputed payout. Distinguish earned fees, paid expenses, unpaid obligations and reserves. Do not let a summary balance replace the transaction history.

Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to explain fee arithmetic when helpful. Check the signed terms first; a calculator does not establish authority to charge a fee.

If the owner challenges conduct or threatens a claim, involve the appropriate leader and adviser. Keep staff from giving conflicting explanations or making unreviewed settlement promises.

## How does documentation help protect the company?

Documentation helps establish what people reported, what the company knew and what action followed. It does not guarantee a favorable result or excuse a failure to act.

Keep the original maintenance report, time received, urgency review and assigned action. Preserve vendor acceptance, visit notes, approval changes and completion evidence. A work order marked closed is not enough if the issue remains unresolved.

Property Meld's maintenance preparation guide recommends thorough records for expense tracking, contractor review and communication. Use that advice to build an auditable case history. Keep factual observations separate from guesses about the cause.

Write notes in plain language. Identify the source of a fact and whether an amount is an estimate. Avoid adding blame or unsupported statements that do not help the next person act.

Preserve original messages and record corrections clearly. Do not rewrite history to make a delayed action look prompt. Ask counsel how to manage a hold on records when a dispute or investigation appears.

Test whether another employee can reconstruct the case without asking the original handler. If the answer depends on memory, add the missing process for future cases.

Limit access to personal and financial data. Good documentation also needs a safe storage and sharing process so the record does not create another problem.

## How does insurance fit into lawsuit risk?

Insurance can support financial risk management, but the policy terms determine coverage. Ask a licensed insurance adviser to review your actual activities and exposures.

The SBA distinguishes general liability coverage from professional liability coverage. Its guide describes professional liability as addressing risks such as errors and negligence in services. Those categories help frame a discussion; they do not confirm that your policy covers a specific claim.

Give the adviser accurate details about leasing, maintenance coordination, client funds and data handling. Discuss staff and vendor roles as well as the company's own work. Do not assume a property owner's policy covers the management company.

Review exclusions, deductibles, limits and reporting conditions. Ask how the policy treats work performed before a change in coverage. Keep the answer with the policy documents rather than relying on an informal sales assurance.

Ask counsel and the insurer how to respond when a demand or claim arrives. Follow the applicable reporting process and preserve the records. Do not assume you can settle first and ask about coverage later.

Insurance review should accompany operating controls. A policy does not replace timely repair follow-up, sound screening or correct accounting.

## What should I review before the next dispute?

Choose a small set of current cases and trace each from first contact through resolution. Look for missing authority, incomplete notices and work with no assigned follow-up.

Have counsel review local requirements in the areas your team handles. Ask the responsible broker to check trust and licensing controls. Give staff clear escalation contacts they can use before making a risky decision.

Check a rejected application, a disputed invoice and an old maintenance case. Confirm that each file has source records, a decision basis and a clear next action. Use the findings to improve the process.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

Start with the oldest unresolved complaint. Preserve the record, confirm the current facts and assign the next action to someone with authority to complete it.
