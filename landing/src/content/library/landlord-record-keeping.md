## Which application and screening records should I keep?

Keep the application, the criteria you used and the decision record in a restricted file. Preserve enough context to explain the result without putting sensitive reports in general office folders.

Record which criteria version applied when the person submitted the application. Save any requested clarification and the response. Distinguish an incomplete application from a decision based on a completed review.

The FTC explains that using a consumer report for an adverse housing decision triggers notice duties under the FCRA. Keep proof of the notice you gave. When you finish using report information, the FTC requires secure disposal so others cannot reconstruct it. [Read the FTC landlord guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/using-consumer-reports-what-landlords-need-know).

Set permissions by job. Leasing staff may need the decision and approved lease terms. A repair vendor does not need the applicant's credit history or identity document. Avoid forwarding the full application to answer a narrow question.

If a dispute starts, pause routine deletion for relevant records. Ask counsel what the preservation scope includes before deleting messages, reports or account exports.

## Which leases and notices belong in the tenancy file?

Keep the signed lease and every later change with clear dates. An unsigned draft should never look like the final agreement.

Use a simple naming pattern that includes property, tenancy and document type. Mark old versions as replaced rather than deleting the history. A future reviewer should see which terms applied to a disputed event.

Keep guaranties, pet terms, approved occupants and renewal records with the lease. Store proof that required disclosures reached the tenant. Save the actual notice, not only a calendar task saying someone sent one.

Build a notice log. Record the reason, approved form, delivery method and service evidence. Keep related messages so the reader can follow the event. Do not edit a sent notice to make a later copy look correct.

For manager changes, test the handoff. Ask the receiving manager to find the lease, current rent and open issue from the exported file. A file is useful only if another person can read it.

## What payment and receipt records do I need?

Keep a tenant ledger tied to bank activity and source receipts. The ledger explains why money entered or left the account.

| Record group | Keep together | Access approach |
|---|---|---|
| Lease terms | Signed lease, changes and renewal | Staff with tenancy duties |
| Payment history | Ledger, receipt and settlement proof | Accounting and assigned manager |
| Security deposit | Deposit ledger, bank proof and return record | Staff with trust duties |
| Screening | Criteria, reports and notices | Restricted leasing access |
| Repairs | Request, work order, invoice and photos | Assigned maintenance staff |
| Legal matter | Notices, service and counsel instructions | Restricted case access |

Keep deposit money and deposit records distinct from rent. A transfer between accounts is not new rental income merely because it appears on a statement.

Document refunds, reversals and returned payments. Do not erase the original entry. Use an adjustment that shows what changed and why.

For rental tax records, IRS Publication 527 explains income, expenses and the treatment of deposits. Use it with your tax professional when setting accounting categories. [Read Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527).

## How do inspection photos and repair records fit together?

Inspection photos need dates, locations and the related work record. A folder of unnamed images leaves the reviewer to guess what happened.

Keep the tenant's original request, the response and access coordination. Attach the vendor's findings and invoice to the same issue. Show when the repair was completed and how you checked the result.

Preserve the original photo file. Add a description in your system rather than changing the image to remove context. Keep a separate annotated copy if you need to mark damage.

For a deposit dispute, connect the move-in condition to the move-out condition and claimed work. Avoid treating the invoice alone as proof that the tenant caused the problem.

Record preventive work too. Roof inspections, leak checks and equipment service help explain maintenance history. They also help a new manager avoid repeating work or missing an open concern.

## How long should I keep each record?

Set retention by the record's purpose and the rules that apply to it. Tax, deposit, screening and litigation files can need different periods.

The IRS generally calls for 3 years for ordinary tax records, but lists longer periods for specific situations. Its guidance says property records can need retention through disposal and the relevant tax limitation period. Do not use the ordinary period to delete purchase and improvement proof. [Read the IRS retention guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/how-long-should-i-keep-records).

State tenancy rules add separate duties. Massachusetts chapter 186 section 15B requires specified deposit records for 2 years after tenancy ends. New York RPP 235-e separately requires cash rent receipt records for at least 3 years. Neither rule is a national retention schedule. [Read New York's receipt rule](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPP/235-E).

Ask counsel and your tax professional to approve a table for your properties. List the record, purpose, retention trigger and deletion owner. Add a hold flag for active disputes.

Test backups and exports before deleting anything from the live system. Confirm that a recovered file still contains attachments and dates. Keep a disposal log that identifies the category and approval, without copying sensitive content into that log.

Start with a sample tenancy and check whether someone else can reconstruct it. Browse the [rental guide library](/guides) for related operations tasks.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney. For tax records, ask a tax professional.
