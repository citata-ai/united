## What is the operating expense ratio?

The operating expense ratio (OER) is the share of a rental's income that goes to operating expenses. It tells an owner how much of each rent dollar the building eats before the mortgage. A lower OER means more income is left for debt and profit.

OER answers 3 owner questions:

- Is this building more costly to run than similar buildings?
- Did costs grow faster than rent this year?
- Will the income support the loan I want?

OER is the mirror of the NOI margin. If OER is 40%, the NOI margin is 60%.

## How do you calculate OER?

You calculate OER by dividing operating expenses by income, then multiplying by 100.

OER = operating expenses / effective gross income x 100

Effective gross income (EGI) is the rent you expect to collect after vacancy and bad debt, plus other income such as fees and laundry. Some people use gross potential rent instead. Pick one and use it every year, so your numbers compare.

Worked example, a 4-unit building with made-up numbers:

| Line | Per year |
|------|----------|
| Gross potential rent (4 x $1,500 x 12) | $72,000 |
| Vacancy and bad debt (5%) | −$3,600 |
| Other income (laundry, fees) | $1,200 |
| Effective gross income | $69,600 |
| Property taxes | $9,000 |
| Insurance | $3,600 |
| Repairs and maintenance | $5,400 |
| Owner-paid utilities (water, trash) | $3,000 |
| Management fee (8% of collected rent) | $5,472 |
| Landscaping, snow, cleaning | $1,500 |
| Total operating expenses | $27,972 |
| OER ($27,972 / $69,600) | 40.2% |
| NOI | $41,628 |

What is not in operating expenses:

- **Mortgage principal and interest.** That is debt service.
- **Depreciation.** It is a tax entry, not a cash cost.
- **Big capital projects,** such as a new roof. Many owners show a yearly reserve line for these, but list it on its own line.
- **Income taxes** on the rental profit.

## What is a normal OER for rentals?

No single OER is normal for rentals. Location, age, size and who pays utilities move the number a lot. Use national data as a rough guide, then compare with similar buildings near you.

National apartment data gives one reference point. The National Apartment Association's 2025 Dollar of Rent study, reported by CRE Daily, splits each $1 of apartment rent like this:

| Where the rent dollar went (2025) | Cents |
|----------------------------------|-------|
| Debt service | 43 |
| Operating costs (insurance, utilities, upkeep, management and more) | 27 |
| Property taxes | 10 |
| Payroll | 7 |
| Capital reserves | 1 |
| Owner's share | about 11 |

Add the non-debt lines: 27 + 10 + 7 + 1 = 45 cents. That implies an OER near 45% for the apartments in NAA's data. NAA notes totals can miss 100 because of rounding. The same report says annual operating cost per unit rose 4.6% in 2025, to $8,213, while revenue per unit rose 2.7%.

What that means: when costs grow faster than rent, OER rises even if nothing at the building changed. Check OER every year.

## What is the 50 percent rule?

The 50 percent rule is a quick screen that assumes operating expenses take about half of a rental's gross rent, before the mortgage. Landlord Studio describes it as a guideline for estimating that "roughly half of its gross income" will go to operating costs.

How to use it, with made-up numbers:

1. **Take gross rent.** A house rents for $2,000 a month, so $24,000 a year.
2. **Take half.** Estimated operating expenses: $12,000.
3. **Find estimated NOI.** $24,000 − $12,000 = $12,000.
4. **Compare with the mortgage.** If yearly principal and interest is $13,000, the deal loses money before any surprise.

Limits of the rule:

- **Sources do not agree on what it includes.** Landlord Studio lists taxes, insurance, vacancy, repairs and utilities, and leaves out management and HOA fees. Other guides include management. Write down your own list.
- **It is a screen, not an analysis.** Landlord Studio says the rule "should only ever be used as a starting point for further research and analysis."
- **New buildings often run lower.** Older buildings with owner-paid utilities often run higher.

## Which costs raise OER the most?

Property taxes, payroll, insurance, repairs and owner-paid utilities usually raise OER the most. In NAA's 2025 data, property taxes alone took about 10 cents of each apartment rent dollar, and payroll took 7.

| Cost | Why it moves OER | What an owner can do |
|------|-----------------|---------------------|
| Property taxes | Reassessments after a sale or a rate change | Check the assessment; appeal with comps |
| Insurance | Premiums rise after regional disasters | Shop the policy each year; review deductibles |
| Repairs | Old systems and slow response | Plan replacements; fix small leaks fast |
| Owner-paid utilities | Water and heat in older buildings | Submeter where legal; fix leaks; low-flow fixtures |
| Turnover | Make-ready and lost rent | Raise renewal rates |
| Management and payroll | Staff and fees | Compare quotes; measure results, not only the fee |

Watch for: a very low OER is not always good. It can mean deferred repairs. A roof that no one fixes keeps OER low for 2 years, then costs a lot at once.

## How a property manager reports OER

A property manager should report OER each year and compare it with the year before. Ask for:

- **An income statement with each expense line**, not only totals.
- **OER and NOI** for the year and the prior year.
- **A note for any line that moved more than 10%**, with the reason.
- **A reserve line** for capital items, separate from repairs.
