## Can a Section 8 rent beat market rent?

A Section 8 rent cannot beat market rent for the same unit. Under 24 CFR 982.507, the PHA must find that the rent to owner is reasonable compared with similar unassisted units. The rule looks at location, quality, size, unit type, age, amenities and the utilities you supply. At all times, the rent to owner "may not exceed the reasonable rent."

There is a second cap: the payment standard. The PHA sets it at 90% to 110% of HUD's Fair Market Rent (FMR) without HUD approval (24 CFR 982.503(c)). The payment standard covers rent plus tenant-paid utilities. So if the tenant pays utilities, the rent you get without extra cost to the family is the payment standard minus the utility allowance.

So the voucher question is not "who pays more rent." It is "which tenancy costs less to run."

## How do vacancy days compare?

Vacancy days can be higher on a voucher lease-up, because the PHA must approve the unit first. 24 CFR 982.305(b) says three things must happen before the lease starts: the unit passes inspection, the lease and tenancy addendum are signed, and the PHA approves the tenancy.

The federal clocks:

| Step | Federal timing rule |
|---|---|
| Inspection after request for tenancy approval | Within 15 days for a PHA with up to 1,250 budgeted units; "within a reasonable time" for larger PHAs (982.305(b)(2)) |
| HAP contract signed | No later than 60 days after the lease starts (982.305(c)) |
| HAP for days before the contract | Paid after signing, for up to 60 days (982.305(c)(3)) |

For context, the US rental vacancy rate was 7.3% in Q2 2026, up from 7.0% a year earlier (U.S. Census Bureau, via FRED). We have no federal data on vacancy days for voucher units versus market units. Use your own turnover records.

## What do inspections and repairs add to costs?

Inspections add a repair risk, not a big fee. Under 24 CFR 982.405, the PHA inspects before move-in and at least every 2 years during the tenancy. The PHA may not charge you for the first inspection. It may charge "a reasonable fee" only for a reinspection that shows you did not fix an item you were responsible for.

The real cost is repair speed. Under 24 CFR 982.404, you must fix a life-threatening item within 24 hours and other items within 30 days. If you do not, the PHA abates the HAP. For a well-kept unit, most of these repairs are work you would do anyway.

## How does payment reliability change cash flow?

The HAP part of a voucher rent is a contract payment from a government agency. 24 CFR 982.451(b)(5) says the PHA "must pay the housing assistance payment promptly when due." The HAP contract must also allow late penalties against the PHA in some cases, for example when you charge the same late fee to all tenants.

The tenant share is like any other rent: you collect it. In many voucher tenancies the tenant share is a small part of the rent, so the amount at risk each month is smaller. In our example below, the PHA pays most of the rent.

## Worked example: 3-bedroom single-family home

HUD's FY2027 FMR for a 3-bedroom in the Chicago-Joliet-Naperville HUD Metro FMR Area is $2,586 a month, up 12.7% from $2,294 in FY2026 (HUD User). FMRs are set at the 40th percentile of recent-mover rents in the area (24 CFR 888.113). Everything else below is a made-up assumption, so swap in your own numbers.

**Assumptions**

| Item | Market tenant | Voucher tenant |
|---|---|---|
| Payment standard (100% of FMR, assumed) | n/a | $2,586 |
| Utility allowance, tenant pays (assumed) | n/a | $250 |
| Monthly rent to owner | $2,350 | $2,336 (payment standard − allowance) |
| Vacancy per year (assumed) | 3 weeks | 5 weeks |
| Rent not collected, % of gross rent (assumed) | 3% | 1% (tenant share only at risk) |
| Extra repairs for inspections (assumed) | $0 | $400 a year |

**Annual result**

| Line | Market tenant | Voucher tenant |
|---|---|---|
| Gross rent (× 12) | $28,200 | $28,032 |
| Vacancy loss | −$1,627 | −$2,695 |
| Rent not collected | −$846 | −$280 |
| Inspection repairs | $0 | −$400 |
| Net rent | $25,727 | $24,657 |

In this example, the market tenant nets about $1,070 more. Now change one assumption: if the voucher tenant stays longer and the yearly vacancy drops to 3 weeks, the voucher side gains about $1,078. Then the two are about even.

Takeaway: vacancy and turnover decide the result, not the rent. Two extra weeks of vacancy cost more than the inspection repairs in this example.

## When does the voucher rent match or beat your market rent?

The voucher option looks best in four cases:

1. **Your market rent is near or below the payment standard minus utilities.** Then the voucher pays your full asking rent.
2. **The FMR went up.** In the Chicago area the 3-bedroom FMR rose 12.7% for FY2027, so payment standards there can rise too.
3. **Your PHA uses Small Area FMRs or exception payment standards.** These can set higher caps by ZIP code (24 CFR 982.503(d)).
4. **Your turnover is high.** Fewer move-outs mean fewer vacant weeks.

It looks worst when your market rent is well above the payment standard. Then the family must pay the gap, and the 40% cap at move-in (24 CFR 982.508) can block the lease.
