## What happens to Google reviews after a rebrand?

Google review treatment depends on whether the change qualifies as the same business. Google's review guidance says reviews remain for minor name changes and qualifying multi-location name changes. Do not promise that every rebrand will preserve reviews.

Separate the public brand, legal entity, ownership, address, and service changes in your plan. A domain update is not the same event as buying another company. Give support the actual facts rather than asking a broad question about rebranding.

Read Google's representation guidelines before editing the business profile. Those guidelines set the rebrand criteria and distinguish some changes as a new business. Ask support about a doubtful case before taking irreversible profile steps.

Save the current profile link, business details, access owners, and support history. Keep records of the real-world brand you use. Do not create a duplicate profile just to experiment with a new name.

Treat search rankings and review handling as separate workstreams in the same plan. A correct site redirect does not decide review eligibility. A retained profile does not prove that every old page has moved correctly.

| Change | Main check | Evidence to keep |
|---|---|---|
| Public name | Profile rebrand eligibility | Support response and brand records |
| Domain | Old-to-new page mapping | Redirect test results |
| Address | Correct location process | Current office and contact facts |
| Ownership | Proper access transfer | Account ownership record |
| Service scope | Accurate promises | Approved page and agreement text |

The table is a planning aid. A combined change needs each relevant check. Do not bundle unknowns into a single launch-day edit.

## How should you set up redirects?

Map each useful old URL to its closest relevant new URL before launch. Google's site-move guide recommends permanent server-side redirects and direct destinations. Ask the developer to document the mapping and test it.

Start with the pages owners use. Include service pages, fees, contact forms, local office pages, guides, and shared documents. Add pages that receive search traffic or outside links. Keep a record of pages you intentionally remove.

Do not send every old address to the new home page. An owner who opened a repair process needs that information, not a broad introduction. Keep the same task available at the destination wherever possible.

Check internal links, preferred page addresses, sitemap entries, and images. Update the site's own links to the new destination rather than relying only on redirects. Test the public result with a fresh browser.

Google's guide recommends keeping redirects for at least a year. Plan hosting and domain access around that requirement. Keep old addresses under your control so past owner emails and partner links continue to work.

Ask the developer to use Search Console's move tools where applicable and to monitor old and new properties. Google's guide warns that rankings can fluctuate during a move. Set expectations before launch rather than interpreting every early movement as permanent damage.

## Which listings should you update?

Update the listings and partner records that owners use to verify and contact the company. Begin with an inventory, then assign each entry a responsible person. Do not assume a bulk directory service covers every important source.

Include Google Business Profile, Bing Places, social profiles, professional memberships, referral partners, and relevant local directories. Add vendor records, email signatures, public PDFs, and sales proposal templates. Check business facts against an approved name sheet.

Track the listing URL, old details, new details, access owner, submission date, and public result. Mark an update complete only after checking what a visitor sees. A submitted change can still leave the public link or phone wrong.

Ask important partners to update direct links to the new pages. Give them the correct name and destination in a short message. Do not ask them to rewrite independent reviews or invent new praise.

Keep earlier business names visible where they help owners confirm continuity, subject to brand and legal review. Explain the change on a useful company page. Avoid a confusing mix of old and new names with no context.

## How should you tell owners and tenants?

Tell owners and tenants what changed, when it takes effect, and how to verify any action requested. Use separate messages for each audience's tasks. A brand announcement should not bury urgent service information.

Owners may need to know the public name, contact details, report access, agreement implications, and service continuity. Tenants may need help finding maintenance, rent, and lease contacts. State checked facts, not broad assurances about things the team has not tested.

Give staff a short approved explanation. Include an independent verification route, such as the existing known phone number. Keep the old website notice useful so a person following an earlier message can understand the change.

Do not use a rebrand email as the sole authority for payment changes. Any real bank or payment update needs a separate verified process. Ask the responsible finance and legal staff to approve that process.

Prepare answers for confused callers. Explain whether the company, manager, office, or only the public name changed. Escalate contract questions to the appropriate reviewer instead of guessing.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## What should the rebrand launch checklist cover?

The launch checklist should test the tasks owners and tenants need to complete. Choose checks with a visible result and a responsible person. Keep design preferences separate from a failed contact or payment route.

- **Check identity.** Confirm the approved public name across the new site and messages.
- **Test old links.** Open important old URLs and inspect the final destination.
- **Submit owner forms.** Confirm receipt, reply access, and the confirmation message.
- **Test service access.** Check portals and maintenance routes without exposing private records.
- **Check account control.** Confirm domain, hosting, search, listing, and email access.
- **Review search reports.** Watch errors and important landing pages after launch.
- **Record open faults.** Assign each fault and retest after the repair.

Use the [website search checklist](/guides/ai-search-checklist-property-management-website) for the site review. Keep [traffic tracking](/guides/track-ai-traffic-property-management) consistent so the domain change does not create a false reporting trend.

Judge the launch by working contact paths and checked business facts before judging early traffic. Keep the old domain and support records available. Fix errors quickly, then continue monitoring the owner pages that matter to your business.
