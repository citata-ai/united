## What do BLS data show for property managers?

The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) puts the median wage for property, real estate and community association managers at $69,990 a year in May 2025. That is $33.65 an hour. Half of the workers earn more and half earn less.

BLS gives the spread too. The lowest 10% earned less than $41,010. The top 10% earned more than $139,680. The mean was $83,710, which is higher than the median because a few high earners pull it up (BLS OEWS, May 2025).

| BLS measure, May 2025 | Value |
|---|---|
| Median annual wage | $69,990 |
| Median hourly wage | $33.65 |
| Mean annual wage | $83,710 |
| Lowest 10% earned less than | $41,010 |
| Top 10% earned more than | $139,680 |
| Median wage, real estate industry | $64,290 |
| Wage-and-salary jobs in OEWS | 311,180 |
| All jobs, incl. self-employed (OOH, 2025) | 460,400 |
| Job growth, 2025–2035 | 4% |
| Openings per year | about 36,900 |

Two notes before you use these numbers. First, the occupation code (SOC 11-9141) mixes three jobs: residential property managers, commercial real estate managers and community association managers. Second, BLS says its wage data does not include self-employed workers. BLS counts 31% of the occupation as self-employed. So the median is for employees, which is what you need when you hire.

The median in the real estate industry is $64,290, a bit below the all-industry median. Most residential management companies sit in that industry. Use $64,290 as your national anchor for a portfolio manager role at a third-party firm.

State numbers also hide city gaps. A manager in Chicago and a manager in rural Illinois share one state median. For a closer number, look up your metro area in the BLS OEWS tables. Search for SOC code 11-9141.

## Salary or salary plus bonus?

Salary plus a small bonus fits most third-party management companies. A fixed salary gives the manager a stable life. A bonus points their effort at the results owners pay you for.

Pure commission fits leasing agents better than portfolio managers. A portfolio manager does a lot of work that does not close a deal: owner calls, maintenance approvals, notices, renewals. Pay only on new deals, and that work gets less care.

Here is a simple way to set the numbers:

1. **Start from the local median.** Use the state or metro OEWS median for SOC 11-9141.
2. **Adjust for the role.** A new assistant manager starts below the median. A senior manager with 150+ doors and owner duties starts at or above it.
3. **Set a bonus pool you can pay in a bad year.** Base it on your margin, not on hope.
4. **Pay the bonus each quarter.** A year is too long for most people to connect effort to reward.
5. **Write it down.** Put the KPI, the target and the payout in one page that both of you sign.

## Which KPIs should a bonus use?

A bonus should use 3 or 4 KPIs that the manager controls and that your software already tracks. More KPIs make the plan hard to read. KPIs outside the manager's control feel unfair and stop working.

| KPI | Why it matters | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Lease renewal rate | Each renewal saves a turnover and a vacancy | Do not reward renewals of tenants the owner asked to non-renew |
| Average days vacant | Vacancy is the owner's biggest cost | Measure from move-out to new lease start |
| Owner retention (doors lost) | Lost doors cut your revenue for years | Exclude owners who sell the property |
| Work order time to close | Slow repairs drive tenant and owner complaints | Use median days, not mean, so one hard job does not skew it |
| Rent collected by the 5th | Shows follow-up on late rent | Set the target per portfolio, since tenant mix differs |

A worked example (example input, not a benchmark): a manager earns a $64,000 base. You set a bonus target of 8% of base, or $5,120 a year, paid as $1,280 a quarter. Each of 4 KPIs is worth $320 a quarter. Hit 3 of 4 targets, and the payout is $960 that quarter.

Do not tie a bonus to fees charged to tenants, such as late fees. That can push a manager to make life harder for tenants. It also creates a Fair Housing risk if staff treat tenants in different ways.

## How do you check that your pay plan works?

Check your pay plan against your revenue each year. Payroll is the biggest cost line for most management companies. If pay rises faster than revenue per door, your margin shrinks.

Three checks help:

- **Payroll share of revenue.** Add base, bonus, payroll tax and benefits. Divide by total revenue.
- **Doors per manager.** Count doors per full-time manager. A raise with no change in doors cuts your margin.
- **Turnover of staff.** Count how many managers left in 12 months. A pay plan below the local median often shows up here first.
