## The 5 parts of your website and who owns each

Your website is not one thing. It is 5 assets, and the contract can give each one a different owner. Check them one by one.

| Asset | Who owns it by default | What gives you control |
|---|---|---|
| Domain name | The registrant on the registrar record | Registrant = your company, in your own registrar account |
| Site code and design | The vendor, unless the contract says otherwise | A clause that gives you the code once paid, plus an export |
| Blog posts and page text | The writer or vendor, unless assigned in writing | A written assignment of copyright to your company |
| Photos and graphics | The photographer, or the stock library's license holder | Your own photos, or a license that moves with the site |
| Google Analytics, Search Console, Google Business Profile | Whoever created the account | Your company account as Administrator or verified owner |

When you leave a vendor, you take only what you control. A vendor can be honest and still keep a part you thought was yours, because the contract said so.

## Who holds the domain registration?

The domain belongs to the registrant on file with the registrar. If your agency bought the domain in its own account, it controls where the domain points, even if you paid for it.

Check it today:

1. **Ask your vendor where the domain is registered.** Get the registrar name and the account owner.
2. **Look at the registrant field.** It should name your company, not the agency.
3. **Get your own login.** You should be able to sign in without the vendor.
4. **Turn on auto-renew.** A lapsed domain is the fastest way to lose a site.

If you need to move it, ICANN explains the process. You start a transfer with the new registrar, and both registrars confirm that you want the move. ICANN also says a registrar may deny a transfer within 60 days of a new registration. A change to the registrant name, organization or email also starts a 60-day lock on transfers. Some registrars offer an opt-out. So if you plan to move registrars, ICANN suggests you finish the transfer before you change contact details.

## Can I take my blog posts and photos?

You can take blog posts and photos only if you hold the rights to them. US copyright law does not hand you the text just because you paid for it.

The U.S. Copyright Office explains this in Circular 30. A "work made for hire" belongs to the party that hired the work in 2 cases. One is work an employee makes as part of the job. The other is certain kinds of commissioned work, with an express written agreement signed by both parties. Work from an outside agency is not employee work. So the safe path is a contract clause that assigns the copyright in your blog posts and page text to your company.

Photos need a separate check:

- **Photos you took or paid a photographer for** are yours if the photographer's terms say so.
- **Stock photos** come with a license. The license may belong to the vendor, not to you.
- **Vendor templates and graphics** often stay with the vendor.

One vendor says this in plain words. The Property Manager Websites FAQ says you own the site's HTML once it is live and paid for. It will give you a download on request. The same answer says licensed stock images, graphics and assets it provides stay under its license and cannot transfer. You must buy them separately to keep them.

## What do vendor contracts say about ownership?

Vendor contracts differ, and the public pages show part of the difference. We read 3 vendor pages on 2026-10-19. These are public statements, not the full contracts.

| Vendor | What the public page says about ownership | Term on the page |
|---|---|---|
| Goodjuju Marketing | "You own your website and all assets outright once they're paid for" | Website is a one-time build; care plans and SEO month to month |
| Property Manager Websites | You own the site HTML once live and paid; licensed stock images stay with PMW | Packages with 1-, 2- or 3-year commitments; Rentvine subscription required |
| ClearLead Digital | Website add-on listed as "New PM website (owned)" | 12-month term for the website add-on; SEO plans month to month |

"Once paid for" is the key phrase. If the build cost is spread over a 1-year or 2-year plan, the site may not be fully paid until the term ends. Ask what happens if you leave early.

## Who owns your data accounts?

Your Google accounts hold years of history, so they matter as much as the site. Google Search Console says a property must have at least one verified owner, and owners can add and remove other users. Google Analytics says you need the Administrator role to add or change users. Google Business Profile Help says a profile can have many owners but only one primary owner.

Set them up like this:

- **Search Console:** your company account is a verified owner. The agency is a full user.
- **Google Analytics 4:** your company account is Administrator. The agency is an Editor.
- **Google Business Profile:** your company account is the primary owner. The agency is a manager.

If the agency created these accounts, ask it to add your account as owner, then check that you can sign in on your own.

## How do I check before I sign?

Check ownership before you sign, because after you sign you have less to bargain with. Ask the vendor these 8 questions and get the answers in the contract, not in an email.

- [ ] Is the domain registered in my company's name, in an account I can log in to?
- [ ] Do I own the site code and design once paid? Can I get an export?
- [ ] Does the contract assign copyright in all page text and blog posts to my company?
- [ ] Which photos and graphics stay under your license?
- [ ] When is the site "fully paid", and what do I owe if I leave early?
- [ ] Will you give me the redirect list and form settings when I leave?
- [ ] Is my company the owner of Google Analytics, Search Console and Google Business Profile?
- [ ] How many days' notice do I need to cancel?
