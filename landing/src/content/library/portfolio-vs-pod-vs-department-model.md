## How do the portfolio, pod and department models compare?

The three models differ in one thing: who owns a door. In the portfolio model, one person owns it. In the department model, a task team owns each part of it. In the pod model, a small team owns it together. Blanket describes all three, with pros and cons, in its company structure post.

| | Portfolio | Pod | Department |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who owns a door | One portfolio manager | A team of 3, sometimes 4 | Task teams: leasing, maintenance, accounting |
| Owner contact | One person | One main person, team behind | Changes with the task, unless you add an owner relations role |
| Skill depth | Generalist | Some specialty per seat | Deep specialist |
| Cover for vacations | Weak | Built in | Built in |
| Risk if one person leaves | High: work and relationships leave | Medium | Low |
| Overhead | Low | High until you reach scale | Medium to high |
| Main failure | Burnout, turnover | Cost too early | Handoffs, owner repeats the story |

Source for the pros and cons: Blanket. The table layout and the "main failure" row are our summary.

## What is the portfolio model?

The portfolio model gives one manager every task for a set of accounts. That manager sends maintenance, does leases and renewals, and talks to owners. Blanket lists the main benefit: one point of contact, so context is rarely lost and the owner relationship gets strong.

Blanket also lists the risks. The model puts a lot on one person, which can lead to high turnover. And because the relationships are strong, a manager who leaves may take accounts with them.

The portfolio model fits a small company. The founder often is the first portfolio manager. It breaks when the manager cannot take a week off without missed calls, or when the second manager runs the work in a different way from the first.

## What is a pod, and how many people are in one?

A pod is a small team that manages a defined set of properties as one unit. Blanket says the typical pod has 3 employees, sometimes 4. One person handles owners, one handles residents and one handles maintenance. They can cover for each other during vacations.

Rentvine describes pods as a mix of the portfolio and department models: each person specializes, but the whole pod shares responsibility for its properties. Rentvine claims faster service from clear responsibility. That is a vendor claim, and Rentvine sells pod features in its software, so test it on your own numbers.

Blanket names the cost: overhead is expensive until you reach scale. Blanket also says a pod still has some of the single-point-of-failure risk of the portfolio model, but less of it.

## What is the department model?

The department model splits the company by task. A leasing team leases every unit. A maintenance team runs every work order. An accounting team does every statement. Blanket lists the benefits: staff become specialists, hiring and training are easier, and simple processes make quality control easier.

The cost is the owner experience. The owner's contact changes with each need. Blanket compares it to a support call where you explain your problem 3 or 4 times to 3 or 4 people. Blanket also gives a fix: a department company can add a customer success or owner relations manager who keeps the owner's context.

## How do I size a pod? A worked example

Size a pod from your own doors-per-person number. We do not publish a standard ratio, because no source we read gives one for all markets. Use your own payroll data.

Example, with numbers that you replace with your own:

| Step | Example value |
|---|---|
| Doors you manage now | 450 |
| People in one pod (Blanket: typical 3) | 3 |
| Doors one pod can serve well, from your own data | 150 |
| Pods needed: 450 ÷ 150 | 3 |
| Pod staff: 3 pods × 3 people | 9 |
| Shared roles outside pods (accounting, leasing agent, owner growth) | your count |

If your current team serves 450 doors with 7 people, a move to 9 pod staff raises payroll. That is the "overhead until scale" that Blanket warns about. Do the math before you reorganize. A pod model pays off only if it cuts owner churn, cuts staff turnover, or lets each pod take more doors after 6 to 12 months.

## When should I switch models?

Switch when the current model shows the same failure again and again, not when a new idea sounds good. Use this checklist.

Signs to leave the portfolio model:

- [ ] A portfolio manager's vacation causes missed calls or late work orders
- [ ] Two managers do the same task in two different ways
- [ ] You lost an owner when a manager left
- [ ] One manager is the only person who knows an owner's history

Signs to leave the department model:

- [ ] Owners complain that they must repeat their story
- [ ] A task falls between two teams, for example a move-out charge that neither leasing nor accounting owns
- [ ] Nobody can answer "who owns this owner?"

Signs that pods are too early:

- [ ] You cannot fill all 3 pod seats with trained people
- [ ] Pod payroll is higher than the revenue of the doors it serves
- [ ] One pod member is in fact doing every role

Blanket's view is that each model can work: the right one depends on the balance between efficiency and personal service that you want. For a company focused on long-term owner relationships, keep owner context in one place, whichever model you pick.

## How does the team model change owner retention and growth?

The team model changes who the owner talks to, and owners judge you on that contact. A model with one clear owner contact makes it easier to keep owners. Keeping owners is only half of growth.
