## What is a legal pre-screening question?

A legal pre-screening question asks about the rental or about your written criteria. It does not ask about who the person is. Move-in date, pets and income criteria are fine. Religion, children and disability are not.

The federal rule is the Fair Housing Act. Under 42 U.S.C. 3604, it is unlawful to refuse to rent, or to set different terms, because of race, color, religion, sex, familial status or national origin. It also covers handicap, which the law uses for disability. Section 3604(c) adds that any statement indicating a preference or limitation based on these classes is unlawful. A question can be such a statement.

States add more classes and rules. Two examples:

- **California:** Government Code 12955 adds classes such as gender identity, sexual orientation, marital status, source of income, veteran status and genetic information. Section 12955(b) makes it unlawful for an owner to make any written or oral inquiry about most of these classes.
- **Washington:** RCW 59.18.255 bars a landlord from refusing a renter based on source of income. It also bars any ad or notice that shows a preference based on source of income.

Your city or county may add more classes. Check local law before you write your questions.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## Which questions should I ask before a showing?

Ask questions that tell you if the unit fits the renter and if the renter fits your written criteria. Keep the list short. Eight questions are enough.

| # | Question | Why you ask | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | When do you want to move in? | Matches your vacancy date | None |
| 2 | How long a lease do you want? | Matches your lease terms | None |
| 3 | How many people will live in the unit? | Applies your occupancy rule | Do not ask ages or if they are children |
| 4 | Do you have pets? What kind and how many? | Applies your pet policy | A renter with a disability may ask for an exception to a pet rule; 42 U.S.C. 3604(f)(3)(B) requires reasonable accommodations in rules |
| 5 | Does anyone who will live here smoke? | Applies a no-smoking rule | Ask everyone |
| 6 | Our income rule is [your written rule]. Do you expect to meet it? | Saves a showing for both sides | Count voucher income as your state requires |
| 7 | Our screening covers [credit, rental history, background]. Are you OK with that? | Sets expectations early | Follow your state and city rules on criminal history |
| 8 | Do you have questions about the unit? | Builds trust | None |

Send these questions in writing, by email or a form. Keep each renter's answers in your records.

## Can I ask about income before a showing?

Yes, you can ask about income before a showing, if you ask every renter and tie the question to a written rule. A good way is to state your rule and ask if the renter expects to meet it. Do not ask where the money comes from unless your criteria need it and your state allows it.

Source of income is a protected class in some places. California Government Code 12955 lists it. Washington RCW 59.18.255 protects it and sets a specific income rule.

**Worked example under Washington RCW 59.18.255(3).** Rent is $2,000 a month. Your rule is income of 2.5 times the rent. A renter has a voucher that pays $1,400 of the rent.

| Step | Math | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Rent | | $2,000 |
| Minus voucher share | $2,000 − $1,400 | $600 |
| Income needed | $600 × 2.5 | $1,500 a month |

Without the voucher rule, you would ask for $5,000 a month. Under RCW 59.18.255(3), you subtract the voucher first. Other states may differ, so check your own law.

The 2.5 multiple here is an example, not a standard. Pick your own rule and apply it to everyone. For voucher rents, see our [Section 8 rent calculator](/tools/section-8-rent-calculator).

## Can I ask why they are moving?

You can ask why a renter is moving, but the question carries risk. The answer may reveal a protected class. A renter may say "we have a new baby," which points to familial status. Another may mention a medical need, which points to disability.

Once you hear that fact, you cannot unhear it. If you later reject the renter, it can look like the fact played a part.

Safer options:

- **Skip it.** Ask only about the move-in date.
- **Ask a narrow version.** "Is your current landlord OK with us calling for a reference?"
- **If you ask it, ask everyone.** Write the answer down and judge only on your written criteria.

## Which questions must I never ask?

Never ask about a protected class, directly or indirectly. This list covers the federal classes in 42 U.S.C. 3604 and common state additions.

- Where are you from? What is your first language?
- Are you married? Is this your partner?
- Do you have kids? Are you pregnant? How old are the people moving in?
- Do you have a disability? What medication do you take?
- What church do you go to?
- Do you get Section 8 or other aid? (risky in states that protect source of income)

Also avoid steering. Do not say a unit or area "is good for families" or "is quiet, mostly older people." Section 3604(c) covers statements as well as decisions.

## How do I ask every renter the same questions?

Use one written process and follow it every time. Consistency is your best proof of fair treatment.

1. **Write your criteria first.** Income rule, occupancy rule, pet policy and screening scope. Date the document.
2. **Use one form.** The same questions, in the same order, for every inquiry.
3. **Answer in order of arrival.** Reply to inquiries by time stamp, not by name or voice on the phone.
4. **Book showings the same way.** Offer the same times to everyone who meets your criteria.
5. **Keep records.** Save each form, the date and the outcome.
6. **Review your ads.** Make sure your listing says nothing that shows a preference.

A written process also saves time. You show the unit only to renters who fit, and you can explain every decision with a document. For more on running rentals, see our [property management resources](/property-management).
