Each year you own a rental, depreciation lowers your taxable rental income. Many owners like that part. The bill for it comes at the sale, and it surprises people who did not plan for it.

This guide explains what the IRS calls unrecaptured section 1250 gain, how to figure it, and how it works with the home sale exclusion.

This is general information, not tax advice. Check the current law or ask a tax professional.

## What Is Depreciation on a Rental?

Depreciation is a yearly deduction for the wear of the building. IRS Publication 527 says residential rental property is depreciated over 27.5 years under the General Depreciation System. You cannot depreciate land, because Publication 527 says land does not wear out.

Each dollar of depreciation also lowers your basis. A lower basis means a larger gain when you sell. That larger gain is where recapture starts.

| Example item (invented numbers) | Amount |
|---|---|
| Price paid for the building, not the land | $220,000 |
| Recovery period (Publication 527) | 27.5 years |
| Depreciation per full year ($220,000 ÷ 27.5) | $8,000 |
| Depreciation after 10 full years | $80,000 |

The first and last years are partial years under IRS conventions. The table shows full years to keep the math simple.

## What Is Unrecaptured Section 1250 Gain?

Unrecaptured section 1250 gain is the part of a long-term gain on real property that is due to depreciation. IRS Publication 544 gives this definition. IRS Topic 409 says this part is taxed at a maximum 25% rate.

The word "recapture" can mislead. For a residential rental, there are two rules:

- **Section 1250 ordinary recapture.** The Form 4797 instructions say this applies if you used an accelerated method or claimed a special depreciation allowance. Publication 544 calls the excess "additional depreciation".
- **Unrecaptured section 1250 gain.** This covers straight-line depreciation. Most single-family and small multifamily rentals fall here.

So for a typical rental, the depreciation part of the gain is not taxed as ordinary income. It is taxed at your ordinary rate, but never above 25%. The rest of the gain gets the 0%, 15% or 20% long-term rates in Topic 409.

## How Do I Figure the Recapture Amount?

The recapture amount is usually the smaller of your total gain or your total depreciation. Publication 544 says unrecaptured section 1250 gain can't be more than the net section 1231 gain. Use this worked example with invented numbers:

| Step | Item | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Amount realized after selling costs | $350,000 |
| 2 | Original basis (building and land) | $280,000 |
| 3 | Depreciation allowed or allowable | −$80,000 |
| 4 | Adjusted basis (2 + 3) | $200,000 |
| 5 | Total gain (1 − 4) | $150,000 |
| 6 | Unrecaptured section 1250 gain (up to 25%) | $80,000 |
| 7 | Other long-term gain (0%, 15% or 20%) | $70,000 |

Now change one input. Say the home sells for $250,000 after costs. The total gain is $50,000. The 1250 part is then $50,000, not $80,000, because it can't be more than the gain.

Publication 544 says you use the Unrecaptured Section 1250 Gain Worksheet in the Schedule D instructions. You report the sale on Form 4797.

## Does Recapture Apply If I Never Took Depreciation?

Yes, in most cases. IRS publications use the phrase "allowed or allowable". Publication 544 says the greater of the depreciation allowed or allowable is generally the amount you use. It also says that if you took no depreciation at all, the allowable amount is figured with the straight-line method.

That rule gives you the worst of both results. You did not get the yearly deduction. You still have the lower basis at sale.

If you missed depreciation, talk to a tax professional before you sell. IRS Publication 946 says you generally file Form 3115 to request a change in your method of accounting for depreciation. A professional can tell you if it fits your case.

## How Does Recapture Work With the Home Sale Exclusion?

The home sale exclusion does not cover depreciation. IRS Publication 523 says you may exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly, if you meet the tests. It also says you can't exclude gain equal to depreciation allowed or allowable after May 6, 1997. That part must be recaptured under section 1250.

This matters for owners who rented a former home. Here is the common path:

1. **You live in the home** for 2 years or more.
2. **You rent it out** and take depreciation.
3. **You sell within the window** in Publication 523, so you may still meet the ownership and use tests.
4. **You exclude the other gain**, but you still pay tax on the depreciation part.

Publication 523 also has rules on nonqualified use after 2008. It says the time after your last use as a main home, within the 5-year period before the sale, does not count as nonqualified use. Ask a tax professional to apply these rules to your dates.

## Where Does the IRS Explain This?

Three IRS publications cover the topic. Read them in this order:

| Publication | What it covers for recapture |
|---|---|
| Publication 527 | Depreciation of rental property, 27.5-year period, basis of a converted home |
| Publication 544 | Section 1250 property, allowed or allowable, unrecaptured section 1250 gain |
| Publication 523 | Home sale exclusion and the depreciation that it can't exclude |

Topic 409 gives the 25% maximum rate and the yearly income limits for the 0%, 15% and 20% rates.

## Can I Plan Around Depreciation Recapture?

You can plan for it, but not wish it away. Use this checklist before you sell:

- **Collect your depreciation schedules** from every past return.
- **Confirm the depreciation is correct**, including years you may have missed.
- **Run the numbers** with the table above, using your real figures.
- **Set cash aside** for the 25% part before you spend sale proceeds.
- **Ask about timing.** The year of sale changes your taxable income and so your rate.
