## What is market rent?

Market rent is the rent a unit can get today from a new tenant on the open market. Similar units nearby set it, not the owner's costs or the owner's mortgage. Two identical units on one street have about the same market rent, even if one owner paid twice as much for the building.

Market rent is an estimate. No list or website holds the "true" number for your unit. A property manager builds the estimate from recent rents of similar units, then tests it when the unit goes on the market.

HUD uses the same idea in the Housing Choice Voucher program. Under 24 CFR 982.507, a housing authority must compare the rent for a voucher unit with rents for "comparable unassisted units". It looks at location, quality, size, type and age, plus amenities, services, maintenance and utilities. That list is a good checklist for any rent comp.

## How do you run a rent comp analysis?

A rent comp analysis compares your unit with 3 to 6 similar units that rented recently, then adjusts for each difference. Follow these steps:

1. **Write down your unit's facts.** Beds, baths, square feet, floor, parking, laundry, outdoor space, condition, and which utilities the rent includes.
2. **Find recent comps.** Use units that rented in the last 60 to 90 days, within about 1 mile, of the same type. Signed rents are better than asking rents.
3. **Remove weak comps.** Drop units with a different bedroom count, a different building type, or a very different condition.
4. **Adjust each comp to your unit.** Add value where your unit is better. Subtract where the comp is better.
5. **Take the middle of the adjusted range.** Use the median, not the highest number.
6. **Test the number.** List at the estimate and watch inquiries and showings in the first 7 to 14 days.

## A worked rent comp example

The table below is an example with made-up numbers, not market data. The subject unit is a 2-bed, 1-bath unit with in-unit laundry and no parking.

| Comp | Rent | Laundry | Parking | Condition | Adjusted rent |
|------|------|---------|---------|-----------|---------------|
| A | $1,650 | Shared (+$50) | None | Same | $1,700 |
| B | $1,800 | In-unit | 1 space (−$75) | Same | $1,725 |
| C | $1,600 | In-unit | None | Older kitchen (+$75) | $1,675 |
| D | $1,900 | In-unit | None | New reno (−$150) | $1,750 |

The adjusted range is $1,675 to $1,750. The median is $1,712.50. A manager would list near $1,700 to $1,725 and watch the first 2 weeks.

Watch for: the adjustment values. Each one is a judgment. Base them on what local tenants pay for the feature, and write down why you chose each number. An owner can then check the logic.

## Market rent vs contract rent: what is the difference?

Contract rent is the rent written in the current lease. Market rent is what the unit would get today from a new tenant. The two drift apart over time.

| Term | What it is | Who sets it | When it changes |
|------|-----------|-------------|-----------------|
| Market rent | Estimate for one unit today | The market, read through comps | Every month |
| Contract rent | Rent in the signed lease | Owner and tenant | At renewal or new lease |
| Fair Market Rent (HUD) | Area-wide number for vouchers | HUD, each year | Once a year |
| Reasonable rent (HUD) | Rent a housing authority approves for one voucher unit | Housing authority | Before approval and before increases |

Fair Market Rent is not market rent for your unit. Under 24 CFR 888.113, HUD sets it at the 40th percentile of rents for standard units rented by recent movers. It includes utilities, except telephone. It excludes public housing and substandard units. So a well-kept unit can have a market rent above or below the area FMR. If you rent to voucher holders, see our guide to [Section 8 rent limits](/guides/section-8-rent-limits).

Gap to watch: when contract rent sits far below market rent, the owner loses income each month. When contract rent sits above market rent, the tenant may move at renewal. Both cost money.

## How often should rent be checked against the market?

Rent should be checked against the market at 3 points:

- **Before each new listing.** Run a fresh comp. Old comps miss recent moves.
- **60 to 90 days before each renewal.** The manager needs time to send a renewal offer and to meet any notice rule in the lease or local law.
- **When the market moves fast.** A new building nearby, a large employer, or a slow season can move rents in a few months.

HUD sets a similar rhythm for voucher units. Under 24 CFR 982.507, the housing authority must redetermine reasonable rent before any increase in rent to owner. It must also redetermine it when the FMR drops 10% or more, measured 60 days before the contract anniversary.

Some cities limit rent increases. A market rent estimate does not override a local rent cap. This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## What happens if you price above market rent?

A unit priced above market rent usually takes longer to lease. Each empty month costs 100% of that month's rent. The worked math below shows why a small premium rarely pays.

Example, with made-up numbers:

- Market rent: $1,700. Premium price: $1,800.
- Premium earns $100 x 12 months = $1,200 more per year, if a tenant signs.
- If the premium adds 1 extra vacant month, the owner loses $1,800.
- Net result: the owner is $600 behind for the year.

Signs that the price is too high:

- Few inquiries in the first 7 days.
- Showings, but no applications.
- Applicants ask for a concession, such as a free month.

What to do: cut to the comp median, or add a feature the comps lack. Do not wait 30 days to act on weak data.

## How a property manager reports market rent to owners

A good manager shows the work, not only the number. Ask your manager for:

- The list of comps, with addresses, rents, dates and sources.
- The adjustment for each difference, with a short reason.
- The final range and the recommended list price.
- A note on any rent cap or voucher rule that applies.
