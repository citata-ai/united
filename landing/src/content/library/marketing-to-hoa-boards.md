## How do HOA boards choose a management company?

Help an HOA board choose by making your service easy to inspect and compare. HOA means homeowners association. A board evaluating management needs more than a rental management pitch with a different heading.

An AppFolio and HOA-USA survey described in CAI sponsor content identified financial management, communication, and responsiveness as board concerns. Treat that source as vendor research, not a current census of all boards. Ask each prospect about its own needs.

Start discovery with the current workload. Ask which tasks remain with volunteers, which projects are stalled, and which reports are hard to use. Ask who can provide the request for proposals and how the board plans to compare replies.

Do not assume that an unhappy board wants a full service change. It may need accounting help, a project process, or a different communication routine. Explain what your firm offers and where it does not fit.

Use this suggested evidence table during discovery:

| Board concern | Evidence to provide | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Financial clarity | Redacted sample report | What is unclear in current reports? |
| Slow replies | Defined routing and escalation | Which requests get stuck? |
| Unfinished work | Sample project status record | Which handoffs cause delays? |
| Vendor oversight | Bid and approval workflow | Who approves scope and cost? |
| Transition risk | Records handoff checklist | What must transfer before service starts? |

These are proposed sales tools. Do not claim the table reflects a formal board scoring standard. Adapt it to the association's request and the services your staff can deliver.

## What should an HOA services page include?

An HOA services page should show association-specific scope, staff roles, service area, and a clear proposal route. Keep rental leasing and tenant screening content on the relevant rental pages. A board should not have to guess which services apply.

Describe financial reporting, assessment collection support, vendor work, meeting support, and resident communication where offered. Explain exclusions and tasks that remain with the board or counsel. Avoid implying that management takes over every board duty.

Show a sample report with private information removed. Label sample or invented data clearly. Explain who prepares the report and how the board can ask questions. Do not publish client financial records to prove that you do accounting.

Include the actual manager or team role that would serve the account. State how the board contacts the team and how urgent issues reach the right person. Publish only service promises your operating process can support.

Explain how a proposal request works. Ask for enough information to scope the work, without collecting resident records through a public sales form. Give the board a secure path for later due diligence documents.

Link to your [property management services](/property-management) only where the relationship helps readers understand your broader work. Keep the HOA offer distinct enough that a board can assess it on its own terms.

## Which proposals help boards compare HOA contracts?

A useful HOA proposal makes scope, exclusions, costs, and responsibilities easy to compare. Do not promise that a format will win the contract. The board's needs, selection process, and service fit will shape the result.

Begin with a short statement of the problems the board named. For each problem, show the proposed task, responsible role, information needed, and evidence of completion. Keep claims tied to a process the board can inspect.

List included services and separately charged work. Explain the conditions that change a fee. Do not hide an exclusion in a vague sentence about additional services. Ask the board to compare complete scope across proposals.

Add a transition plan covering records, funds, open projects, vendors, resident access, and staff contacts where relevant. State what the outgoing manager and board must supply. Do not promise a start date before you know the handoff requirements.

Use references only with consent. Help the board ask about relevant work rather than offering a list of unrelated properties. Keep confidentiality limits clear when sharing examples.

Have local counsel review the agreement and any statements about board duties, collections, meetings, records, or notices. This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## How can an HOA website become a lead source?

An HOA website can support trust by showing useful public information and a clear service contact route. Keep its main purpose centered on residents and the association. The community's site is not automatically a management company's sales asset.

Separate public information from private board and resident records. Do not publish contact lists, account details, disputes, or financial documents as marketing examples. Ask the association to approve public case material before using it.

On your own company site, publish checked guides for board tasks. A proposal checklist, report explainer, or handoff guide can give a board a reason to contact you. Build each resource around a question your team has heard.

Keep attribution on a community site modest and approved. Do not add promotional links or sales forms without the authority needed. Agree ownership, domain access, hosting, and exit steps before offering a website as part of management.

An association should not lose its records or website access when the management relationship ends. Put the handoff process into the scope review. Clear exit planning gives the board a concrete issue to assess before signing.

## How should you reach boards and measure progress?

Reach boards through relevant education, referrals, and respectful proposal work. Choose channels where association decision makers can review useful information. Avoid mass messages that pretend to know a community's private problems.

Ask local professional groups whether a report-reading or manager-handoff session would help their members. Work with qualified speakers where the topic involves legal duties. Keep the event useful even for a board that does not hire your company.

For commercial email, the FTC's CAN-SPAM guide covers sender accuracy, opt-out duties, and other requirements. Do not assume a board contact is exempt because the message concerns business. Check the full guide before outreach.

Track the path from inquiry to qualified discussion, requested proposal, board review, and signed association. Record why a proposal stopped. Separate fee objections from scope gaps and timing issues.

Use [the guide library](/guides) as a model for clear answers, not as a substitute for association-specific knowledge. Start with the last board question your sales team could not answer clearly. Build the proof that would let that board make a sound choice.
