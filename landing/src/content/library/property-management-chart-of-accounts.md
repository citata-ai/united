## Which accounts are company accounts and which are trust records?

Company accounts show the management business's own income, costs, assets and liabilities. Trust records track money held for other people under the applicable rules. Start by identifying who owns the funds, not by looking at the bank balance alone.

Buildium's chart-of-accounts guide describes grouping transactions through a consistent account structure. Use that structure to make reports clear, while keeping company books distinct from property and beneficiary records.

California DRE's trust-fund guidance describes a control record, separate beneficiary or transaction records, and reconciliation. Those records serve a legal purpose beyond a company profit report. Ask the responsible broker how the required records map to your system.

Use the generic starter chart below as a discussion tool. It is not a complete accounting policy, tax classification or legal form. Check local law and have an accountant adapt the chart.

```text
Company books
Assets
  Operating cash
  Earned fee receivables
  Prepaid company expenses
Liabilities
  Company accounts payable
  Payroll and tax liabilities
  Other company obligations
Equity
  Owner investment
  Retained company results
Income
  Management fees
  Leasing fees
  Renewal fees
  Approved coordination and service fees
Expenses
  Staff compensation
  Software
  Insurance
  Office and communications
  Marketing
  Professional services

Client and property records
Cash records
  Client cash by approved bank account
Beneficiary records
  Owner balances by property and owner
  Resident security deposit liabilities
  Other funds held for identified beneficiaries
Property activity
  Rent receipts
  Owner contributions
  Property expenses
  Management charges
  Owner distributions
Reconciliation records
  Bank reconciliation
  Control record review
  Beneficiary balance comparison
  Open differences with named resolution leads
```

Keep labels consistent across reports so staff do not confuse a fee with a transfer. Add clear definitions before adding account numbers.

## How do I code management fees?

Code management fees through a documented owner-side and company-side process. The owner's property record may show the management charge, while the company's books show the earned fee. The mapping should prevent duplicate income and unexplained transfers.

Ask the accountant when the fee is earned under your agreement and accounting method. Do not let the software's default date answer that question by accident. Keep the agreement and calculation available for review.

Record the fee base and any minimum separately in the billing setup. A percentage applied to collected rent differs from one applied to billed rent. Make the selected method visible to staff preparing owner statements.

Trace a completed fee from calculation to owner statement, transfer and company receipt. Check that the amount and period agree. Review adjustments through a controlled process rather than editing prior records without explanation.

IRS Publication 583 discusses business accounting methods and supporting records. Use that source to frame the recordkeeping conversation, while letting your accountant determine the correct treatment for the business.

This is general information, not tax advice. Check the current law or ask a tax professional.

For price planning, use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator). The calculator estimates charges; your accounting records must still show what the agreement actually earned and what the company received.

## How do I code markups and other service fees?

Code service fees by what the company earned and how the agreement describes the work. Keep a vendor bill distinct from the company's coordination charge. Do not hide additional income inside a broad repair expense label.

Ask the accountant whether the company acts as agent or principal for a particular service. That review can affect whether a report shows gross service revenue and cost or a separate fee. Do not decide based only on which presentation looks larger.

Use separate income categories when they help explain results. Leasing, renewal and project coordination can have different work patterns. Keep the chart compact enough that staff choose the right account consistently.

Attach the approval and source invoice to the transaction record. If an affiliate performed work, identify the provider and keep the required disclosure evidence. Have counsel review the applicable disclosure rules before billing.

Avoid using a miscellaneous account as the permanent home for unclear charges. Route uncertain items for review and resolve them before closing the period. A temporary holding item needs a person and a clear next action.

Review sample owner statements with staff who answer billing questions. They should be able to explain each line from the source documents without inventing an accounting story.

## How detailed should the chart be?

The chart should support required reports and management decisions without forcing staff to choose between nearly identical accounts. Use owner, property, unit and service fields for dimensions that do not need separate general-ledger accounts.

Write a short coding guide with examples and exclusions. Explain the difference between a property repair cost and a company office cost. Include how staff handle an invoice covering several properties.

Keep a controlled process for new accounts. Ask what report or decision the new category will improve. Check for an existing category before approving another one.

Map old accounts before a software change. Preserve historical report meaning where possible. A new name should not silently change the content of a year-to-date comparison.

Review uncoded items and unusual balances with the accountant. Reconcile transfers between systems as well as balances within each system. Matching a bank total alone does not show that every beneficiary record is correct.

California's trust-fund requirements are state-specific. This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.
