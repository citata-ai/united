## What is a capital expenditure on a rental?

A capital expenditure (CapEx) on a rental is money spent to improve the property, not just to keep it running. A new roof, a replaced furnace and a full bathroom remodel are CapEx. Fixing a leaky faucet is a repair.

The line matters for 3 reasons:

1. **Tax.** Repairs are deducted in the year paid. Improvements are capitalized and depreciated over years.
2. **Owner reports.** CapEx is shown apart from operating costs, so a roof year does not look like a bad operating year.
3. **Budgets.** CapEx is large and lumpy. Owners need a reserve to pay for it.

The IRS Schedule E instructions say it plainly: repairs and maintenance are deductible, improvements are not.

## Repair vs capital improvement: what is the line?

The line is set by the IRS tangible property regulations. An expense is an improvement, and so CapEx, if it is a betterment, a restoration or an adaptation. The IRS defines the 3 tests this way:

| Test | IRS meaning | Rental example |
|---|---|---|
| Betterment | Fixes a material defect, materially adds to or enlarges the property, or materially increases its capacity | Adding a bedroom; upgrading from window units to central air |
| Restoration | Replaces a major component, returns deteriorated property to working order, or rebuilds it to like-new | Replacing the whole roof; replacing the full plumbing system |
| Adaptation | Converts the property to a use not consistent with its original use | Turning a garage into a rental unit |

The test runs on each "unit of property". For a building, the IRS applies it to the building structure and to each key building system. The IRS lists plumbing, electrical, HVAC, elevators, escalators, fire protection and alarm, gas distribution, and security systems.

So scale matters. Replacing 1 broken HVAC fan motor is generally a repair. Replacing the entire HVAC system is a restoration of a building system, so it is CapEx.

### 3 safe harbors that move costs to "repair"

The IRS gives 3 safe harbors that let owners deduct some costs that might otherwise be capitalized:

1. **De minimis safe harbor.** Deduct purchases up to $2,500 per invoice or item if you have no applicable financial statement ($5,000 if you do). Elect it each year on your return.
2. **Small taxpayer safe harbor.** This is for owners with average annual gross receipts of $10 million or less. The building must have an unadjusted basis under $1 million. You can then deduct repairs and improvements on that building, up to a yearly cap. The cap is the lesser of 2% of the building's basis or $10,000.
3. **Routine maintenance safe harbor.** Deduct recurring work on a building that you expect to do more than once in 10 years. The 10 years start when the building is placed in service.

A worked example of the small taxpayer safe harbor. A rental house has an unadjusted basis of $300,000. 2% of $300,000 is $6,000, which is less than $10,000. So the limit is $6,000. If all repairs and improvements on that house total $5,400 for the year, the owner can elect to deduct all $5,400. If they total $7,000, the safe harbor does not apply to that year.

This is general information, not tax advice. Check the current law or ask a tax professional.

## How is CapEx depreciated?

CapEx on a rental building is depreciated, not deducted at once. IRS Publication 527 (2025) says that under the general depreciation system, an addition to residential rental property is depreciated "over 27.5 years".

A simple worked example. An owner replaces a roof for $16,500. Straight-line over 27.5 years, that is about $16,500 ÷ 27.5 = $600 a year. The real first-year amount is smaller because of the mid-month rule, so the tax preparer works out the exact figure. The point: a $16,500 cost becomes a yearly deduction of about $600, not $16,500 in one year.

Land is not depreciable, as the Schedule E instructions note. Only the building and its improvements are.

## How much should owners budget for CapEx?

Owners should budget for CapEx from a list of major components, their replacement costs and their remaining lives. A flat rule of thumb is a start, not a plan.

One lender benchmark is the Fannie Mae Multifamily Guide, section 105.01. For its multifamily loans, the minimum replacement reserve is the greater of a property calculation or "$250 per unit per year". That is a floor for loan underwriting, not a full budget for an older building.

Build a component budget instead. Here is a worked example for one single-family rental. The costs and lives are sample inputs; use local quotes and the age of each item.

| Component | Replacement cost | Expected life (years) | Years left | Yearly reserve |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Roof | $12,000 | 25 | 10 | $1,200 |
| HVAC system | $7,500 | 15 | 5 | $1,500 |
| Water heater | $1,800 | 12 | 4 | $450 |
| Flooring | $6,000 | 10 | 6 | $1,000 |
| Exterior paint | $5,000 | 10 | 5 | $1,000 |
| Appliances | $3,600 | 12 | 6 | $600 |
| Total | | | | $5,750 |

The math: yearly reserve = replacement cost ÷ years left. For the roof, $12,000 ÷ 10 = $1,200.

At a rent of $2,000 a month ($24,000 a year), this reserve is $5,750 ÷ $24,000 = about 24% of rent. That is high because the HVAC and water heater are near their end. In 5 years, after they are replaced, the yearly reserve will drop. A component budget shows that; a flat percent does not.

## How is CapEx shown on an owner report?

CapEx is shown on its own line, below net operating income (NOI) and above cash flow. This keeps the operating numbers clean and shows the owner where the cash went.

A sample annual owner report:

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Rental income | $24,000 |
| Operating expenses (taxes, insurance, repairs, management) | − $9,200 |
| Net operating income (NOI) | $14,800 |
| Capital expenditures (new water heater, roof section) | − $4,300 |
| Debt service | − $7,800 |
| Cash flow after CapEx and debt | $2,700 |

The math: $24,000 − $9,200 = $14,800 NOI. Then $14,800 − $4,300 − $7,800 = $2,700.

In your chart of accounts, post CapEx to a fixed asset account, not a repairs account. Attach the invoice and photos to each CapEx line, so the owner's tax preparer can set up depreciation.

## Is CapEx part of NOI?

No, CapEx is not part of NOI in most owner reports and valuations. NOI measures the income a property earns from operations, before debt and before capital spending. A roof replaced once in 25 years is not an operating cost of that year.

That is also why a big CapEx year does not lower the property's NOI or its value based on NOI. It does lower the owner's cash flow that year. Show both numbers so the owner sees the full picture.
