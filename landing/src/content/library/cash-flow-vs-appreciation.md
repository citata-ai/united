## What is the total return of a rental?

The total return of a rental is the sum of 4 parts, divided by the cash you put in. Each part behaves differently.

| Part | What it is | When you get it | Certain? |
|------|------------|-----------------|----------|
| Cash flow | Rent minus all costs and the mortgage | Monthly | Fairly, if rent and costs are realistic |
| Loan paydown | Principal your tenant's rent pays off | Monthly, as equity | Yes, while the loan is paid |
| Appreciation | Rise in the home's value | Only when you sell or refinance | No |
| Tax benefit | Depreciation that lowers tax on rent | At tax time | Depends on your tax situation |

Worked year-1 example, with example numbers. The rate comes from Freddie Mac's survey: the 30-year fixed rate averaged 7.40% on 2026-10-15. The appreciation rate is FHFA's latest 12-month national figure.

| Line | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Price | $300,000 |
| Cash invested (25% down plus $9,000 closing costs) | $84,000 |
| Loan | $225,000 at 7.40%, 30 years |
| Yearly cash flow (example) | $1,800 |
| Year-1 principal paydown | $2,115 |
| Appreciation at 2.6% | $7,800 |
| Total, before tax benefit | $11,715 |
| Total return on cash | 13.9% |
| Cash flow only, on cash | 2.1% |

The total looks strong. But 67% of it is appreciation, which is not cash and not certain.

## How does equity paydown add to return?

Equity paydown adds to return because part of each mortgage payment cuts the loan balance. The tenant's rent pays it, and you keep the equity.

On the example loan of $225,000 at 7.40% for 30 years:

| Period | Principal paid | Loan balance after |
|--------|----------------|--------------------|
| Year 1 | $2,115 | $222,885 |
| Years 1 to 5 | $12,323 | $212,677 |

The monthly payment stays at $1,558, but the principal share grows each month as interest falls. At high rates, paydown is slow in the early years. At lower rates, it is faster.

Paydown is real wealth, but you cannot spend it without selling or borrowing against the home.

## What risks come with an appreciation-only plan?

An appreciation-only plan buys a home that loses money each month, in the hope that a price rise covers the loss. It works when prices rise fast. It hurts when they do not.

Same house, 5 years, 3 price paths. The cash flow is set to −$3,000 a year to model an appreciation-only buy. Selling costs and tax are left out.

| 5-year result | Prices +2.6%/yr | Prices flat | Prices −5%/yr |
|---------------|-----------------|-------------|---------------|
| Cash flow | −$15,000 | −$15,000 | −$15,000 |
| Loan paydown | $12,323 | $12,323 | $12,323 |
| Appreciation | $41,081 | $0 | −$67,866 |
| Net result | $38,404 | −$2,677 | −$70,543 |

The risks behind that table:

1. **Price stalls.** FHFA reported US prices up 2.1% from the second quarter of 2025 to the second quarter of 2026. A slow year does not cover a $250 monthly loss for long.
2. **Forced sale.** A job loss or large repair can make you sell when prices are down.
3. **Vacancy.** A negative cash flow gets worse in an empty month. The Census Bureau put the national rental vacancy rate at 7.3% in the second quarter of 2026.
4. **Rate risk.** An adjustable loan can reset higher.
5. **Tax on the gain.** When you sell, IRS Topic 409 says unrecaptured section 1250 gain, mostly from past depreciation, is taxed at up to 25%. The appreciation you "made" shrinks after tax.

## Cash flow plan vs appreciation plan

| Question | Cash flow plan | Appreciation plan |
|----------|----------------|-------------------|
| Typical market | Lower prices relative to rent | Higher prices relative to rent |
| Monthly result | Positive or break-even | Often negative at first |
| Reserves needed | Normal | Large, to carry losses |
| Main risk | Weak price growth; more repairs on older homes | Prices stall or fall |
| Best for | Owners who need income or have thin reserves | Owners with strong income, long horizon and reserves |
| Exit flexibility | Can hold through a downturn | May be forced to sell |

A balanced rule many careful owners use: buy at break-even cash flow or better, with conservative rent and full costs. Then any price growth is a bonus, not a need.

## How do I compare both on my own house?

Compare both on your own house by filling in the same 4 lines for 3 price paths. It takes 15 minutes.

Worksheet:

```text
MY RENTAL: 5-YEAR VIEW
Cash invested:                         $______
Yearly cash flow (after all costs):    $______  x 5 = $______
Principal paid in 5 years:             $______  (from your loan amortization table)
Current value:                         $______

                         Up ___%/yr   Flat    Down ___%/yr
Appreciation (5 years)   $______      $0      -$______
Cash flow (5 years)      $______      $______  $______
Paydown (5 years)        $______      $______  $______
Total                    $______      $______  $______
Total / cash invested    ____%        ____%    ____%
```

How to read it:

- **If the flat case is positive,** the home pays its way without price help.
- **If the flat case is negative,** check that your reserves can cover 5 years of losses.
- **If the down case would force a sale,** reduce the risk: a bigger down payment, a lower price, or a different home.

Add the tax benefit last. IRS Publication 527 says residential rental property is depreciated over 27.5 years. Ask a tax professional what that saves you.

To improve cash flow on a home you already own, check the cost lines first. Management is often one of the largest. See what yours costs with our [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator).
