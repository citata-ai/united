## How do self-guided photo inspections work?

A self-guided photo inspection gives the tenant a defined set of views and questions to complete. The manager then checks the submission and routes any issues. The workflow should make reporting easier without asking tenants to perform unsafe tests.

Write instructions for each room and fixture. Ask for a wide view that shows location and a close view that shows the concern. Tell the tenant to describe what they observed. A report of a leak needs words as well as an image.

Offer a way to say a task is unsafe, unclear or not possible. Do not ask tenants to open electrical panels, climb onto roofs or move heavy appliances. Provide a contact for urgent issues outside the inspection form. A submitted photo should not become the only way to request a repair.

Keep the capture record tied to the unit, date and person who submitted it. Check that files actually uploaded before closing the task. A completed status does not prove every room was covered. Ask for missing context or arrange a visit when the record is incomplete.

## What can AI flag in photos?

AI can be tested as a tool for suggesting images that need review. Do not assume a product can detect a specific defect until you test it. A vendor demonstration does not measure performance in your homes.

NIST's Generative AI Profile describes the risk of confidently stated false output. It also warns about overreliance on automated output. Apply that warning to any system that writes condition descriptions from photos. A fluent report can still misread an image.

| Proposed AI task | Human check | Decision the AI should not make alone |
|---|---|---|
| Sort room photos | Confirm the room and unit | Whether coverage is complete |
| Suggest visible changes | Compare original views | Whether the tenant caused damage |
| Draft a repair note | Check facts and urgency | Whether the home is safe |
| Find missing views | Review the capture list | Whether inspection duties are met |
| Draft a summary | Match each statement to evidence | Whether a deposit charge is lawful |

Use a sample of your actual property images to test the workflow. Include poor lighting, reflected light and partly blocked views. Record false alerts and missed issues. Keep the final decision with a named reviewer.

## When is an in-person visit still needed?

Use an in-person visit when the question requires physical testing, safe access or a qualified judgment that photos cannot provide. Do not wait for an AI score when a tenant reports an urgent condition.

A photograph cannot establish that an appliance completes its cycle or that a hidden pipe stays dry. A clean-looking wall does not establish the cause of a stain. Those are limits of the evidence. Route the question to the person who can perform the needed check.

Create clear visit triggers in your operating policy. Possible triggers include repeated leaks, uncertain damage, missing views and a tenant request for help. These are proposed controls, not a legal inspection schedule. Check your local access and notice rules before scheduling entry.

Combine reporting methods when useful. A tenant can describe an issue by phone while staff review a photo. A contractor can inspect the source and attach a repair note. Keep those records together so a later reviewer sees the full sequence.

Do not punish a tenant for lacking a suitable phone or internet connection. Offer another reporting route. A process that only works for a comfortable phone user will leave gaps in your condition records.

## Will the photos hold up in a deposit dispute?

Photos can support a deposit dispute when they show relevant condition and connect to a lawful charge. They do not decide the dispute by themselves. Preserve the originals and the context needed to explain them.

Washington RCW 59.18.280 requires documentation supporting damage charges and bars retention for ordinary wear under its terms. California Courts also explains that deductions cannot cover normal wear. These state examples show why a damage label alone is not enough.

Match the move-in and move-out location. A close-up without a room label can be hard to interpret later. Keep the condition checklist, repair order and cost evidence beside the image. Explain what changed and which portion needed work.

Do not use generated fill, object removal or other AI edits on evidence photographs. If you brighten a viewing copy or add a pointer, keep it separate. Label the copy so no one mistakes it for the original. Maintain the original export and available capture details.

Check the applicable deposit statement and delivery rules. A well-organized photo file does not extend a statutory deadline. Have counsel review contested charges before you send them.

## How should I run a controlled pilot?

Run a pilot with a narrow purpose and a manual review path. Choose routine documentation before relying on automation for more serious decisions. Write down what a successful result would show.

Assign a reviewer to compare the tool's draft with the source images. Record corrections and missed context. Measure the time needed for capture, review and follow-up together. Do not count only the time the software takes to draft a report.

Review the provider's handling of tenant images. Ask who can access them, whether they train models and how deletion works. Keep private documents and people outside the frame where possible. Provide clear instructions before tenants begin the task.

Keep a backup process. If the tool fails, staff still need to receive reports, arrange visits and save evidence. Test that process before rollout. Use our [guide library](/guides) for wider operating procedures.

Start with a sample inspection file. Check whether another employee can identify the unit, condition, required action and supporting evidence without asking the original reviewer. If not, improve the record before expanding the process.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.
