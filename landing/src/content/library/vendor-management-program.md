## Which Documents Should Vendors Give You?

Ask for records that let you verify identity, job authority, insurance and payment details. A folder full of unchecked documents is not a screening program.

Buildium's vendor screening guide recommends reviewing location, hours, licenses, insurance and experience. Use those categories as the start of a work-specific review. Its older guide does not establish current legal requirements for your trade or location.

Request the vendor's legal business name, contact person and service area. Ask for a current certificate of insurance, or COI, and the license details relevant to the work. Use the issuing body's lookup where available. Ask your insurance adviser what evidence and coverage fit your contracts.

Ask accounting which tax form and payee records it needs. Use a secure route for sensitive tax and bank data. A completed W-9 can support your records, but it does not prove competence or insurance coverage.

Check references for similar work. Ask about scope changes, resident access and invoice disputes. Record the result rather than a simple approved stamp. Set review dates for records that expire, and block work that needs a missing approval.

## How Do You Set Vendor Rates?

Agree the rate structure and change rules before the vendor accepts a work order. The cheapest starting rate can hide travel, minimums and return costs.

Request a written rate card or job quote. Separate labor, materials, travel, diagnostics and after-hours charges. Ask whether the diagnostic fee remains when the vendor completes the repair. Define how the vendor gets approval when the original diagnosis changes.

Use comparable scopes when seeking bids. A repair quote and a replacement quote solve different problems. Ask each vendor to state the condition found, proposed work and exclusions. Let the owner see a clear decision when approval is needed.

Create a work-order template with these fields:

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Reported issue | Preserve the resident's description |
| Approved scope | State what work the vendor may perform |
| Spending authority | Show the limit and approval route |
| Access plan | Confirm scheduling and key handling |
| Completion evidence | Request notes, photos and testing |
| Change approval | Record who approved added work |
| Invoice reference | Connect payment with the work order |

If your company adds a coordination charge, explain it in the owner terms. Keep the vendor's invoice separate from your own fee. A clear record helps resolve a pricing question without hiding either charge.

## How Should You Score Vendor Performance?

Score vendors on reliable completion, communication and repair quality, with cost in context. Do not rank them by speed or price alone.

Track whether the vendor accepted the job, kept the agreed visit and reported changes promptly. Record work that needed a repeat visit for the same unresolved issue. Distinguish a failed repair from a new defect or an owner-approved temporary fix.

Review completion notes before marking the job closed. A paid invoice does not prove the repair worked. Ask for evidence suited to the task. A photo may confirm an installed item but not prove that an intermittent fault has stopped.

Record resident feedback about access and communication. Check complaints against the job history before drawing conclusions. A vendor should have a chance to explain a delay caused by unavailable parts or denied access.

Review results by trade and job type. Emergency plumbing and planned painting have different work patterns. Compare like jobs and keep enough context to avoid penalizing a vendor for taking difficult work. Use the score to decide training, scope limits or removal from the approved list.

## How Many Vendors Do You Need Per Trade?

You need enough approved capacity to cover demand and the loss of a provider. The right count depends on geography, job volume and emergency coverage.

Start with the jobs you actually receive. Map where properties sit and when service is needed. Ask vendors what work they accept, their travel limits and when they decline calls. A long vendor list gives little protection if everyone refuses the same night job.

Build backup coverage for critical trades. Test the backup through a real, suitable job before relying on emergency availability. Keep the dispatch contact and escalation route current. A generic inbox may not reach the person who can authorize an urgent visit.

Check concentration risk. If a provider handles most of a critical trade, ask what happens during illness, staffing loss or a busy weather event. Your plan should identify a substitute and the approval needed to use that provider.

Avoid promising constant work to every approved vendor. Be clear about demand and selection. A small, active group with known capacity can be more useful than an unused directory. Revisit coverage when your service area or property mix changes.

## How Do You Run the Program Day to Day?

Use the same file, dispatch and payment checks for routine work and reviewed exceptions. Emergencies need a fast route, not an undocumented route.

- Confirm the vendor is approved for the trade and location.
- Send a work order with scope and access instructions.
- Record owner approvals and any spending exception.
- Tell the resident how to confirm the appointment.
- Check completion evidence and unresolved issues.
- Match the invoice with the approved work before payment.

Limit access to resident data and keys to the work needed. Remove access when the job ends or the vendor leaves the program. Record lost keys or access concerns promptly and follow your incident process.

Ask counsel and your insurer to review contract language, insurance terms and trade-specific duties. This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

Use the [fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) when discussing your own service charges with owners. Start the vendor program by reviewing the file for a provider you already use often.
