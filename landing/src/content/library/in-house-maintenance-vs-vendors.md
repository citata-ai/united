## In-house team vs outside vendors at a glance

An in-house team gives you control of speed and quality. Outside vendors give you flexible cost and licensed trades. Most growing firms end up with a mix.

| Factor | In-house technician | Outside vendors |
|---|---|---|
| Cost model | Fixed: salary, benefits, vehicle, tools | Variable: pay per job |
| Response time | You set the schedule | Depends on the vendor's load |
| Licensed trades | Only if the tech holds the license | Licensed plumbers, electricians, HVAC |
| Quality control | Direct: you train and check | Indirect: you choose and rate vendors |
| Slow months | You still pay the tech | You pay nothing |
| Admin load | Payroll, insurance, fleet, scheduling | Vendor onboarding, insurance certificates |
| Owner trust risk | Owner may doubt your rate | Owner may doubt the vendor choice |

Rentvine, a property management software company, calls internal maintenance "one of the hardest areas" in property management. Its point: without one system to assign, schedule and bill tech work, teams fall back on spreadsheets and workarounds. Plan the software and billing before you hire.

## What does one technician cost?

One technician costs the wage plus benefits, payroll taxes, a vehicle, tools and insurance. In the worked example below, the wage is only 60% of the full cost.

The facts we use:

- **Wage.** The BLS median for general maintenance and repair workers was $49,590 a year, or $23.84 an hour, in May 2025.
- **Benefits.** US private industry employers paid $46.89 per hour worked in total compensation in June 2026. Benefits were $14.07 of that, a 30.0% share (BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation).
- **Licensed trade context.** The BLS median for plumbers, pipefitters and steamfitters was $63,800 a year, or $30.67 an hour, in May 2025.

## Worked example: when does a tech pay off?

This example uses stated inputs. Only the wage and benefit share come from BLS. Replace the example inputs with your own numbers.

| Input | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Tech wage | $49,590 a year | BLS median, May 2025 |
| Benefit share of total cost | 30% | BLS ECEC, June 2026 |
| Vehicle, fuel, tools, insurance | $12,000 a year | example input |
| Paid hours | 2,080 a year | 40 hours × 52 weeks |
| Billable share of paid hours | 65% | example input |
| Vendor rate for the same work | $95 an hour | example input |
| Work orders per door per year | 4 | example input |
| Share of work orders a non-licensed tech can do | 50% | example input |
| Hours per work order | 2 | example input |

The steps:

1. Loaded labor cost: $49,590 ÷ 0.70 = $70,843.
2. Full yearly cost: $70,843 + $12,000 = $82,843.
3. Billable hours: 2,080 × 0.65 = 1,352.
4. Cost per billable hour: $82,843 ÷ 1,352 = $61.27.
5. Gap against the vendor: $95 − $61.27 = $33.73 an hour.
6. Tech hours each door creates: 4 × 50% × 2 = 4 hours a year.
7. Doors needed to fill the tech: 1,352 ÷ 4 = 338 doors.

At about 340 doors, the tech is full, and each hour costs $33.73 less than the vendor. Below that, the tech has idle hours that you still pay for. At 170 doors, half the billable hours sit empty, and the true cost per job hour doubles to about $122.

Two inputs move the answer most: the billable share and the vendor rate. Track them for 3 months before you hire. Your work order software can export hours by job type.

## What insurance and licenses do I need?

You need workers' compensation, liability cover for the work, and trade licenses for any licensed work the tech does. The rules come from your state and city.

- **Workers' compensation.** The US Department of Labor says private-sector workers injured on the job should contact their state workers' compensation board. The state sets the rules for employers.
- **Trade licenses.** BLS says complex work, such as electrical or plumbing tasks, may require a license, and that "licensing requirements vary by state and locality." It also says most states and some localities require plumbers to be licensed.
- **Employee or contractor.** The IRS looks at behavioral control, financial control and the type of relationship. A tech who works only for you, on your schedule, with your tools, looks like an employee. Classify the role before you pay.
- **Vehicle and tools.** Commercial auto cover for company vehicles, and cover for tools and stock.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## How do I avoid conflicts of interest?

Avoid conflicts by telling owners in writing that you run in-house maintenance, at what rate, and how you choose between your team and a vendor.

NARPM's Code of Ethics, Standard 3-4, says members accept "no commissions, rebates, profits, discounts, or any other benefit which has not been fully disclosed to and approved by the Client." An in-house team earns a profit on labor. So the owner must know and agree.

Practical steps:

1. **Put the hourly rate and trip charge in the management agreement.**
2. **Show tech labor and materials as separate lines** on the owner statement.
3. **Set a bid rule.** For jobs above a set amount, get one outside quote too.
4. **Keep the maintenance company's books apart.** Rentvine notes that many firms run maintenance under a separate LLC or EIN, and its software can set the maintenance LLC as the payee.
5. **Publish your rates** on your fee page, so owners see them before they sign.

## Where to start

Start with a part-time or shared tech for turns and small repairs, and keep vendors for licensed trades and after-hours calls. Measure the billable share for a quarter. Then decide on a full-time hire with your own numbers in the table above.
