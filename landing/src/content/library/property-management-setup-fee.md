## What Work Happens During Owner Onboarding?

Owner onboarding should turn the signed scope into records, balances and tasks your team can use. Price the actual work before choosing a setup fee.

Rentec Direct describes setup fees as a possible charge for starting the management relationship. Its fee guide separates onboarding from monthly service and leasing. That supports a useful distinction, but it does not set your price.

Start with the property file. Request the signed lease, amendments, resident contact details, condition reports, warranties and open work orders. Ask for the current rent ledger and the status of deposits. Mark missing records as open tasks rather than assuming that no record means no issue.

Next, map the owner's decisions. Confirm repair approval limits, emergency contacts, reporting needs and the funding process. Record who can approve work when the owner is away. Give the team a clear contact list.

Finish with a handoff review. Compare the records with what sales promised. Flag unsafe conditions, unresolved resident issues and disputed balances for the right person. A setup charge should not imply that every property problem will be solved during intake.

## Should the Setup Fee Apply Per Property or Per Unit?

Choose the price basis from the work that creates cost. An owner account, a building and an occupied unit create different tasks.

Use this planning table to sort the work. It is a suggested pricing method, not a market fee schedule.

| Work | Main cost driver | Possible price basis |
|---|---|---|
| Owner identity and agreement file | Owner account | Account charge |
| Building records and access | Property | Property charge |
| Lease and balance review | Occupied unit | Unit charge |
| Missing record recovery | Condition of records | Separate approved scope |
| Site condition review | Travel and building needs | Property quote |

Time completed intake work by task. Include review, corrections and owner questions, not just data entry. Use your staff cost and outside costs to set a floor. Add a margin that supports the service you promise.

Do not charge the same work under different names without an explanation. If a placement fee includes preparing the property file, show how setup differs. An owner should see where each service starts and ends.

For a portfolio transfer, quote after checking sample files. A large number of units can share some account work. Poor records can still make each lease review slow. Size alone does not tell you the full cost.

## Does a Setup Fee Lose Owner Leads?

A setup fee can affect sales, but you need your own records to judge the result. Do not assume that removing it improves the business.

Track why owners decline the offer. Separate a concern about the fee from a concern about unclear work. An owner may accept the price after seeing the intake checklist. Another may prefer to keep managing the property alone.

Review the full offer with the owner. Explain the monthly charge, placement charge and any work outside scope. A small setup fee can feel unfair when it appears late. A larger disclosed fee can still be a poor fit if it covers little useful work.

Measure expected margin on signed accounts. Include the time spent selling and onboarding them. A fee waiver can bring work that takes too long to serve. A charged setup fee can also fail if staff repeatedly redo the same intake tasks.

Use the [fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to discuss the wider cost picture. Then show a written scope for setup. A calculator does not replace the agreed price or the specific property review.

## Can You Waive the Setup Fee as an Offer?

You can consider a waiver when the work cost and approval terms are clear. Treat the waiver as a funded sales decision.

Write down who can approve a waiver. State which charge disappears and which services remain included. Avoid vague promises that all start-up work is free. Special inspections or missing records may still need separate approval.

Use objective offer terms that staff can apply consistently. An example could be a defined portfolio intake package with records supplied in the required format. The condition should relate to work cost or service fit. Do not base housing decisions on protected traits.

Check whether an early exit would leave intake cost unpaid. Decide how your pricing handles that risk before the sale. Have local counsel review any repayment clause. Do not invent a cancellation charge after the owner gives notice.

Keep waived fees visible in internal records. This lets you compare the full-price and waived groups without guessing. Review service cost and owner concerns alongside sales results.

## What Should the Setup Fee Process Look Like?

Use a clear intake process that shows what has been earned and what remains open. This checklist is a suggested workflow.

- List the records and access items needed before takeover.
- Define setup tasks, exclusions and the billing trigger in writing.
- Confirm the quote and any waiver before work starts.
- Assign each intake task to a named team member.
- Check imported balances against the transfer records.
- Send the owner a list of completed work and remaining issues.

Do not mark intake complete just because the software account exists. Check whether the resident knows where to send requests. Confirm that open repairs have an owner. Make sure accounting knows about disputed funds.

When intake fails, record the reason. Missing records, unclear scope and staff training need different fixes. Use those findings to improve the checklist before increasing the charge.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney. Before you send your next proposal, compare the setup scope with the work your team actually performs.
