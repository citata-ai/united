## What is a maintenance markup or coordination fee?

A maintenance markup adds a charge to the vendor cost of a repair. A coordination fee can instead charge for arranging and tracking the work. The contract should explain the name, amount, and service covered.

Ask whether the fee covers dispatch, quotes, tenant access, completion checks, and invoice review. Ask whether the manager charges it on work handled by an outside vendor, an affiliated firm, or its own staff. Different routes need clear prices.

Here is an assumed comparison, not a market average:

| Repair charge | Percentage markup example | Flat coordination example |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Vendor bill | $800 | $800 |
| Added fee | 10%, or $80 | $60 |
| Owner total | $880 | $860 |

The lower total does not prove better service. Compare what each fee covers and whether other charges apply. A cheap dispatch fee can still leave you to manage the repair.

California DRE's hiring guide advises owners to read the management agreement. Use that step to find all repair-related charges. Do not rely on a sales summary that mentions only the monthly management fee.

## What is a normal repair approval limit?

A repair approval limit should match your property, cash reserve, and agreed service process. There is no universal amount that makes a contract fair. Ask what the proposed limit permits the manager to do without contacting you.

Define the limit per incident or project. A rule that applies only per invoice can be unclear when one repair needs several trades. Ask how the manager groups related work and what happens if a quote grows.

Separate routine repairs from urgent action to prevent harm. The agreement should explain how the manager reaches you, what it can authorize, and what records follow. Have local counsel check any legal duties that affect the terms.

Give the manager reliable contact details and a backup decision-maker. A strict approval rule does not work if nobody can respond. State your preferred channel and what information an approval request must contain.

For a planned project, ask for scope, total price, fee, schedule, and warranty terms. Approve the full owner cost rather than only the vendor estimate. Keep the approval with the work order so the statement review can follow the same record.

## Can you use your own contractor?

Your contractor can fit the process if the management agreement allows it and the roles are clear. Ask before signing rather than during an urgent repair. The manager may need insurance, license, tax, and contact records.

Clarify who schedules access and who supervises the work. Tell the contractor where to send the invoice and who can approve added work. Avoid giving instructions that conflict with the manager's scope.

Ask whether the coordination fee changes when you choose the vendor. If the manager still arranges access and checks completion, some agreed service may remain. If you manage the entire job, ask which fee still applies and why.

Set a rule for callbacks. A contractor's return visit might be warranty work or a new problem. Ask the manager to check before it authorizes another paid visit. Keep model numbers, photos, and warranty papers with the property record.

Choose on total service and documented work. A personal connection does not replace a clear scope or suitable insurance. Keep tenant communication with the assigned contact so access and repair updates remain consistent.

## How do you see the vendor's real invoice?

Ask for the original vendor invoice and a separate explanation of any manager charge. The record should connect the reported problem, approved scope, completed work, and final owner total. Ask how you can download that record.

An invoice should identify the vendor, property, work date, and work performed. Request enough detail to distinguish labor, materials, and repeat visits. Ask about discounts or credits that change the final cost.

Related vendors need an added question: does the manager own or receive compensation from the repair business? Ask for written disclosure and a full price basis. Do not assume an affiliated company is automatically cheaper or more expensive.

For tax records, IRS Topic 414 distinguishes repairs from improvements. Keep the work scope with the invoice so your preparer can review the category. A markup follows the project record rather than proving that the whole job is a current repair expense.

If your contract does not explain record access, resolve that gap now. A portal summary can help you scan costs. It should not be the only record available when you need to understand a large job.

## How should you compare maintenance terms?

Compare the full repair process alongside the fee. Use the same assumed job for every manager. Ask each firm to show the owner charge and the documents you would receive.

Use this checklist in the discussion:

- Confirm the fee base and any minimum charge.
- Ask whether the fee applies to taxes, permits, and materials.
- Check routine and emergency approval rules.
- Confirm who handles warranty callbacks.
- Ask about affiliated vendors and referral payments.
- Confirm access to invoices and completion photos.
- Ask how disputes and refunds appear on your statement.

Use the [management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) for the broader contract cost. Add repair scenarios outside the ordinary management month. Keep your own reserve for work regardless of the fee model.

Choose a manager that can explain the charge before the work starts and prove the result after it ends. Put the agreed answers into the contract or signed service schedule. Save sales emails as background, but make the signed terms clear enough to use during a real repair.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.
