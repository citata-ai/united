## What does the Fair Housing Act require of a PM company?

The Fair Housing Act bars discrimination in the rental of housing because of race, color, religion, sex, familial status, national origin or disability (42 U.S.C. 3604). A property management company acts for the owner, so its staff, ads and rules all fall under the Act.

The risk is real money. A person may file a complaint with HUD up to 1 year after the act (42 U.S.C. 3610). They may sue in court up to 2 years after it, and a court may award actual and punitive damages (42 U.S.C. 3613).

Federal law is the floor. Many states and cities add protected classes. Check your state and local law and add those classes to every part of this checklist.

| Area | Main federal rule | What you must not do |
|---|---|---|
| Ads and statements | 42 U.S.C. 3604(c), 24 CFR 100.75 | Show a preference or limit based on a protected class |
| Screening and terms | 42 U.S.C. 3604(a), (b) | Refuse to rent or set different terms because of a protected class |
| Disability | 42 U.S.C. 3604(f)(3)(B) | Refuse a reasonable accommodation in rules, policies or services |
| Poster | 24 CFR 110.10 | Fail to post the fair housing poster where you offer rentals |
| Credit-based denials | FCRA, as the FTC explains it | Deny without the adverse action notice |

## Which staff need fair housing training?

Every person who talks to applicants, residents or owners needs fair housing training. That includes leasing agents, portfolio managers, maintenance staff, the front desk and virtual assistants. Maintenance staff count too: a tech who says the wrong thing in a unit creates the same risk as an agent.

Training checklist:

- [ ] Train each new hire before their first applicant contact.
- [ ] Repeat training at least once a year for all staff.
- [ ] Include contractors and offshore assistants who answer calls or messages.
- [ ] Cover the federal classes plus every state and local class where you work.
- [ ] Cover how to spot and pass on a reasonable accommodation request.
- [ ] Cover the words to avoid in ads, calls and messages.
- [ ] Keep a signed record of each session: date, topic, trainer, attendees.
- [ ] Test staff with 3 to 5 real call scenarios, not only a video.

The federal sources in this checklist set no training hour rule for managers. Your state license law may set continuing education rules. Check your state real estate commission.

## How do I review ads and screening criteria?

Review each ad and each screening rule before it goes live. Look at words and at effects. HUD's rule at 24 CFR 100.75 bars ads that convey that dwellings are or are not available to a group. It also bars a choice of media or locations that denies parts of the market information about housing.

Ad review checklist:

- [ ] Describe the property, not the people you want. Say "2 bedrooms, near a park", not "ideal for a young couple".
- [ ] Remove words about religion, family size, age of children, origin or disability.
- [ ] Add an equal housing opportunity statement to each ad.
- [ ] Post the same listing on the same channels for every unit type. Do not pick channels to reach or avoid a group.
- [ ] Check AI-written listings by hand. A model can add biased words you did not write.
- [ ] Keep a copy of each ad and where it ran.

Screening checklist:

- [ ] Write one set of screening criteria per property: income, credit, rental history, criminal history policy.
- [ ] Apply the same criteria to every applicant, in order of application.
- [ ] Show the criteria to applicants before they pay a fee.
- [ ] Review criteria for rules that hit a protected class harder without a real business need, such as blanket bans.
- [ ] Send the FCRA adverse action notice when a screening report drives a denial. The FTC says it must name the screening company, say that company did not make the decision, and explain the right to a free report within 60 days and to dispute errors.
- [ ] If you used a credit score, add the score, its source and range, and the key factors that hurt it (FTC).

## How do I handle reasonable accommodation requests?

Answer each reasonable accommodation request fast, in writing, and without fees. A reasonable accommodation is a change in a rule, policy, practice or service that a person with a disability needs to use and enjoy the home (42 U.S.C. 3604(f)(3)(B)). An example: a reserved parking space near the door, or an exception to a no-pets rule for an assistance animal.

The HUD and DOJ joint statement (2004) sets the main steps:

1. **Accept any form.** The Act does not require a request in a particular form. Treat an oral request or an email as a valid request.
2. **Log the request.** Write down the date, the request and who received it.
3. **Ask only what you need.** If the disability and the need are obvious, you may not ask for more information. If they are not obvious, you may ask for reliable information that shows the disability and the link to the request. Medical records are usually not needed.
4. **Charge nothing extra.** You may not require extra fees or deposits for the accommodation.
5. **Talk before you deny.** If a request is not reasonable, discuss other options with the person. The joint statement calls this an interactive process.
6. **Answer in writing.** Send the decision and the reason. Keep a copy.

Train staff to pass every request to one trained person. A tech who says "no pets, sorry" to a resident with an assistance animal has made a decision for the company.

## Which records should I keep?

Keep records that show you treated each person by the same rules. If a complaint comes, these records are your evidence. Federal law sets no single period, but the 2-year limit to sue (42 U.S.C. 3613) means 2 years is the minimum that makes sense. Keep longer where your state requires it.

| Record | What to keep | Suggested minimum |
|---|---|---|
| Ads | Text, photos, channels, dates | 2 years |
| Screening criteria | Each version with its start date | 2 years after replaced |
| Applications | All applications, approved and denied, with reason | 2 years |
| Adverse action notices | Copy of each notice sent | 2 years |
| Accommodation requests | Request, information asked, decision, dates | 2 years after move-out |
| Training | Sign-in sheets, materials, test results | 2 years |
| Complaints | Each complaint and what you did | 2 years after closed |

The poster is a record too, in a way. Under 24 CFR 110.10, a broker or agent who rents a single-family home must post the fair housing poster at the place of business where it is offered. Post it in your office and add it to your website.
