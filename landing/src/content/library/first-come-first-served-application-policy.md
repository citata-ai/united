## What is a first-come, first-served policy?

A first-come, first-served policy means you screen complete applications in the order they arrive. You offer the unit to the first applicant who meets every criterion you published. You do not rank qualified applicants against each other.

The policy answers a hard question with a simple rule. Two applicants both pass your income, credit and rental history bars. Who gets the unit? Under this policy, the one whose complete application came first.

Why landlords use it:

- **It is easy to explain.** Each applicant knows the rule before applying.
- **It limits bias.** You do not make a judgment call between two qualified people.
- **It leaves a record.** The time stamps show why you chose one applicant.
- **It is fast.** You stop screening when the first qualified applicant accepts.

The Fair Housing Act, at 42 U.S.C. 3604, bars a refusal to rent because of race, color, religion, sex, familial status or national origin. Section 3604(f) also bars discrimination because of a handicap (disability). A clear order of review helps you show that none of these played a role.

## Which cities require first-in-time rules?

Seattle and Portland, Oregon, have laws that require landlords to process applications in order of receipt. Most other places leave the choice to you.

| City | Rule | Key steps | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seattle, WA | First-in-time, SMC 14.08.050 | Give notice of criteria and the minimum for each. Record when each complete application arrives. Screen in order. Offer the unit to the first qualified applicant, who has 48 hours to accept. | Chong Yim CNA LLC v. City of Seattle (2019); Seattle Office for Civil Rights |
| Portland, OR | Application and screening, PCC 30.01.086 | Publish the rental notice at least 72 hours before you take applications. Record the date and time of each complete application. Accept, conditionally accept or deny in order of receipt. | Portland City Code 30.01.086 |

The Seattle rule has exceptions. The Washington Supreme Court opinion lists an accessory unit where the owner lives on the same lot, and units set aside for specific vulnerable groups. It also lets applicants with disabilities get extra time to complete an application.

The Seattle rule survived court review. The Washington Supreme Court upheld it on November 14, 2019. The City of Seattle reports that the U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear an appeal on April 20, 2020.

In Portland, an application that arrives before the open period counts as received 8 hours after the period starts, under PCC 30.01.086(C)(2)(a)(2). Check your city for similar rules before you list a unit.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## Can I pick the higher income?

Federal law does not stop you from setting a higher income bar, if you set it before you take applications and apply it to every applicant. The Fair Housing Act bars choices based on protected classes, not on income as such. But a "pick the highest income" habit has 2 risks.

First, some states and cities protect source of income. California Government Code 12955(p) defines source of income to include housing subsidies, such as Section 8 vouchers. In those places, how you count income can become discrimination. For voucher math, see [Section 8 rent limits](/guides/section-8-rent-limits).

Second, a choice between two qualified applicants is hard to explain later. If the applicant you rejected is in a protected class, your reason must be clear and consistent. "Higher income" is a weak reason when both met your bar.

A better approach: set the income bar you need, such as 2.5 or 3 times the rent. Then treat everyone who meets it the same. Use the order of receipt to break the tie.

## How do I time-stamp applications?

Record the date and time each application becomes complete, not the time it was started. An application is complete when you have every item your criteria list: the form, the fee if you charge one, ID and income proof.

Use this checklist:

1. **Publish criteria first.** Post your screening criteria with the listing. List each minimum, such as income, credit and rental history.
2. **Define "complete".** List the exact items an application must include.
3. **Use one channel.** An online system time-stamps each submission. For paper, write the date and time on the first page and log it.
4. **Log every application.** Name, time complete, result, and date of decision.
5. **Screen in order.** Do not open the next file until you decide on the current one.
6. **Ask in writing.** If you need more information, ask in writing and note the time.
7. **Set an accept window.** Give the approved applicant a set time to accept, such as 48 hours, then move to the next.
8. **Keep the records.** Keep the log, the criteria and each decision.

A sample log:

| Applicant | Complete at | Result | Next step |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 2026-10-12 09:14 | Did not meet income bar | Written denial sent 10-12 |
| B | 2026-10-12 11:02 | Met all criteria | Offer sent 10-12 14:00; accepted 10-13 |
| C | 2026-10-12 15:40 | Not screened | Told the unit is taken |

## What if the first qualified applicant declines?

Move to the next complete application in time order and screen it the same way. Do not reopen the unit to new applicants while qualified applicants are still waiting. Send each applicant who did not get the unit a short, polite notice.

If you deny an applicant based in whole or in part on a consumer report, the Fair Credit Reporting Act, 15 U.S.C. 1681m(a), requires an adverse action notice. Follow your screening company's process for that notice.

A written policy, applied the same way each time, protects you and the renter. If you would rather hand screening to a team, read about [property management services](/property-management).
