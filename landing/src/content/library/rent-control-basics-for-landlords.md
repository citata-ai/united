## Which kinds of rent regulation exist?

Rent regulation can limit the price, the increase process or the reasons for ending a tenancy. Check each layer separately before sending a notice.

A rent cap sets a limit on increases for covered housing. Local rent control or stabilization can add registration, allowed increases and other duties. A just-cause rule controls why a landlord can end a covered tenancy.

California's Department of Real Estate explains that the Tenant Protection Act includes rent limits and just-cause protections. It also warns that local rules can add protections. [Read the DRE guide](https://dre.ca.gov/Publications/ResourceGuidebook/gb10_movingout.html).

Oregon ORS 90.323 sets rules for covered residential rent increases. ORS 90.324 requires the state to publish the applicable annual maximums. Use the current official figure for the correct tenancy type and year. [Read the Oregon statutes](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors090.html).

Do not use a national list as the final answer for a property. The list can help locate a rule, but it cannot establish your exemption or notice duties.

## Are single-family homes exempt from rent limits?

A single-family home is not automatically exempt from every housing rule. Read the exact exemption and check its conditions.

The California DRE guide explains that many single-family homes and condominiums can be exempt from the Tenant Protection Act. It also notes ownership limits and required exemption notices. A missing disclosure can matter even when the building type looks eligible.

Check local law separately. A state exemption does not necessarily answer every city rule or required-cause question. Ask the local housing office which ordinance applies at the exact address.

Oregon uses its own statutory coverage and exceptions under ORS 90.323. Do not import California's single-family analysis into an Oregon file.

Build a property coverage record. Include the legal owner, property type, construction evidence and any required notice. Mark the conclusion as reviewed on a specific date.

Recheck after ownership changes or major property changes. An exemption tied to ownership details needs review when those details change. Keep the earlier evidence so staff can explain past decisions.

## What are just-cause eviction rules?

Just-cause rules require a legally recognized reason to end a covered tenancy. They can apply even when the landlord follows the basic notice period.

California's DRE guide separates at-fault and no-fault grounds under the Tenant Protection Act. It describes conditions for coverage and different consequences for certain no-fault actions. Do not treat the category name as enough to serve a notice.

Write the actual reason before choosing a form. Nonpayment, owner move-in and substantial work involve different facts. Get counsel to check the required proof and any relocation obligation.

A lease ending on its printed date does not always settle the right to require move-out. Read the coverage rule and renewal requirements. Avoid telling staff that every fixed term guarantees vacant possession.

Rent limits and just cause should appear as separate checks in your workflow. A property may have a different answer for each. That separation helps prevent an exemption from being applied too broadly.

## Where do I check local rules?

Start with official state law and the local housing or rent board. Use the property's address, not just the mailing city.

| Coverage question | Evidence to collect | Who can confirm |
|---|---|---|
| Which jurisdiction applies? | Parcel and address record | City or county office |
| Is a rent cap active? | Current statute or ordinance | Housing office and counsel |
| Does an exemption apply? | Owner and building records | Qualified local adviser |
| Was disclosure required? | Lease and notice history | Counsel |
| Is cause required to end? | Tenancy and coverage facts | Counsel or housing office |
| Which process applies now? | Effective date and current forms | Official local source |

Save the source page or official document with the review date. A search result can show an outdated figure. Read the rule and its effective date before using it.

California's Attorney General maintains a landlord and tenant issues page with current state guidance. Use that as a starting point, then check the local office. [Read the Attorney General's guidance](https://oag.ca.gov/consumers/general/landlord-tenant-issues).

If sources disagree, do not choose the larger permitted increase. Ask the responsible office or counsel to resolve the conflict.

## How do I prepare a lawful rent change?

Prepare a property-specific calculation and notice checklist before changing the ledger. A legal increase limit is only part of the task.

Confirm the current lawful rent and the effective date of the last change. Review concessions, added charges and lease terms. Ask counsel how the law treats changes in included services or mandatory fees.

Then check the permitted amount, notice period, service method and required wording. Confirm whether the tenant's subsidy arrangement adds another approval step.

Have someone review the whole notice package against the coverage record. Check that the tenant name, unit, amount and date match the ledger. Do not let an automatic renewal letter bypass that review.

Explain the change plainly to the tenant. Keep the notice and proof of delivery together. Record the new amount only for the approved period, with the old amount preserved in history.

Use the [management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to plan management costs separately from any rent increase. Your budget does not establish a legal increase limit.

Your next step is to create the coverage record for each rental address. Update it when the law or property facts change.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.
