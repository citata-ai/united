## What does the lease say about outdoor care?

The lease can assign lawful tasks, but state and local duties come first. Read the clause alongside the rules for the exact property.

Separate private yards, shared paths, parking areas and public sidewalks. These areas can have different duties. A short clause saying the tenant handles outside care may leave the most important area unclear.

Massachusetts guidance distinguishes single-unit homes from shared areas in multifamily housing. It places shared paths of egress under the housing provider's responsibility and describes conditions for certain tenant-controlled areas. Check the cited sanitary code and local sidewalk rule. [Read Massachusetts guidance](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/snow-removal-and-accessibility-in-massachusetts).

Michigan's court benchbook explains duties under MCL 554.139, including common areas fit for their intended use. That is a different legal framework from a simple assignment of shoveling. [Read the Michigan court guide](https://www.courts.michigan.gov/4a5c71/siteassets/publications/benchbooks/lltbb/lltbbresponsivehtml5.zip/LLTBB/Ch_2_Specific_Acts/Habitability_Covenants.htm).

Do not copy a rule from one state into another lease. Have local counsel review the task clause and any limits on changing statutory duties.

## What is the slip-and-fall risk?

An injury claim depends on duties, conditions and the facts of the event. A signed task clause does not by itself settle liability.

Review where people need to walk. A cleared parking space does not help if the route to the door remains blocked. Think about stairs, ramps, trash access and emergency exits.

Check conditions after service. Melting snow can refreeze, and a plow can leave a ridge across a walkway. Treat the contractor's completion message as evidence of a visit, not proof that every route is usable.

Keep an incident record if someone reports a fall. Preserve the report, service log, photos and weather information you actually reviewed. Do not guess about cause or ask staff to change earlier notes.

Notify the insurer under the policy process. Ask counsel and the insurer before making admissions or promising payment. Continue addressing the hazard while the claim receives review.

The Michigan court guide discusses fitness for intended use as a fact-specific question. Do not turn a single court example into a universal acceptable snow depth.

## Should I hire a service and include the cost in rent?

A service can help when you need consistent work across shared areas or cannot verify tenant performance. Compare the full operating cost and the lawful lease structure.

Ask the contractor to walk the site. Mark each included area and any excluded work. Clarify who handles ice treatment, refreezing checks and blocked access.

| Task | Scope question | Proof to request |
|---|---|---|
| Snow clearing | Which paths, stairs and spaces? | Dated service log |
| Ice treatment | Which surfaces and products? | Work record and follow-up |
| Lawn mowing | Which areas and standard? | Visit record |
| Trimming | What plants and sight lines? | Scope and photos |
| Waste cleanup | Who removes clippings or debris? | Disposal plan |
| Backup response | Who acts if the crew cannot attend? | Contact and escalation plan |

Check insurance and the contract's risk terms with qualified advisers. Do not assume that a certificate alone answers coverage for your property.

Use the [management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to plan management costs, then add actual service quotes to your budget. Do not invent a standard monthly outdoor-care charge.

Before shifting costs to an existing tenant, review the lease and applicable rent rules. A new service does not automatically authorize a new mandatory payment.

## How do I write a clear duty into the lease?

Write the exact task, location, timing standard and backup process after legal review. Avoid broad phrases that require the tenant to guess what satisfactory work means.

Identify who supplies equipment and materials. State where they are stored and who maintains them. Do not assign a task that staff know cannot be done safely with the available tools.

Set a reporting method for damaged equipment, hazards and missed service. Explain who the tenant contacts when they cannot complete an assigned task. Give the manager a backup vendor rather than relying on informal favors.

Keep lawn work separate from tree hazards, major drainage and other repairs. A routine care clause should not disguise an owner repair duty. Ask counsel which responsibilities can lawfully shift.

Address requests for disability-related changes through the proper accommodation process. Federal Fair Housing law includes reasonable accommodation duties in covered housing. Review requests individually rather than treating an inability to shovel as automatic misconduct. [Read 42 USC 3604](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/3604).

## How do I check that the plan works?

Inspect the service outcome and keep a record that explains the next action. A plan needs a person responsible for follow-up.

Use this recommended seasonal review:

- **Map the site.** Identify shared, private and public areas.
- **Check the law.** Confirm the current local duties and lawful lease assignments.
- **Define the work.** Match the task list to the tenant or vendor agreement.
- **Prepare supplies.** Check equipment, materials and access.
- **Set a backup.** Name the person who acts when service fails.
- **Keep records.** Save service times, photos and reported hazards.

For lawn care, review overgrowth, debris and drainage concerns through the same reporting process. Do not treat a cosmetic issue and a blocked safe route as equal priorities.

At renewal, compare the written plan with what actually happened. Correct gaps before the next season. Give tenants clear contact details and a practical way to report an unsafe condition.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.
