## How often should you inspect an occupied rental?

Plan at least 1 inspection a year for an occupied rental. You can add a short look at the outside each quarter. A yearly visit finds slow leaks, missing alarms and lease issues before they turn into a large repair or a deposit dispute at move-out.

The law sets the notice, not the schedule. Florida law lets a landlord enter to inspect with proper notice (Fla. Stat. 83.53). Arizona law says the tenant may not unreasonably withhold consent to an inspection (A.R.S. 33-1343). Neither statute sets how many inspections a year you may do. So write your schedule into the lease, and keep to it.

Watch for: too many visits. Both Florida and Arizona say a landlord may not abuse the right of access or use it to harass the tenant. One planned visit a year, plus visits for repairs, is easy to defend.

## How much notice must you give before the inspection?

Give the notice your state requires, in writing, with the date, time window and purpose. Here are 4 states as examples.

| State | Notice rule | Source |
|---|---|---|
| California | Written notice; 24 hours presumed reasonable, 6 days if mailed; normal business hours | Cal. Civ. Code 1954 |
| Florida | At least 24 hours for repairs; repair hours 7:30 a.m. to 8:00 p.m. | Fla. Stat. 83.53 |
| Arizona | At least 2 days' notice, at reasonable times | A.R.S. 33-1343 |
| Oregon | At least 24 hours' actual notice, at reasonable times | ORS 90.322 |

California's list of entry reasons names repairs, agreed services and showings. It does not name a routine inspection. If you manage in California, ask a local attorney how to fit a yearly visit into the allowed reasons, for example by tying it to agreed smoke alarm service.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## Which safety items must you test?

Test every smoke alarm and every carbon monoxide (CO) alarm at each visit. These 2 devices protect lives, and a test takes under a minute each.

The U.S. Fire Administration (USFA) gives these rules for smoke alarms:

- **Test monthly.** This applies to battery, long-life and hardwired alarms.
- **Replace the whole alarm every 10 years.** Check the date on the back.
- **Replace batteries once a year** in 9-volt alarms and in the backup of hardwired alarms.
- **Place alarms** inside and outside each bedroom and on every level, on the ceiling or high on the wall.

The Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) says to put CO alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas. It notes that more than 200 people in the U.S. die each year from accidental non-fire CO poisoning tied to consumer products.

The USFA also reports that failure to clean was the leading factor in home clothes dryer fires from 2018 to 2020, at 31%. So check the dryer vent at each visit.

## The annual inspection checklist

Use this list room by room. Mark each line pass, fail or not present, and take a photo of each fail.

**Safety**

- [ ] Each smoke alarm sounds on test; date under 10 years
- [ ] Each CO alarm sounds on test (homes with gas, a fireplace or an attached garage)
- [ ] Fire extinguisher present, gauge in the green, if the lease or local code needs one
- [ ] Dryer lint screen and vent clear; vent hood flap opens
- [ ] Stair rails and handrails firm
- [ ] Door and window locks work; bedroom windows open for exit

**Water and plumbing**

- [ ] No drips or stains under the kitchen and bath sinks
- [ ] Toilets do not run or rock; no soft floor near the base
- [ ] Tub and shower caulk and grout intact
- [ ] Water heater: no rust or water at the base; relief valve pipe in place
- [ ] Washer hoses not cracked or bulging

**Heating and cooling**

- [ ] HVAC filter date and condition (the Department of Energy says to clean or replace filters once a month or as recommended)
- [ ] Heat and cooling both run; thermostat works
- [ ] Outdoor unit clear of plants and trash

**Interior**

- [ ] Walls, ceilings and floors: new stains, holes or cracks
- [ ] Signs of mold, pests or droppings
- [ ] Appliances in the lease work (stove, refrigerator, dishwasher)
- [ ] Electrical: outlet covers in place, no scorch marks, GFCI outlets trip and reset

**Exterior**

- [ ] Roof: missing shingles, gutters attached and clear
- [ ] Siding, trim and paint: rot, gaps, peeling
- [ ] Walks and steps: trip hazards
- [ ] Yard care done to the lease standard

**Lease compliance**

- [ ] Number of occupants matches the lease
- [ ] Pets match the pet addendum
- [ ] No unapproved changes (paint, locks, appliances)

Keep the list neutral. Note conditions, not opinions about the people who live there. Fair Housing rules apply to inspections too: use the same checklist and the same schedule for every unit.

## How do you report findings to the owner?

Send the owner a one-page report within 2 business days of the visit. The owner should see the result in 30 seconds and the detail if they want it.

Use this format:

| Section | What it holds |
|---|---|
| Header | Address, date, inspector, tenant present or not |
| Safety summary | Pass or fail for each alarm and device |
| Findings | One line each: room, issue, photo link, priority (urgent, soon, watch) |
| Cost | Estimate for each urgent and soon item |
| Tenant items | Damage the tenant must fix or pay for, per the lease |
| Next step | What you will do now, and what needs owner approval |

Worked example: a 3-bedroom house with 5 smoke alarms and 1 CO alarm. You find 1 smoke alarm dated 2014, a slow drip under the bath sink and a filter that has not been changed. The report shows "Safety: 5 of 6 pass." It lists 3 findings: replace the alarm (urgent), fix the drip (soon), change the filter (done on site). The owner approves 1 line, and you close the work order the same week.

What to do: store the photos with the move-in report. At move-out, the side-by-side photos make deposit decisions faster and easier to defend.

## How do you schedule the inspection with the tenant?

Pick a window, send written notice, and confirm 1 day before. A clear purpose and a short window make the visit easier for the tenant.

1. **Send the notice** 3 to 7 days ahead, longer than the legal minimum.
2. **State the purpose:** "annual safety and maintenance inspection."
3. **Give a 2-hour window**, inside normal business hours.
4. **Invite the tenant** to attend and to list repairs they want looked at.
5. **Bring basic parts:** batteries, filters, a spare alarm.
6. **Leave a note** if the tenant is not home, with what you did.
