## What is a normal days-on-market for a rental?

A normal days on market (DOM, the days from listing to signed lease) depends on your local market. No single national number fits every city, price band and season. Use local data for your benchmark.

National data gives context. The U.S. Census Bureau's Housing Vacancy Survey reported a national rental vacancy rate of 7.3% in the second quarter of 2026. It said that rate was virtually the same as the first quarter of 2026 (7.3%) and not statistically different from the second quarter of 2025 (7.0%).

A vacancy rate is not the same as days on market. It shows the share of rental homes vacant and for rent at a point in time. A higher local rate usually means more competition and longer DOM.

**How to find your local benchmark:**

1. **Search active and recently rented listings** for similar units within about 1 mile.
2. **Note the listing date** and the rented or removed date where the site shows them.
3. **Write down 10 comparables** with rent, beds, baths and DOM.
4. **Use the middle value** as your target.
5. **Repeat each season.** Your benchmark changes over the year.

## What does a long vacancy cost?

A long vacancy costs one day of rent for each extra day. The cost adds up faster than most owners expect.

**Worked example:** rent is $1,950 a month, or about $64 a day.

| Days vacant | Lost rent |
|---|---|
| 14 | $896 |
| 30 | $1,920 |
| 45 | $2,880 |
| 60 | $3,840 |

Compare that with a price cut. Cutting rent by $50 a month costs $600 over a 12-month lease. If the cut leases the unit 2 weeks sooner, it saves about $896 of vacancy. The cut pays for itself.

## Which season leases fastest?

In many markets, late spring and summer bring the most renters, and winter brings the fewest. School calendars and job moves drive much of that. But local patterns differ, so check your own records.

Build a simple seasonal table from your history:

| Month listed | Units | Average DOM | Rent vs asking |
|---|---|---|---|
| January | | | |
| April | | | |
| July | | | |

After 2 years, you will see your real peak months. Then use lease lengths to move turnovers into them. For example, an 18-month lease that starts in November ends in April.

## What does the inquiry-to-showing ratio show?

The inquiry-to-showing ratio shows where your leasing funnel breaks. Track 4 numbers each week for each listing:

1. **Views** of the listing (if the site shows them).
2. **Inquiries:** calls, messages and forms.
3. **Showings** held.
4. **Applications** received.

Then read the funnel:

| Funnel step | If it is low | Likely cause | Try this |
|---|---|---|---|
| Views | Few people see the ad | Weak title, few sites, poor first photo | Add sites; lead with the best photo; check the title |
| Inquiries per view | People look but do not ask | Price too high; missing details | Check comps; add rent, deposit, pets, utilities, available date |
| Showings per inquiry | People ask but do not come | Slow replies; hard to book | Reply within 1 hour; offer self-booking or group times |
| Applications per showing | People visit but do not apply | Condition, smell, noise, price | Fix what visitors mention; recheck price |

Set your own target ratios from your past listings. A step that falls well below your own average is the one to fix first.

**Response time matters.** A renter who waits a day for a reply may have booked another showing. Use auto-replies with your booking link.

## When do I change price or photos?

Change photos first when views or inquiries are low, and change price when the listing gets attention but no action. Use a fixed review schedule so you do not wait too long.

| Day | Check | Action if behind |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | Views and inquiries | Fix title, first photo, missing details |
| 7 | Inquiries vs comparables | Replace photos; add a floor plan or video |
| 14 | Showings and applications | Cut price by a small step, such as 2% to 3% |
| 21 | Applications | Cut again or add a move-in incentive |
| 30 | Total funnel | Review everything: price, condition, screening rules |

**Photo checklist:**

- [ ] Daylight, lights on, blinds open.
- [ ] Wide shot of each main room.
- [ ] Kitchen and each bathroom.
- [ ] Outside and parking.
- [ ] No people, no personal items, no clutter.
- [ ] First photo is the best room, not the front door.

**Listing text checklist:**

- [ ] Rent, deposit and fees.
- [ ] Available date.
- [ ] Beds, baths, square feet.
- [ ] Pet rules and utilities.
- [ ] How to book a showing.

Describe the home, not the ideal tenant. HUD's rule at 24 CFR 100.75 bans ads that show a preference based on race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status or national origin.

## What does a long vacancy tell me?

A long vacancy tells you something in the funnel is off. Check these causes in this order, from easiest to test to hardest:

1. **Price** compared with similar local listings.
2. **Photos and details** in the listing.
3. **Response time** to inquiries.
4. **Condition** of the unit at showings.
5. **Screening rules** that turn away qualified renters.
6. **Season.** A winter listing may take longer.

Fix one thing at a time, then watch the funnel for a week.

## What you can do next

Start a weekly funnel log for your next listing: views, inquiries, showings, applications. If leasing takes too much time, see how [property management](/property-management) handles it.
