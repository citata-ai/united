## What does a vacancy fee cover?

A vacancy fee should pay for a stated service that continues while rent stops. Start with the actual work: property checks, utility coordination, access control, owner updates and repair scheduling. List the work you already charge elsewhere.

A leaking home still needs a response when no resident lives there. Yet an owner may reasonably ask why a management fee continues without rental income. Answer with a service schedule rather than a claim that every manager charges it.

Build the schedule around the property. A home awaiting repair needs different work from a ready unit with active marketing. Define when vacancy service begins, changes and ends.

Use a simple comparison:

| Work | Possible pricing home | Check for overlap |
|---|---|---|
| Condition check | Vacancy service | Separate inspection fee |
| Advertising and showings | Leasing service | Placement fee |
| Repair coordination | Base scope or project fee | Maintenance markup |
| Utility administration | Vacancy service | Direct utility charges |
| Owner progress report | Base management | Account administration fee |

The table is a planning tool, not a market fee schedule. Choose a single clear pricing home for each task. If a task appears in several packages, explain when each applies.

Keep vendor bills separate from the management company's fee. An owner should be able to identify who performed the work and why the charge exists.

## How do owners see a vacancy fee?

Owners can see a vacancy fee as fair oversight or as a charge for poor leasing results. The difference depends on scope, communication and billing design. Test the explanation before adding the fee to your offer.

Show a sample vacant-month statement. Include the fee, direct property bills and any separate leasing charge. Ask the owner what they think each line buys. If they cannot tell, revise the wording.

Explain what happens during owner-caused delays without blaming the owner. A home may remain off market while waiting for funds or a sale decision. State whether active oversight continues and who can stop it.

Avoid charging for work you did not perform. Keep visit records and progress notes where the owner can review them. A scheduled check should leave evidence, including unresolved conditions and the next step.

When a vacancy lasts longer than expected, discuss leasing decisions separately from billing. Review price, property readiness, access and application progress. Do not use a vacancy fee to make a weak leasing process financially comfortable.

If your company advertises no fee during vacancy, check every page and agreement for consistency. A small exception hidden in a fee schedule can undermine that promise.

## What about a minimum monthly fee?

A monthly minimum can pay for continuing service without creating a separate vacancy charge. Define whether it replaces the percentage fee or adds to it. Those designs produce different owner bills.

Use a proposed formula before choosing amounts: bill the greater of the earned percentage fee and the agreed minimum. That is one possible design, not an industry rule. Another company may choose a fixed package with different scope.

Test occupied, partly paid, vacant and owner-use periods. Check whether a minimum applies during onboarding, termination or a property being held off market. Write each answer before staff prepare invoices.

SBA's finance guidance recommends records and financial planning for business decisions. Apply that method to the cost of continuing service. Use actual staff time and overhead rather than a competitor's unexplained price.

Compare total annual owner cost using the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator). Then add the vacancy and minimum rules in a separate scenario. A headline percentage does not show the whole bill.

Have staff explain the fee aloud without a spreadsheet. If the explanation needs several exceptions, simplify the design. Complexity can create billing errors as well as sales friction.

## How does no-rent-no-fee pricing change incentives?

A collected-rent model ties the base fee to actual receipts, but it does not guarantee good leasing decisions. A separate vacancy fee pays for ongoing work, but it does not excuse avoidable delays. Assess the whole operating system.

Keep leasing quality controls independent of fee design. Staff should follow approved criteria and lawful procedures rather than rush an application to restart revenue. Do not reward speed without checking work quality.

Show managers the current status of vacant homes. Identify readiness delays, unanswered owner decisions and stalled showings. Give each issue a responsible person. Pricing cannot repair a missing handoff.

If you waive a vacancy fee, define the waiver in writing. State its scope and approval process. Do not let informal exceptions create different invoices for owners with the same agreed terms.

Washington's WAC 308-124D-215 requires a written management agreement that states compensation. It also requires signed written changes. Those are Washington rules, not national requirements, but they show why a clear fee schedule matters.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## How should I set and launch the fee?

Set the fee after testing the cost and the owner explanation. Review vacant-home work records, identify what the base service already covers, and price the remaining scope. Label any trial price as your own proposal.

Ask operations staff to prepare sample invoices for likely edge cases. Include a mid-period move-out, delayed repairs and an owner who removes the listing. Fix unclear billing events before introducing the fee.

Create a short sales explanation with the service included, billing trigger and stop condition. Keep it beside the written agreement. Give staff a route to ask about unusual cases without inventing terms.

Track complaints about value separately from invoice errors. An accurate charge can still describe a weak service. Review both the work performed and the owner's understanding.
