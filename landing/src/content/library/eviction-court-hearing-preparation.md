## Which documents do I bring?

Bring every document that proves the tenancy, the breach and the notice. Put them in order, and bring copies. The California Courts self-help site tells landlords to make 2 copies of each piece of evidence: one for the judge and one for the tenant. Keep your own original too.

**Hearing binder checklist:**

- [ ] **Court papers:** the complaint, summons, proof of service of the court papers, and any answer the tenant filed.
- [ ] **The lease** and every addendum, signed.
- [ ] **The notice** you served before filing.
- [ ] **Proof of service** of the notice.
- [ ] **Rent ledger** with each charge, payment and balance.
- [ ] **Messages** with the tenant about rent, repairs or the breach.
- [ ] **Photos** of damage or lease breaches, dated.
- [ ] **Receipts** for repairs you claim.
- [ ] **Witness list** and the questions you will ask.
- [ ] **A short written outline** of what you will say.

Tab the binder by section. When the judge asks for the notice, you want to find it in 5 seconds.

## How do I show the notice was served?

Show the notice was served with a written proof of service that states who served it, how, where and when. Judges check the notice and its service closely. A defect can end your case, even when the rent is unpaid.

Notice rules differ by state. Two examples:

| State | Notice before filing | Source |
|---|---|---|
| California | 3-day notice to pay rent or quit; counts exclude Saturdays, Sundays and judicial holidays; must state the amount due and who to pay | Code of Civil Procedure 1161(2) |
| Texas | At least 3 days' written notice to vacate, unless the written lease sets a shorter or longer period | Property Code 24.005(a) |

Texas 24.005 also says: for a tenant who was not late before the month of the notice, a nonpayment notice must be a notice to pay rent or vacate.

Some properties have federal rules too. The CARES Act section at 15 U.S.C. 9058(c) says the lessor of a "covered dwelling" may not require the tenant to vacate before 30 days after a notice to vacate. Covered properties include those with a federally backed mortgage loan or in certain federal housing programs. Ask a local attorney whether and how this applies to your property today.

**Proof to bring for service:**

1. **The signed proof of service** form.
2. **A copy of the exact notice** served.
3. **Photos** of the notice posted, if posting was used.
4. **Mail receipts** or tracking, if mailing was used.
5. **The server** as a witness, if possible.

## What do tenants raise as defenses?

Tenants raise defenses that attack the notice, the landlord's conduct or the amount claimed. Prepare an answer for each defense before the hearing.

| Defense | What the tenant says | Evidence that answers it |
|---|---|---|
| Defective notice | Wrong amount, wrong dates, missing details | The notice, your ledger, the statute's checklist |
| Improper service | Notice was never delivered properly | Proof of service, photos, server testimony |
| Retaliation | You filed because they complained or asked for repairs | Timeline showing the breach came first; ledger |
| Repair problems | The unit had serious defects | Repair requests and dates fixed; invoices |
| Rent paid or offered | They paid or tried to pay | Ledger; records of any refused payment and why |
| Discrimination | You acted because of a protected trait | Your written policy applied the same to every tenant |

Texas shows how retaliation rules work. Property Code 92.331 bars a landlord from filing an eviction within 6 months after a tenant in good faith takes certain steps. Those steps include giving a notice to repair or complaining to a code agency. Section 92.332 lists exceptions, such as delinquent rent. Other states have their own rules.

Do not change locks or remove belongings to force a tenant out. Texas Property Code 92.0081 bars a landlord from keeping a tenant out except by judicial process, with narrow exceptions. Self-help hurts your case.

## How do I present the case?

Present the case in short, clear steps, and show your evidence as you go. The California Courts self-help site says the landlord usually speaks first. It suggests starting with: "Your Honor, I believe my tenant should move out because…"

A simple structure:

1. **Who and what:** "I own the unit at [address]. The tenant signed this lease on [date]."
2. **The breach:** "Rent of $1,800 for [months] is unpaid. Here is the ledger."
3. **The notice:** "I served this notice on [date] by [method]. Here is the proof of service."
4. **What you ask for:** possession, and the amount due if your state allows it in this case.

Stay calm, even if the tenant is upset. Speak to the judge, not the tenant. If the judge asks a question, answer it directly.

**Practical tips from the California Courts self-help site:**

- Be ready to wait up to 4 hours after your start time.
- Watch an eviction trial at your courthouse before your own, if you can.
- Write down what you will say before court.

## Do I need an attorney?

You do not always need an attorney for an eviction hearing, but one helps in many cases. Get one when:

- **The tenant has an attorney.**
- **The tenant filed an answer** with defenses such as retaliation or repairs.
- **The property has special rules,** such as rent control, just cause or a federal program.
- **You own through an LLC or corporation.** Check your court's rules on who may appear for a business.
- **You are not sure the notice was correct.** A lawyer can tell you whether to start over.

The California Courts self-help site says evictions can take 30 to 45 days or more, from service of court papers to move-out. A mistake can restart the clock. The cost of an attorney is often less than another month of lost rent.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## What you can do next

Build your hearing binder the day you serve the notice, not the night before court. Keep one ledger for every tenant from move-in. If evictions take too much of your time, see how [property management](/property-management) handles them.
