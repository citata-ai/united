## What does the law say about assistance animals in rentals?

An assistance animal request is a request for a reasonable accommodation under the Fair Housing Act. The federal regulation, 24 CFR 100.204, makes it unlawful to refuse a reasonable accommodation in "rules, policies, practices, or services." The test is whether a person with a disability needs it for equal use of the home. The regulation's own first example is a blind applicant with a guide dog in a "no pets" building. Refusing the dog is a violation.

So a "no pets" rule does not end the talk. The question is whether the person has a disability and a disability-related need for this animal.

## What changed when HUD withdrew its animal guidance?

HUD withdrew 8 fair housing guidance documents, effective September 17, 2025. The Federal Register published the list on April 6, 2026. It includes FHEO-2020-01, the 2020 notice on assistance animal requests, and FHEO Notice 2013-01 on service and assistance animals. HUD says those documents "should not be relied upon as authoritative."

What did not change:

- **The statute.** The Fair Housing Act still bans a refusal to make reasonable accommodations.
- **The regulation.** 24 CFR 100.204 still applies, with its guide dog example.
- **Private lawsuits.** The same notice says complainants may still sue in federal or state court within 2 years.
- **The 2004 joint statement.** The HUD and Justice Department joint statement on reasonable accommodations is not on the April 2026 list or on the July 2026 list of withdrawn HUD legal guidance. It is still on justice.gov.

Watch for: older blog posts still quote FHEO-2020-01 as current. Build your process on the statute, the regulation and your state law.

## Service animal vs emotional support animal: what is the difference?

A service animal, under the ADA, is a dog trained to do work or tasks for a person with a disability. ADA.gov says dogs whose only job is comfort do not qualify under the ADA. But the same page says this definition "does not affect or limit the broader definition of 'assistance animal' under the Fair Housing Act."

| Point | ADA service animal | Fair Housing Act assistance animal |
|---|---|---|
| Where it applies | Places open to the public | The home itself |
| Species | Dogs (some rules for miniature horses) | Not limited to dogs |
| Training needed | Yes, for a task | Not always |
| Emotional support | Not covered | Can be a reasonable accommodation |
| Questions allowed | 2 set questions under the ADA | Reliable information if the need is not obvious |

Inside the unit, the Fair Housing Act test applies. Do not use the ADA's narrow dog-only test to deny a housing request.

## Can I charge a pet fee for an assistance animal?

No. The 2004 joint statement answers this in plain words: housing providers "may not require persons with disabilities to pay extra fees or deposits as a condition of receiving a reasonable accommodation." Its example is a tenant with a hearing impairment and an assistance animal: no fee, no extra deposit.

What you may do:

1. **Charge for real damage.** If the animal damages the unit, you may bill the cost or deduct it from the standard deposit that all tenants pay. This applies only if you charge all tenants for damage they cause.
2. **Keep your normal deposit.** The standard security deposit stays the same as for any tenant.

Florida's statute 760.27 says the same at the state level: the person "may not be required to pay extra compensation for such animal."

## What documents can I ask for?

The answer depends on what you can already see. The 2004 joint statement sets 3 cases.

| Case | What you may ask for |
|---|---|
| Disability and need are both obvious (blind tenant, guide dog) | Nothing more |
| Disability is obvious, need for the animal is not | Information about the need only |
| Neither is obvious | Reliable information that shows the disability, the animal needed, and the link between them |

The joint statement says a doctor, a peer support group, a non-medical service agency or a "reliable third party" can verify a disability. It also says medical records or details of the disability are usually not needed. Keep what you get confidential.

State law can add detail. Two examples:

- **Florida (760.27):** lists what supporting information may include, such as a government disability finding or information from a health care practitioner. An out-of-state practitioner counts only if that person gave in-person care at least once. The landlord may not ask for the diagnosis, the severity or medical records. The landlord may ask for proof of local licensing and vaccination.
- **Iowa (216.8C):** a licensed provider who writes a finding must state whether the provider-patient relationship lasted at least 30 days. The finding is valid for 12 months or the lease term, whichever is longer.

Check your state's statute before you write your form.

## When can I deny a request?

You can deny an assistance animal request only on narrow grounds. The 2004 joint statement lists these:

- The request was not made by or for a person with a disability.
- There is no disability-related need for the accommodation.
- The accommodation would be an undue financial and administrative burden.
- It would fundamentally alter your operations.

The statement adds a direct threat ground. It must rest on an individual assessment and objective evidence, not on fear or a guess. Florida's statute uses the same idea: a direct threat to health, safety or property that no other accommodation can reduce.

Before you deny, talk with the person about other options. The joint statement calls this an "interactive process."

## A 6-step process for every request

Use the same steps for every request, so each tenant gets the same treatment.

1. **Log the request with the date.** A request counts even if the tenant never says "reasonable accommodation."
2. **Check what is obvious.** Use the table above to decide what, if anything, to ask for.
3. **Ask in writing, once.** Ask only for what you need. Do not ask for a diagnosis.
4. **Decide promptly.** The joint statement warns that an undue delay may count as a denial.
5. **Put the decision in writing.** If you approve, state that no pet fee, pet rent or pet deposit applies.
6. **Apply normal rules after move-in.** Noise, waste and damage rules still apply to the tenant, as they do to everyone.

If you manage many doors, write this as a one-page policy. Train every leasing agent on it. If you would rather hand this work to a manager, see how [property management services](/property-management) handle it.
