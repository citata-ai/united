## What is the break-even formula for a property management company?

The break-even door count is your fixed monthly costs divided by the monthly profit one door leaves after its own costs. That is the whole formula. The hard part is to get honest inputs.

`Break-even doors = fixed monthly costs ÷ (revenue per door − variable cost per door)`

Run it three times:

1. **Survival:** fixed costs only. The company covers its bills, but you work for free.
2. **Owner salary:** fixed costs plus the salary you need. This is the real break-even.
3. **First hire:** fixed costs, your salary and one employee. This tells you when a hire pays for itself.

Many online answers give a range of 50 to 150 doors. Wexford Insurance, for example, calls the band under 50 doors the stage where "most owners are effectively buying themselves a job". A range does not help you plan. Your own numbers do.

## How much revenue does one door bring?

Revenue per door is everything a door pays you in one year, divided by 12. Count the monthly management fee and the monthly average of leasing, renewal, inspection and other fees.

Worked example:

- Monthly rent: $1,800 (example).
- Management fee: 8% of collected rent = $144.
- Leasing and renewal fees spread over 12 months: about $6 a month (example).
- **Revenue per door: $150 a month.**

Then take off the variable cost of each door. Variable costs grow with door count: per-unit software fees, payment fees you do not pass on, owner statements, and the drive time for that door. In the example, variable cost is $20 a month. That leaves $130 of profit per door.

## How many doors do I need at each stage?

Plug the example into the formula. The owner salary is the BLS median annual wage for property, real estate and community association managers: $69,990 in May 2025 (BLS). That is $5,833 a month.

The first hire is a full-time admin. The BLS median wage for secretaries and administrative assistants was $48,310 in May 2025, or $4,026 a month. I add 20% for payroll tax and benefits. The 20% is an assumption; ask your payroll provider for your real load. That gives $4,831 a month.

| Stage | Monthly costs to cover | ÷ profit per door | Break-even doors |
|---|---|---|---|
| Survival | $2,000 | $130 | 16 |
| Plus owner salary | $7,833 | $130 | 61 |
| Plus first admin hire | $12,664 | $130 | 98 |

Two lessons come out of the table:

- **The owner salary is the biggest cost.** It moves break-even from 16 to 61 doors. If you skip it in your plan, you hide most of the cost.
- **Each hire needs its own block of doors.** In the example, one admin needs about 37 more doors to pay for itself.

Change one input and the answer moves a lot. At $1,200 rent and 8%, revenue per door drops to $96 plus fees. With the same costs, the salary break-even rises to about 96 doors.

## How does door density change the answer?

Door density is how close your doors are to each other. Dense doors cost less time per door. Scattered doors cost more.

Each inspection, showing and repair visit takes drive time. That time is a variable cost, even if you pay it with your own hours. Wexford Insurance makes the same point: 100 scattered single-family homes can need more travel and coordination than 100 units in one building.

To put a number on density:

1. Log drive minutes for each door for one month.
2. Multiply the hours by the hourly cost of the person who drives.
3. Divide by door count and add the result to variable cost per door.

If drive time adds $15 a month to each scattered door, profit per door in the example drops from $130 to $115. Salary break-even then rises from 61 to 69 doors.

## At what door count do I need a team?

You need a team when your hours run out, not when a door count says so. The break-even table tells you when a hire is affordable. Your calendar tells you when it is needed.

Use this checklist each month:

- [ ] You work more than 50 hours a week for 2 months or more.
- [ ] Owner or tenant calls wait more than one business day for an answer.
- [ ] You turn away owner leads because you have no time to onboard them.
- [ ] Your door count is at or above the "first hire" break-even line.

If the first three are true but the fourth is not, raise revenue per door first, or hire part time. If all four are true, hire.
