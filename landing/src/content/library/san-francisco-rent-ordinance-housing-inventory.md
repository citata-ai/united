## Which San Francisco units are under rent control?

Many San Francisco units built on or before June 13, 1979 have both rent control and eviction protection under the Rent Ordinance (SF Administrative Code Chapter 37). The SF Rent Board tells owners to check the build date first on the city's Property Information Map.

| Unit type | Rent control | Just cause eviction protection |
|---|---|---|
| Built on or before 1979-06-13, not a single-family dwelling | Yes, in most cases | Yes |
| Built after 1979-06-13 | No | Yes |
| Most single-family homes and condos (tenant moved in on or after 1996-01-01) | No | Yes |
| Substantially rehabilitated units | No | Yes |
| Units regulated by another government agency | No | Yes |
| Hotels and rooming houses with stays under 32 days, dorms, care facilities, resident-run co-ops | No | No |

Source: SF Rent Board, "Learn about San Francisco rental laws." Some units are hard to classify. The Rent Board offers free counseling at 415-252-4600 on weekdays.

Watch for: a unit with no local rent control may still fall under the California Tenant Protection Act (AB 1482). The Rent Board flags this on the same page.

## What is the yearly allowable increase in San Francisco?

The San Francisco allowable annual increase is 1.6% from March 1, 2026 through February 28, 2027. The Rent Board sets it at 60% of the rise in the Bay Area consumer price index for all urban consumers.

| Period | Allowable increase | Security deposit interest |
|---|---|---|
| 2026-03-01 to 2027-02-28 | 1.6% | 4.2% |
| 2025-03-01 to 2026-02-28 | 1.4% | 5.0% |
| 2024-03-01 to 2025-02-28 | 1.7% | 5.2% |

Source: SF Rent Board current rates page.

Rules that change the math:

1. **Once a year.** You can raise rent only once every 12 months. The date of the increase becomes the tenant's anniversary date.
2. **Base rent only.** Apply the percentage to base rent, including housing services like parking. Do not round up to the next dollar.
3. **Banking.** If you skip an increase, you can bank it and add it later. A skipped increase is lost if you move the anniversary date before 12 months pass.
4. **Notice.** Give 30 days' written notice, or 90 days if the increase is more than 10% in a year. Add 5 days if you mail it.
5. **New tenants.** There is no limit on the first rent you charge when you rent an empty rent-controlled unit.

A worked example: a tenant pays $3,697, the HUD FY2027 fair market rent for a 2-bedroom in the San Francisco area. A 1.6% increase is $3,697 x 0.016 = $59.15. The new base rent is $3,756.15. Compare that with the HUD area 2-bedroom FMR rise of 2.6% on the [San Francisco fair market rent 2027 page](/guides/section-8-rent-limits/san-francisco-fair-market-rent-2027).

If you skipped three years, the Rent Board's own example shows how banking adds up: 1.7% (2024) + 1.4% (2025) + 1.6% (2026) = 4.7% on the anniversary date.

## Who must report to the SF Rent Board Housing Inventory?

All San Francisco residential property owners must report unit information into the Rent Board Housing Inventory each year by March 1. The rule covers condos and single-family homes too.

The rollout came in two steps, per the Rent Board:

- **Buildings with 10 or more units:** first report in 2022. The license rule took effect on July 1, 2022.
- **Condos, single-family homes and buildings with 1 to 9 units:** first report and license rule from March 1, 2023.

The link to rent: an owner who reports a unit as tenant-occupied gets a rent increase license. Without the license, you may not impose annual allowable or banked increases until you complete the report. The Rent Board's 2023 press release said it had issued 12,000 licenses at that point, under 5% of the city's estimated residential units.

Reporting runs through the Rent Board Portal at portal.sfrb.org. The Rent Board mails a yearly notice about the Rent Board fee and the Housing Inventory. The 2027 notice is on sf.gov now.

Checklist for each March 1:

- [ ] Report every residential unit into the Housing Inventory.
- [ ] Confirm you hold a rent increase license for each tenant-occupied unit.
- [ ] Check that the license covers the effective date of each increase you plan for the next 12 months.
- [ ] Pay the Rent Board fee. The current rates page lists $29.50 as the landlord's share and $29.50 as the tenant's share for the 2024-2025 tax year.
- [ ] Put each tenant's anniversary date in one calendar.

## What are SF just cause rules for single-family homes?

Most San Francisco single-family homes and condos have just cause eviction protection, even with no rent control. That means you need a lawful reason to end the tenancy. The Rent Board lists examples:

- The tenant fails to pay rent or is often late.
- The tenant breaks the rental agreement or uses the unit for an illegal reason.
- The tenant interferes with others' comfort or safety.
- You or an immediate family member plan to move in.
- You plan to convert to a condo and sell.
- You plan work that makes the unit unlivable during construction, with the right to return after.
- You remove all units from the rental market under the state Ellis Act.

Some no-fault evictions cost money. For an eviction notice served from 2026-03-01 to 2027-02-28 for owner move-in, demolition, temporary capital work or substantial rehabilitation, the Rent Board lists:

| Relocation payment (Section 37.9C) | Amount |
|---|---|
| Per tenant | $8,245 |
| Maximum per unit | $24,733 |
| Extra for each elderly (60+) or disabled tenant, or household with minor children | $5,497 |

For Ellis Act evictions in the same period, the amounts are $11,110.05 per tenant, up to $33,330.13 per unit, plus $7,443.90 for each elderly (62+) or disabled tenant.

A worked example: an owner move-in for two adult tenants with one child costs 2 x $8,245 + $5,497 = $21,987. That is below the $24,733 per-unit cap, so you pay $21,987 plus your legal costs.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## What should a San Francisco owner do next?

The safest routine is to tie the March 1 report to your rent increase calendar. Many owners give annual and banked increases on the same date each year, as the Rent Board suggests, to avoid losing an increase.

If a manager runs your building, ask three things: who files the Housing Inventory, who tracks each tenant's anniversary date, and who checks the license before each notice.
