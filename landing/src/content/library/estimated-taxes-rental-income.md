## When are estimated tax payments due?

The IRS 2026 Form 1040-ES lists payment dates across the tax year and the following January.

For a calendar-year individual, the form lists the following regular dates. These are federal payment dates for the specified tax year. Check IRS relief notices or special rules that may apply to you. State estimated tax rules need a separate review.

| Payment | Regular due date in IRS 2026 Form 1040-ES |
|---|---|
| First | April 15, 2026 |
| Second | June 15, 2026 |
| Third | September 15, 2026 |
| Fourth | January 15, 2027 |

The form also states an exception for the January payment when a return and full balance meet its early-filing conditions. Read the current instructions before relying on an exception. Do not confuse an annual filing extension with permission to delay tax payments.

The IRS estimated tax page says individuals generally need estimated payments when they expect to owe $1,000 or more at filing. The full Form 1040-ES test also considers withholding, credits, and required payment levels. A rental receipt alone does not prove that payments are due.

Save confirmation for each payment with the tax year and taxpayer details. Check the records if a payment appears missing from your account. An accurate bank withdrawal record helps, but you also need to know where the IRS applied the payment.

## What safe harbor can help avoid an underpayment penalty?

IRS safe-harbor rules compare timely payments with current-year tax or qualifying prior-year tax, not simply rental profit.

The IRS estimated tax FAQ generally describes a 90% current-year threshold or 100% of prior-year tax. For certain higher-income taxpayers, the prior-year figure becomes 110%. The prior return must cover a full year. Special situations can require a different analysis.

The FAQ identifies the higher-income threshold as prior-year adjusted gross income above $150,000, or $75,000 if married filing separately. Check the filing status and prior-year figure before choosing a target. Do not assume gross rent is the income measure used for that threshold.

Use an illustrative annual calculation. Assume prior-year tax of $12,000 and that the 100% prior-year rule applies. Assume expected qualifying withholding of $8,000. The remaining annual target would be $4,000. These figures are planning assumptions, not a personal tax recommendation.

That annual difference does not settle payment timing. Ask your preparer how withholding and estimated payments apply across the required periods. Paying a large amount late may not correct an earlier shortfall under the regular calculation.

A safe harbor can limit an underpayment penalty while leaving a balance due with the return. Keep a separate forecast of the final tax bill. Money reserved for that balance should not become an owner distribution by mistake.

## Can I increase W-4 withholding instead?

The IRS says employees can request extra wage withholding through a new Form W-4 instead of making separate estimated payments.

This can be practical when you have wages and rental income. Use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator or a preparer's calculation to assess the full return. Include spouse income, other investments, credits, and deductions where relevant. A rental-only estimate may miss a larger household shortfall.

Ask payroll when a change takes effect. Check later pay statements to confirm the actual withholding. A submitted form does not prove that enough tax has reached the IRS. Recalculate if the number of remaining pay periods changes.

The tax question is separate from rental banking. You may still need to reserve rental cash even if payroll handles the tax payment. Otherwise, the extra withholding can reduce household cash while you distribute all rental receipts.

Revisit the calculation after a sale, a large repair, a vacancy, or a major income change. Taxable profit and cash receipts may move differently. Give the preparer updated records rather than relying on the original annual forecast.

If you have no wages available for withholding, ask about other permitted payment methods and current estimated tax instructions. Use the IRS payment channel that records the correct tax year and payment type. Keep the confirmation rather than relying on a calendar reminder alone.

## How do I estimate rental profit for tax purposes?

Estimate taxable rental profit from expected income and allowable deductions, rather than from cash left after the mortgage payment.

IRS Publication 527 explains rental receipts, expenses, depreciation, and reporting. Common expense categories include insurance, management fees, repairs, and mortgage interest. Debt principal is different from deductible interest. Capital improvements can have different timing from ordinary repair expenses.

Start with the lease and actual collections. Account for refunds, advance rent, and deposit treatment under the IRS rules. Use current bills and known work orders to forecast costs. Keep major improvements separate for tax review.

Add a depreciation estimate from the existing asset schedule. Do not use the entire purchase price as an annual deduction. Ask the preparer to review personal use and loss limits as needed. A loss shown in bookkeeping may not reduce current tax in full.

The [management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) can support a service-cost assumption. It cannot calculate your household tax rate or establish a safe harbor. Give the complete rental forecast and prior return to your tax professional.

Before the next payment date, compare the updated full-return estimate with actual withholding and payments. Adjust the plan using current IRS instructions. Keep both the calculation and the payment evidence with your tax file.
