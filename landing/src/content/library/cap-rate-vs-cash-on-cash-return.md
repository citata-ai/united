Ask two owners how their rental is doing. One says "6% cap rate". The other says "2% cash on cash". They may own the same house. One paid cash. The other has a mortgage. Both numbers are true. They answer different questions.

This guide explains each number, shows why a loan changes one and not the other, and puts both on one house.

## How Is Each One Calculated?

Cap rate rates the property. Cash-on-cash return rates your cash in the deal.

| Measure | Formula | Includes the mortgage? | Answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cap rate | Net operating income ÷ property value | No | How much does this property earn for its price? |
| Cash-on-cash return | Yearly pre-tax cash flow ÷ total cash invested | Yes | How much does my own money earn this year? |

**Net operating income (NOI)** is rent minus vacancy and operating costs: tax, insurance, repairs, management, reserves. It leaves out the mortgage, depreciation and income tax.

**Pre-tax cash flow** is NOI minus the full yearly mortgage payment, principal and interest.

**Total cash invested** is your down payment, closing costs and any upfront repairs.

Both leave out depreciation. IRS Publication 527 treats depreciation as a capital expense recovered over time, not a cash cost.

## Why Does a Loan Change Cash-on-Cash but Not Cap Rate?

A loan changes your cash flow and your cash invested. It does not change the property's NOI or value. So the cap rate stays the same, and cash-on-cash moves.

The key number is the loan constant: the yearly payment as a share of the loan balance. On 2026-10-15, the Freddie Mac 30-year fixed average was 7.40%, per FRED. A 30-year loan at 7.40% costs about $18,694 a year per $225,000 borrowed. That is a constant of about 8.3%.

The rule of thumb follows:

- **Cap rate above the loan constant.** Debt raises your cash-on-cash return. This is positive leverage.
- **Cap rate below the loan constant.** Debt lowers your cash-on-cash return. This is negative leverage.

Note that the constant is higher than the interest rate, because each payment also repays principal. Principal paydown builds equity, but it is not cash in your pocket this year.

## Can You Show Both on One Property?

Yes. Here is a worked example with invented numbers. The house is worth $300,000 and has NOI of $18,000, so the cap rate is 6.0%. Closing costs are $9,000.

The loan cases use 25% down. The Fannie Mae Eligibility Matrix (August 5, 2026) allows up to 85% LTV on a 1-unit investment purchase, so 25% down is within the limit.

| Case | Cash invested | Yearly debt payment | Cash flow | Cap rate | Cash-on-cash |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| All cash | $309,000 | $0 | $18,000 | 6.0% | 5.8% |
| 25% down, 7.40%, 30 years | $84,000 | $18,694 | −$694 | 6.0% | −0.8% |
| 25% down, 6.00%, 30 years | $84,000 | $16,188 | $1,812 | 6.0% | 2.2% |

The cap rate stays at 6.0% in every row. Cash-on-cash swings from 5.8% to below zero. At 7.40%, the loan constant (8.3%) is above the cap rate (6.0%), so the loan hurts cash flow.

Now raise NOI to $27,000, a 9.0% cap rate, on the same price:

| Case | Cash flow | Cap rate | Cash-on-cash |
|---|---|---|---|
| All cash | $27,000 | 9.0% | 8.7% |
| 25% down, 7.40%, 30 years | $8,306 | 9.0% | 9.9% |

Here the cap rate (9.0%) is above the loan constant (8.3%). The loan now raises cash-on-cash from 8.7% to 9.9%.

## Which Number Should an Owner of One Rental Watch?

Watch both, for different jobs.

| Job | Better number | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Is my rental paying me each year? | Cash-on-cash | It includes your real mortgage payment |
| Should I sell and buy another home? | Cap rate | It compares properties on equal terms |
| Should I refinance? | Cash-on-cash | A new rate changes the debt line |
| Should I pay off the loan early? | Both | Compare cap rate with the loan rate you would retire |
| Should I hire a manager? | Both | Management cost lowers NOI and cash flow |

Neither number shows appreciation, principal paydown or tax effects. For a long hold, add a simple total return view: cash flow plus principal paid plus estimated value change.

## What Mistakes Do Owners Make With These Numbers?

Watch for these common errors:

- **Mixing NOI and cash flow.** NOI is before the mortgage. Cash flow is after.
- **Leaving out reserves.** A roof or furnace will come. Put a yearly reserve in NOI.
- **Using the listing's NOI.** Sellers often leave out vacancy or management. Rebuild NOI yourself.
- **Forgetting closing costs.** They belong in cash invested.
- **Comparing cash-on-cash across different loans.** Two buyers of one house can see very different numbers.

## How Do You Run Both Numbers Today?

1. **Build NOI** from 12 months of rent, minus vacancy and all operating costs.
2. **Divide NOI by value** to get the cap rate.
3. **Find your yearly mortgage payment** from your statement.
4. **Subtract it from NOI** to get cash flow.
5. **Add up your cash invested.**
6. **Divide cash flow by cash invested** to get cash-on-cash.
7. **Compare the cap rate with your loan constant.**
