## Is a shut-off a habitability problem?

A utility shutoff can make a rental unsafe, so assess the actual conditions at once. Ask which service stopped and whether the tenant faces an immediate hazard. Do not leave the issue in the ordinary billing queue while you debate responsibility.

Record whether the problem affects the unit, the building or the wider area. Ask the provider whether the cause is nonpayment, a network outage, a safety hold or an account error. A repair crew cannot solve every billing problem. A payment cannot solve a damaged service connection.

Washington RCW 59.18.060 includes duties concerning working electrical, plumbing and heating facilities and adequate heat and water facilities. The exact response still depends on the facts and applicable law. Tenant payment duties do not remove the need to assess safety.

Set an incident lead and a contact schedule. Tell the resident what you know, what remains unconfirmed and who is handling restoration. If anyone reports an immediate danger, direct the response through emergency services and the utility. Do not offer a guessed restoration time as a promise.

## Can the utility bill the owner?

A utility may have rights against the owner even when the lease says the tenant pays. Check the provider's service rules and the law for that property. The lease and the utility account are separate records.

La Crosse, Wisconsin, says the property owner remains responsible for utility bills in its system. Unpaid balances may become a property lien. Its guidance also explains that shared-meter multi-unit bills stay in the landlord's name. Do not apply those local rules to every water provider.

Ask the provider for the service address, account holder, billing period and final amount. Check for a wrong unit or a charge from a prior occupant. Request the applicable tariff or written rule if staff say the debt transfers to you.

Do not pay blindly and then post an unexplained tenant charge. Record why payment is needed and whether you dispute any part. Separate a payment to protect the property from a final decision about tenant liability. Ask counsel how to preserve a dispute while restoring service.

## How do I get service restored?

Restore service through the provider's approved process and a written incident plan. Obtain the account holder's cooperation or authorization where needed. Do not impersonate the tenant to gain account access.

| Step | Action | Evidence to save |
|---|---|---|
| Confirm | Ask why service stopped | Provider reference and written notice |
| Assess | Check safety and affected areas | Resident report and site observations |
| Resolve | Meet lawful payment or repair conditions | Receipt, agreement or contractor clearance |
| Restore | Arrange approved reconnection | Provider confirmation and appointment |
| Verify | Confirm service works in the unit | Resident confirmation and work record |
| Reconcile | Review responsibility after restoration | Final bill and itemized account entry |

Never reconnect a disconnected meter yourself. Route equipment work to qualified professionals. Ask whether the provider requires an inspection or clearance. Keep the restoration plan separate from any lease enforcement notice.

Florida Statute 83.67 prohibits landlord-caused utility interruption in covered tenancies. Washington RCW 59.18.300 prohibits intentional landlord termination, with a stated exception for reasonable repair interruptions. Those state examples support a clear operating rule: do not use shutoffs to collect rent or obtain possession.

## Can I deduct utility debt from the deposit?

A deposit deduction requires a lawful basis and proof of the tenant's actual obligation. Check the lease, deposit statute and local utility process. Do not assume every amount the utility charged you belongs on the tenant ledger.

Match the bill to the occupancy period. Remove owner service charges or periods that the tenant did not owe under the applicable terms. Check whether the tenant paid the provider after you obtained the bill. A stale balance can lead to duplicate collection.

La Crosse's landlord guidance discusses deposit use for final utility bills and coordination with its billing agreement. That is a local example, not permission to ignore another state's deduction rules. Ask your own provider how to close the account and prevent conflicting bills.

Save the bill, payment proof and itemized explanation with the deposit statement. Follow the local deadline even if a provider is slow to issue a final balance. Ask counsel how estimates or later claims work where you operate. Do not invent a late fee to cover the delay.

## How can I prevent another shutoff?

Prevent repeat shutoffs with clear account setup and a provider-specific monitoring process. List each service in the lease file. Record whose name belongs on the account and how the service transfers at move-in and move-out.

Request owner notices where the provider offers them and local rules permit them. Keep contact details current. A notice sent to an old owner address may arrive too late to help. Give staff a route for utility letters that arrive outside the usual maintenance channels.

For shared service, write a clear billing method before leasing. Explain what the resident pays, what records support the charge and how questions are resolved. Do not change the allocation method after a high bill without reviewing the agreement and local law.

Check vacant periods too. Confirm that frost protection, alarms and required building systems remain supported during turnover. Assign account transfers to a named employee. Save completion evidence rather than relying on a task marked done.

Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) when reviewing owner operating costs. For the current incident, start with the provider's written restoration conditions. Resolve safe service first, then complete a supported account review.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.
