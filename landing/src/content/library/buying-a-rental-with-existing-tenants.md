## Which documents do I get from the seller?

Get every paper that proves who lives in the unit, what they pay and what they paid in. You take over the leases, so you also take over the seller's promises. Ask for the papers before you remove your contingencies, not at the closing table.

Use this checklist. Put the list in the purchase contract, so the seller must deliver.

| Document | What it tells you | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| Every lease and addendum | Rent, end date, pets, parking, who pays utilities | A lease that is missing or not signed |
| Rent roll | Rent per unit, paid-through date, balance owed | Rent far below market with a long term left |
| Security deposit ledger | Deposit and pet deposit per tenant, interest owed | Deposit totals that do not match the leases |
| 12 months of rent records | Who pays late and who does not pay | Many "cash" payments with no receipts |
| Open notices and court cases | Pending evictions, violation notices | An eviction the seller started but did not finish |
| Maintenance log and open work orders | What tenants asked for and what is still open | Old habitability complaints |
| Side deals in writing | Rent credits, promised repairs, free months | Oral promises the tenant will say you must keep |
| Inspection and permit records | Rental license, lead paint and safety records | A unit with no required rental license |

The rent roll is the seller's claim. The leases and the tenants' answers are the proof. Compare the three before you close.

## What is a tenant estoppel certificate?

A tenant estoppel certificate is a short form the tenant signs to confirm the lease facts. The U.S. House Office of Administrative Counsel says an estoppel certificate "establishes the current status of the lease terms for a third party." The House page says owners use one when they sell a building or refinance a loan. It also says the form asks the tenant to confirm that rent is current and to name any claims against the landlord.

For a buyer, the estoppel certificate catches problems the seller did not disclose. A tenant may say they paid a larger deposit. A tenant may say the seller promised a new fridge or a rent cut. It is better to learn that before closing than after.

Ask each tenant to confirm these items on the form:

1. **Names.** Every adult on the lease and every person who lives in the unit.
2. **Lease dates.** The start date, the end date, and any renewal option.
3. **Rent.** The monthly rent, the due date, and the date rent is paid through.
4. **Deposits.** The security deposit, pet deposit and any prepaid rent, in dollars.
5. **Side deals.** Any promise, credit or discount not in the written lease.
6. **Claims.** Any repair, refund or dispute the tenant has with the landlord.

Most tenants have no duty to sign unless their lease says so. Ask the seller to request the forms, and give tenants a clear deadline. If a tenant will not sign, rely more on the lease and the deposit ledger, and ask the seller for a written statement in the contract.

## How do I transfer security deposits?

Take over the deposits at closing with a written accounting for each tenant. In many states, the buyer becomes liable for the deposit whether or not the seller hands over the cash. So a missing deposit is your loss unless the contract fixes it.

State rules differ. Here are 3 examples:

| State | Rule | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Texas | The new owner is liable for deposits from the date title is acquired. The new owner must give each tenant a signed statement with the exact deposit amount. | Property Code 92.105 |
| Florida | Deposits and advance rent go to the new owner with any interest and an accounting per tenant. The law presumes the new owner got the deposit, up to 1 month's rent. | Statute 83.49(7) |
| California | The seller must transfer the deposit, less lawful deductions, to the buyer and notify the tenant, or return it to the tenant. Before a voluntary sale, the seller gives the buyer a written statement of each deposit. | Civil Code 1950.5(i), (j) |

California Civil Code 1950.5(k) adds a risk. If the seller does not follow the transfer rule, the buyer can share liability for the deposit with the seller.

A worked example shows why the ledger matters. You buy a 4-unit building. The leases show deposits of $1,500, $1,500, $1,200 and $1,800, a total of $6,000. The seller's ledger says $5,000. If you close with a $5,000 credit, you may still owe the tenants $6,000 when they move out. Settle the $1,000 gap in the contract before closing.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## How do I tell tenants about the new owner?

Send each tenant a written notice in the first days after closing. Do it before the next rent due date. A tenant who pays the seller by mistake has not done anything wrong, and the dispute costs you time.

In California, Civil Code 1962(c) gives a successor owner 15 days to give tenants the owner's name, address and phone, and the rent payment details. Section 1962(c) also says a new owner who misses that rule may not evict for rent that came due during the gap. Other states have their own rules, so check yours.

Put these items in the notice:

- The date you became the owner.
- Your name, or your manager's name, plus a mailing address, phone and email.
- The new way to pay rent, and the date it starts.
- The deposit amount you now hold for the tenant.
- How to request repairs, and an emergency phone number.
- A line that says the lease terms stay the same.

Send the notice by the method your lease or state law requires. Keep a copy and proof of delivery.

## What should I do in the first 30 days?

Meet each tenant and walk each unit in the first 30 days. Give written notice before you enter, as your state law and the lease require. Take dated photos of each room. These photos protect both sides when the tenant moves out, because you did not do the move-in inspection.

Then set up your systems:

1. **Rent.** Set up online rent collection and confirm each tenant's first payment to you.
2. **Deposits.** Move the deposits into the account your state requires, if it requires one.
3. **Insurance.** Change the policy to a landlord policy in your name from the closing date.
4. **Repairs.** Close the open work orders from the seller's log.
5. **Leases.** Note each end date. Decide on renewals at least 60 days ahead.
