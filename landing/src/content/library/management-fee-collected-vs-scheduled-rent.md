## What Is the Difference Between Collected and Scheduled Rent?

Collected rent follows receipts, while scheduled rent follows amounts due under the agreed definition. A percentage alone cannot tell an owner the actual management bill.

Rentec Direct tells owners to check whether monthly fees use collected rent or rent due. Its guide treats that distinction as a contract question. Use the same care in your own offer.

A collected basis can connect manager pay with owner receipts. When no eligible rent arrives, a pure percentage charge on those receipts produces no fee. A separate minimum or vacancy fee changes that result. State those terms beside the percentage.

A scheduled basis can pay for ongoing work despite unpaid rent. That may suit a service with fixed staffing costs. The owner still needs to know that the charge can apply before the property receives cash.

Define scheduled rent carefully. Does it mean lease rent for occupied units only? Does a concession reduce the base? How do partial months work? Avoid using gross rent as a substitute for a precise definition. That phrase can mean different things to the owner and accountant.

## Which Basis Do Most Property Managers Use?

The sources reviewed do not prove which fee basis a national majority uses. Describe your own terms without calling them the industry standard.

For an owner who values a close link between receipts and bills, a collected basis may be easier to explain. It also creates timing questions when rent arrives late or a payment reverses. A scheduled basis needs a clear reason and a clear vacancy rule.

Compare the choices against service cost. Staff still handle requests during arrears. A collected model must earn enough across the portfolio to fund that work. A scheduled model must show owners what they receive while cash is short.

Do not choose a basis only to show a lower headline percentage. A low rate on a broad base can cost more than a higher rate on a narrow base. Use the same property scenario when comparing offers.

Ask sales staff to explain the fee without looking at the contract. Then ask accounting to calculate it. If their answers differ, fix the definition before signing more accounts. The [fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) can support estimates, but your agreement controls the real bill.

## What Happens When a Resident Pays Late?

Late receipts need a written timing rule so staff can calculate the charge consistently. State which accounting event starts the fee.

For a collected model, define whether collected means received, posted or settled. Include the treatment of returned payments. Explain when a fee reversal appears on the owner statement if money leaves after a fee was charged.

Do not change the receipt category to make the fee work. Keep rent, deposits and other charges in separate ledger accounts. Correct errors with records that show the original entry and the reason for the change.

The following example uses invented figures for arithmetic only. It is not a market rate or a recommended charge.

| Example assumption | Scheduled basis | Collected basis |
|---|---:|---:|
| Monthly eligible rent due | $2,000 | $2,000 |
| Eligible rent received this month | $1,500 | $1,500 |
| Assumed rate | 8% | 8% |
| Fee before other terms | $160 | $120 |

If the missing $500 arrives later, the collected model could generate a further $40 fee under the example terms. The scheduled model should not charge the same rent twice. A receipt report and billing record should make that clear.

## Do Pet Rent, Utilities and Late Fees Count in the Base?

Only include income categories that the agreement clearly places in the fee base. Avoid treating every deposit into the bank as rent.

Create a fee-base schedule for accounting. List recurring rent, approved recurring charges, reimbursements, late fees and other receipts. Give each item an included or excluded status. Have the owner review the categories that affect the quote.

Separate security deposits from earned fee income. A deposit transfer is not the same event as collecting current rent. Ask local counsel and your accountant how the applicable rules affect each fund category.

For reimbursements, explain whether the management fee applies before or after the reimbursed cost. Owners may view a utility reimbursement differently from rental income. If you charge an added service fee, state how it relates to the percentage charge.

Review software mappings after any chart-of-accounts change. A renamed category can accidentally enter the fee base. Test the billing report with a sample owner file before using a changed setup across the portfolio.

## How Do You Put the Fee Basis Into Practice?

Turn the fee definition into a repeatable billing check. Keep the sales promise, agreement and software settings aligned.

- Write the base in plain words and list each exclusion.
- State the rate, any minimum and all vacancy terms.
- Define receipts, reversals, late payments and partial months.
- Show an owner an example statement before signing.
- Compare fee calculations with the underlying rent ledger.
- Record corrections and explain them on the next statement.

Test awkward cases before the first bill. Include unpaid rent, an overpayment, a deposit transfer and a returned payment. Check whether the owner statement gives enough detail to trace the charge.

When you change the base, treat it as a change to the offer. Do not quietly expand the categories through a software setting. Agree the effective terms and keep the prior version with signed records.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney. Select a recent statement and confirm that every fee dollar traces to the agreed base.
