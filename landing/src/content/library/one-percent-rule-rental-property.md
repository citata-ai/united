## How does the one percent rule work?

The rule compares monthly gross rent with a stated property cost. Rocket Mortgage describes the rule as a quick way to screen an investment. Its method adds needed repairs to the purchase price before applying the percentage.

Write the formula before using it: monthly gross rent divided by stated property cost, multiplied by 100. Gross rent means rent before operating expenses and debt payments. The result is a monthly rent-to-cost ratio, not an annual return.

Here is a hypothetical screen using invented prices and rents. These examples show arithmetic only. They do not describe actual listings or market conditions.

| Example | Cost used | Monthly gross rent | Rent-to-cost result |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Home A | $200,000 | $2,000 | 1.00% |
| Home B | $300,000 | $2,100 | 0.70% |
| Home C | $180,000 plus $20,000 repairs | $2,000 | 1.00% |

Home A and Home C meet this version of the rule. Home B does not. None of the rows tells you whether the home will produce cash after costs.

Keep the denominator consistent. Comparing a purchase price for one listing with an all-in repair cost for another can distort your shortlist. Keep closing and financing costs visible even when your screen excludes them.

## Why can a market fail the screen?

A market fails the screen when rents are low relative to purchase prices. That mathematical result does not explain why prices or rents reached those levels. You need local evidence before making a claim about demand or value.

A higher purchase price does not force renters to pay more. Compare homes with similar size, condition, location, and lease terms. Use a rent supported by current evidence rather than the amount required to pass the formula.

An expensive home can fail the screen despite low repair needs. A cheap home can pass while facing major system failures. The ratio does not inspect a roof, verify a legal unit, or show an insurance quote.

Avoid changing the assumed rent to save a deal. If the advertised rent lacks support, run a lower case. Ask a local manager what the home can rent for without extended vacancy.

The screen also ignores your personal financing. A cash buyer and a borrower can calculate the same ratio but have different monthly cash results. Obtain actual loan terms before deciding which case fits you.

## What does the rule leave out?

The rule leaves out the costs and timing that determine cash flow. Start with operating expenses, then financing, capital work, and income tax. A gross figure cannot stand in for those separate checks.

Include property tax, rental insurance, management, owner-paid utilities, routine maintenance, and association charges. Add vacancy and collection assumptions. Separate repairs already needed from future planned work.

Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) with an actual manager quote. Check leasing fees and other charges outside the base rate. A percentage fee alone may not describe the full agreement.

Next, list loan principal and interest. If the mortgage payment includes escrow, keep taxes and insurance from appearing twice. Make the spreadsheet easy to audit against the loan statement.

IRS Publication 527 explains that tax reporting uses its own rules for rental expenses and depreciation. A tax deduction does not make a cash expense disappear. Do not add an assumed tax benefit before a professional reviews your case.

## Can a property pass and still lose cash?

Yes. Passing the gross rent screen does not prove that expenses and debt payments fit beneath rent. A worked case makes the missing information visible.

Assume a hypothetical home costs $200,000 and has scheduled rent of $2,000 per month. It passes the 1% screen. Now assume collected rent averages $1,850 after a vacancy allowance.

Assume operating costs total $700 per month. That leaves $1,150 before debt service. If principal and interest total $1,200, the cash result is negative $50 before capital reserves and income tax.

The calculation uses invented assumptions. Its purpose is to show the order of operations. It does not establish a recommended expense ratio or predict a loss for any actual property.

A buyer may still choose the home for another reason. They should name that reason and budget the cash need. Expected price growth is uncertain and does not fund today's repair bill.

## What better test can an owner use?

Use a full property budget with evidence for rent, expenses, and loan terms. Keep a normal case and a difficult case. State every assumption so you can replace it when a quote arrives.

- **Verify rent.** Compare actual competing homes and ask about time to lease.
- **Price operating costs.** Use tax records, insurance quotes, bills, and management proposals.
- **Inspect the property.** Get repair costs and confirm the permitted use.
- **Enter financing.** Separate principal, interest, escrow, fees, and any changing terms.
- **Plan liquidity.** Decide how you will fund vacancy and major work.
- **Compare your cash.** Include down payment, closing costs, repairs, and available reserves.

Use the screen to decide which listing deserves a closer look. Use the full budget to decide whether to buy. Save the calculations so the next review can compare the forecast with results.
