## Should I require written maintenance requests?

Yes. Require written maintenance requests for every non-emergency repair. A written request has a date, a description and often a photo. A hallway chat or a voicemail has none of those, and it is easy to lose.

Written requests also match how state law works. Two Florida rules show why:

- **Florida Statutes 83.56(1):** a tenant may end the lease if the landlord materially fails to comply with code duties within 7 days after the tenant delivers a written notice.
- **Florida Statutes 83.60(1)(b):** in a nonpayment case, a tenant may raise the landlord's failure to repair as a defense. The tenant must have sent written notice at least 7 days before.

In those cases, the tenant's written notice starts a 7-day clock. If your process puts every request in writing, you see that clock start. If requests come by text, phone and hallway, you may miss it.

Some states also put a duty on the tenant to report. Virginia Code 55.1-1227(A)(10) says the tenant must promptly tell the landlord about moisture build-up or visible mold.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

Keep one exception: emergencies. Give tenants a phone number for true emergencies. Then log the call as a written request in the same system.

## How should tenants submit maintenance requests?

Tenants should submit maintenance requests through one channel that you check every business day. Most property management software includes a tenant portal with a request form. A set email address also works for small portfolios.

Pick one channel and say it in 4 places:

1. **The lease**, in a maintenance clause.
2. **The move-in packet**, with the portal link or email address.
3. **The portal home page**, or the email signature of your team.
4. **A sticker** inside a kitchen cabinet or near the water heater.

Then set the emergency rule. List what counts as an emergency so tenants know when to call. A common list: no heat in cold weather, a major water leak, sewage backup, gas smell, fire or smoke damage, no working lock on an exterior door, and no electricity in the unit.

## What info should a maintenance request include?

A maintenance request should include enough detail to send the right vendor on the first trip. Each extra trip costs a service call fee and a day of the tenant's patience. Build your form with these fields.

| Field | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Unit address and number | Routes the request to the right property |
| Tenant name and phone | Lets the vendor confirm the visit |
| Room and item | "Kitchen sink, left drain" beats "plumbing" |
| Description | What happened, since when, and what the tenant tried |
| Photos or short video | Lets you pick the trade and parts before the visit |
| Urgency | Emergency, urgent (24–72 hours) or routine |
| Permission to enter | Yes or no, plus pets in the unit |
| Best times | Narrows the schedule for the vendor |

Permission to enter saves time, and the rule differs by state. Virginia Code 55.1-1229 says that if the tenant makes a request for maintenance, the landlord does not have to give notice. For routine maintenance the tenant did not ask for, Virginia sets at least 72 hours' notice. Florida Statutes 83.53 sets at least 24 hours' notice for repairs, between 7:30 a.m. and 8:00 p.m. A permission field on the form gives you a written record either way.

## How do I track maintenance request status?

Track maintenance request status with a fixed set of statuses and one owner per request. A status list tells you at a glance what is stuck. One owner means someone answers for every open item.

Use these statuses:

| Status | Meaning | Who moves it |
|---|---|---|
| New | Request came in, nobody has read it | System |
| Triaged | Urgency set, trade picked | Coordinator |
| Scheduled | Vendor and time confirmed with tenant | Coordinator |
| In progress | Vendor on site or work started | Vendor or coordinator |
| Waiting on parts | Work paused for parts or approval | Coordinator |
| Done | Vendor reports work complete | Vendor |
| Closed | Tenant confirmed, or response window passed | Coordinator |

Set target times per urgency. For example: emergencies get a call back within 1 hour, urgent items get a visit within 72 hours, and routine items get a visit within 7 days. These are internal targets, not legal limits. Your state or city may set its own deadlines for some repairs, such as heat or water.

Review the open list each morning. Sort by age. Any item stuck in one status past its target gets a note and a next step.

## How do I close out a maintenance request with the tenant?

Close out a maintenance request with a short written note to the tenant. "Done" from the vendor is not the end. The tenant lives with the result and knows first if the fix failed.

Use this close-out checklist:

- **Get proof from the vendor**: an after photo and a short note of what was done.
- **Attach the invoice** to the request and the unit record.
- **Message the tenant**: what was fixed, who fixed it, the date, and the after photo.
- **Ask one question**: "Is the problem fixed?" Give a reply window, such as 3 days.
- **Mark it closed** when the tenant confirms or the window passes.
- **Tag repeat items**: a second request for the same item within 90 days goes to a senior tech.

The close-out note does 3 jobs. It shows the tenant you followed through. It gives you a record if a dispute comes up. And it catches failed repairs before they turn into larger damage.

## What does a good process look like on one page?

A good maintenance request process fits on one page that every team member and vendor can read. Here is a sample flow.

1. **Tenant submits** a request in the portal, with photos.
2. **Coordinator triages** within 1 business day: urgency, trade, owner approval if needed.
3. **Coordinator schedules** the vendor and confirms access with the tenant.
4. **Vendor completes** the work and uploads an after photo and invoice.
5. **Coordinator closes out** with the tenant and marks the request closed.
6. **Monthly review**: count requests per unit, repeat items, and average days to close.

The monthly review turns requests into data. A unit with 6 plumbing requests in a year needs a plumber's full inspection, not a seventh visit.
