## What is a maintenance markup?

A maintenance markup is a percent that a property manager adds to a vendor's invoice before the owner pays it. A plumber bills the manager $400. The manager bills the owner $440. The $40 is a 10% markup.

The markup pays the manager for the work around a repair. That work is real: take the call, triage it, find a vendor, schedule access, check the job, collect photos and pay the bill. The question is not whether that work has value. The question is whether the owner knows what it costs.

Managers price that work in 3 common ways:

1. **A percent markup** on each vendor invoice.
2. **A flat coordination fee** per work order.
3. **Nothing extra**, with the cost built into the monthly management fee.

## What is a typical maintenance markup percent?

A typical maintenance markup is 10% to 15% of the vendor invoice. Baselane's property management fee statistics were updated 2026-09-17. They say "most property managers add a markup of 10% to 15% on maintenance and repair invoices, with some charging up to 25%." Baselane cites a fee study from rapideyeinspections.com for this figure.

A markup looks small on one job. It adds up over a year. Here is a worked example for one single-family rental with $4,000 of vendor repairs in a year:

| Markup | Extra cost to owner per year | On 20 rentals |
|---|---|---|
| 0% | $0 | $0 |
| 10% | $400 | $8,000 |
| 15% | $600 | $12,000 |
| 25% | $1,000 | $20,000 |

The math: $4,000 × 10% = $400. The 20-rental column multiplies that by 20.

Now compare it with the management fee. Say the rent is $1,800 a month and the fee is 8%. The owner pays $1,800 × 12 × 8% = $1,728 a year in management fees. A 10% markup on $4,000 of repairs adds $400. The true cost of management is then $2,128, or about 9.9% of rent, not 8%.

The rent, fee and repair amounts are an example. Use your own numbers. To model a fee on your own rent roll, use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator).

## Must a manager disclose a markup?

A manager should always disclose a markup, and some states require it by rule. The clearest example we found is Colorado. Colorado Real Estate Commission Rule 5.17 (4 CCR 725-1) says:

- The broker and brokerage firm "must obtain prior written consent from the Consumer they represent" to charge mark-ups or other compensation for services.
- This applies "regardless if for the benefit of the Broker or another third party."
- The broker "must also disclose all mark-ups and/or other compensation for services performed" that a customer pays.
- The broker must keep records that verify the disclosure and consent, and that account for the amounts or percentages charged.

So in Colorado, a markup with no prior written consent breaks the rule, even if the work was good. Other states have their own rules on fees, disclosure and trust accounts. Read your state's real estate commission rules before you set a markup policy.

The same logic works in any state. Put the markup in the management agreement as a number, not a vague phrase like "reasonable fees". Show it on each invoice the owner sees.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## Markup vs maintenance coordination fee?

A markup is a percent of the invoice, and a coordination fee is a flat amount. The 2 methods pay for the same work but react to job size in opposite ways.

| Feature | Percent markup | Flat coordination fee | Included in management fee |
|---|---|---|---|
| How it is charged | % of each vendor invoice | Fixed $ per work order | No extra charge |
| Cost on a $150 job (10% or $25) | $15 | $25 | $0 |
| Cost on a $3,000 job (10% or $25) | $300 | $25 | $0 |
| Grows with job size | Yes | No | No |
| Easy for the owner to predict | Less | More | Most |
| Risk owners worry about | Incentive to pick costly vendors | Many small work orders | Higher base fee |

The $25 flat fee and 10% markup are examples for the math, not market data.

Neither method is wrong. A markup that is capped (for example, 10% up to $150 per job) mixes the two. What matters is that the owner can see and predict the cost.

## How can owners check vendor invoices?

Owners can check vendor invoices by asking for the vendor's original invoice and comparing it with the charge on the owner statement. A good manager sends the original by default.

Use this checklist each quarter:

- [ ] Pick 5 repair charges from your owner statements
- [ ] Ask for the vendor's original invoice for each one
- [ ] Compare the vendor total with the amount charged to you
- [ ] Divide the difference by the vendor total to get the markup percent
- [ ] Check that percent against your management agreement
- [ ] Look for vendor names that match the manager's company or staff
- [ ] Ask whether the manager gets rebates or referral fees from vendors

A worked check. The statement shows $575 for a water heater repair. The vendor invoice shows $500. The markup is $575 − $500 = $75. The percent is $75 ÷ $500 = 15%. If your agreement says 10%, ask about the $25 gap.

Watch for: an in-house maintenance team billed at "market rates". What to do: ask for the hourly rate and the parts cost in writing, the same as for an outside vendor.

## Is a maintenance markup fair?

A maintenance markup is fair when it is disclosed, agreed and shown. It is unfair when it is hidden. Use 3 tests:

1. **Is it written?** The management agreement states the percent or the fee.
2. **Did the owner agree?** The owner signed the agreement with that term in it.
3. **Is it visible?** Each statement or invoice shows the markup as its own line.

If all 3 are true, a markup is a normal way to pay for maintenance work. If any is false, ask the manager to fix it. Some owners compare managers with AI answers and fee pages before they call.
