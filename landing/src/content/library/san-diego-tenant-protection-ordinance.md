## When did the San Diego TPO take effect?

The San Diego tenant protection ordinance (TPO) took effect on 2023-06-24. City Council passed Ordinance O-21647 N.S. on 2023-05-25. It rewrote Municipal Code Chapter 9, Article 8, Division 7 and renamed it "Residential Tenant Protections." Ordinance O-21769 N.S. amended it on 2024-02-27, with effect from 2024-03-28.

The ordinance has 10 sections, 98.0701 to 98.0710. The ones that matter most to an owner:

| Section | Topic | What it requires |
|---|---|---|
| 98.0703 | Exemptions | Lists housing the ordinance does not cover |
| 98.0704 | Just cause | No termination without an at-fault or no-fault just cause |
| 98.0705 | Notice | A written notice and the Tenant Protection Guide in or with the lease |
| 98.0706 | Termination | Notice contents and relocation assistance |
| 98.0707 | Buyouts | Written disclosures before any buyout offer |
| 98.0709 | Remedies | Damages of at least 3 times the loss |

The ordinance applies to any tenancy, which section 98.0702 defines as a right to occupy for more than 30 days. A fixed-term lease of 3 months or less does not count.

## Which San Diego rentals does the TPO cover?

The San Diego TPO covers most rental housing in the city, with a list of exemptions in section 98.0703. Common exemptions for small owners:

- **Newer housing.** A certificate of occupancy issued within the previous 15 years (except mobilehomes).
- **Owner-occupied home.** A single-family home where the landlord lives and rents no more than 2 bedrooms, 2 ADUs or 2 junior ADUs.
- **Owner-occupied duplex.** The landlord lives in one unit from the start of the tenancy and stays.
- **Separately sold homes.** A unit that can be sold apart from any other unit, if the owner is not a REIT, a corporation or an LLC with a corporate member, and the tenant got the exact written notice. For tenancies started or renewed on or after 2024-01-01, that notice must be in the lease.

Section 8 homes are not exempt. The subsidy exemption in 98.0703(d) says so.

Watch for: even if you think you are exempt, give the notice in the exact words the code requires. Without it, the separately-sold-home exemption does not apply.

## What relocation help does a San Diego owner owe at a no-fault termination?

A San Diego owner owes 2 months of the tenant's current rent at a no-fault termination, or 3 months if the tenant is a senior (62 or older) or disabled. Section 98.0706(c) bases the amount on "actual rent under the tenant's lease in effect at the date of the notice," not on market rent. The owner owes it regardless of the tenant's income or how long the tenant has lived there.

The owner chooses how to pay:

1. **Direct payment** within 15 days of the notice, or
2. **Rent waiver** of the same amount for the final months of the tenancy.

| Current rent | Standard tenant (2 months) | Senior or disabled tenant (3 months) | State law, Civil Code 1946.2 (1 month) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $2,000 | $4,000 | $6,000 | $2,000 |
| $2,823 | $5,646 | $8,469 | $2,823 |
| $3,500 | $7,000 | $10,500 | $3,500 |

We did the math from the code. $2,823 is HUD's FY2027 2-bedroom fair market rent for San Diego. Relocation pay comes on top of the deposit refund.

The termination notice must be in at least 12-point font. It must state the cause, the relocation amount and method, and the tenant's right to an offer to rent again if you put the unit back on the market within 5 years.

## Can a San Diego owner end a tenancy to sell or remodel?

A San Diego owner cannot end a tenancy just to sell. A sale is not on the just cause list in section 98.0704. You can sell with the tenant in place. The 4 no-fault causes are:

- **Owner or family move-in.** The owner, a spouse, domestic partner, child, grandchild, parent or grandparent must live there for at least 12 continuous months and move in within 90 days. For leases signed on or after 2023-06-24, this cause applies only if the tenant agrees in writing or the lease allows it.
- **Withdrawal from the rental market.** The owner takes the property off the rental market.
- **Government order.** An order to vacate, or a habitability order that needs the unit empty for at least 30 days.
- **Demolition or substantial remodel.** See the rules below.

A substantial remodel must be work that cannot be done safely with the tenant in place and needs the unit empty for at least 30 days in a row. It must replace or change a structural, electrical, plumbing or mechanical system that needs a permit, or remove lead paint, mold or asbestos. Painting, decorating and minor repairs do not count.

Remodel step list:

1. **Apply for the permits.** Post the permit application at the property within 3 business days.
2. **Get the permits** before you serve any notice.
3. **Serve the notice** under penalty of perjury. Describe the work and its length, attach the permits, and explain why the tenant cannot stay.
4. **Pay relocation** of 2 or 3 months' rent.

If an owner wants a tenant to leave for a payment, section 98.0707 sets buyout rules. The offer needs a signed written disclosure first. A buyout for less than the required relocation amount is void.

## How does the TPO differ from AB 1482?

The San Diego TPO is stricter than AB 1482 (California Civil Code 1946.2 and 1947.12) on eviction, but it does not add a local rent cap. The state cap still applies to covered units: 5% plus the local cost of living change, at most 10% in 12 months.

| Topic | AB 1482 (Civil Code 1946.2) | San Diego TPO |
|---|---|---|
| Just cause starts | After 12 months of occupancy | For any tenancy over 30 days |
| No-fault relocation | 1 month's rent | 2 months, or 3 for seniors and disabled tenants |
| Owner move-in | Allowed with 12-month stay and 90-day move-in | Same, plus a lease clause or written consent for leases from 2023-06-24 |
| Remodel | Permit-level work, 30+ days | Same, plus permits in hand before the notice and a sworn notice |
| Rent cap | 5% + CPI, max 10% (Civil Code 1947.12) | No local cap; state cap applies |

The penalties are high. Under section 98.0709, a landlord who fails to pay relocation owes at least 3 times the required amount plus actual damages. A court can also award attorney's fees.
