## How do payout fraud schemes work?

Payout fraud can begin with a message that appears to come from a real owner or vendor. The sender asks staff to change payment details. A familiar signature and a correct property address do not prove authority.

The FBI's business email compromise guidance describes spoofed addresses, stolen email access and fake payment requests. Criminals can use an existing billing conversation to make a change seem routine. Treat a payment instruction as something to verify, even when the thread looks familiar.

Separate identity from account access. A person who controls an owner's email may not be the owner. A signed-looking attachment may also fail to prove that the real owner approved the change.

Map where bank details enter your systems. Include onboarding forms, staff emails, vendor invoices and portal requests. A strong rule on the main portal does little if staff can bypass it through a spreadsheet.

Use neutral language when checking a request. You are applying a standard process, not accusing the owner. Explain that the same controls protect every payment change.

Train substitute staff too. Fraud controls should still work when the regular bookkeeper is away or an executive asks for an exception.

## Which controls stop fake bank-change requests?

Bank-change controls should require independent verification before a separate person approves payment. Use a trusted route already on file. Do not rely on a phone number or link inside the new request.

The FBI recommends verifying payment changes and using multifactor authentication where available. Build those practices into a written procedure. The following checklist is a proposed internal control design.

- **Pause the change.** Keep the request out of the next payment batch until verified.
- **Use a known route.** Call the previously recorded contact or use a reviewed secure process.
- **Check authority.** Confirm who can change details for the owner entity.
- **Record the result.** Store the verifier, date and approved request in controlled records.
- **Separate approval.** Have another authorized person review the change and payment.
- **Notify the owner.** Confirm the completed change through the established channel.
- **Review exceptions.** Do not let urgency remove verification.

Limit who can edit bank details. Review access after staff changes and remove shared accounts where possible. A log should show whose account made the edit.

Test the procedure with a mock request during a busy payment run. Staff should know how to pause the batch without losing track of lawful payments. Fix any pressure to skip the control.

## Does cyber or crime insurance cover losses?

Insurance coverage depends on the policy, the facts and the type of loss. Ask about fraudulent instructions, stolen funds, data exposure and recovery costs separately. A broad policy name does not answer those questions.

HUB's property management insurance page distinguishes the owner's coverage and the manager's professional liability risks. Use that distinction when asking your broker who would respond. Do not assume another party's policy pays your loss.

Give the insurance broker a written scenario. An impostor changes an owner's payout account, staff approve the change, and money leaves the trust account. Ask which wording applies and what conditions might affect coverage.

Repeat the exercise for a staff member's theft and for stolen resident documents. Ask about exclusions, limits, deductibles, notice duties and any requirements for approved response vendors. Request a written answer tied to the policy.

Check the management agreement with counsel at the same time. A contract may allocate responsibility without making that allocation insured. Do not promise an owner reimbursement based only on a sales summary.

Keep the policy and claim contact accessible outside the normal email account. If the email system is part of the incident, staff still need a safe way to report the loss.

## What must I do after a breach?

A breach response should contain the problem, preserve evidence and bring in the right specialists. The FTC's response guide recommends a team with technical, legal and communications skills. It also warns against destroying evidence.

Follow the incident lead's instructions for affected systems. Do not let staff wipe devices or delete messages to clean up the account. Preserve logs, payment records and the original request for review.

For transfer fraud, the FBI says to contact the financial institution immediately and report to IC3. Give the bank the transfer details and follow its recovery process. Do not promise that a recall will succeed.

Have counsel identify the notification rules that apply to the incident and affected people. Requirements differ by jurisdiction and data type. Avoid copying a generic national notice deadline into the handbook.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

Use a single approved update for owners and residents. State what you know, what you are checking and how people can reach help safely. Avoid sending new payment instructions through a channel that may be compromised.

## How should I test the payment process?

Test whether the controls work under pressure, not just whether a policy exists. Send a harmless mock bank-change request through the normal route. Check whether staff stop, verify and document it.

Review the access log after the test. Confirm that the same person cannot quietly create a payee change and release the transfer. Ask the bank and software vendor what approval separation the actual plan supports.

Keep sensitive test records out of broad staff chats. Use dummy details and get the process lead's approval before the exercise. Record the gap and verify its fix.
