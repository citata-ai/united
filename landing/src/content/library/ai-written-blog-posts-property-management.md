## What does Google say about AI-written content?

Google says appropriate use of AI is not against its guidelines. In a 2023-02-08 post, Google Search said its systems reward original, high-quality content "however it is produced". The same post says AI used mainly to manipulate rankings breaks its spam policies.

Three lines from Google matter most for a property manager:

- **Quality, not method.** Google focuses on the quality of content, not on how it was made (Google Search Central Blog, 2023).
- **No bonus for AI.** "Using AI doesn't give content any special gains. It's just content."
- **Facts need a person.** Google's guidance on generative AI warns that models "predict a likely sequence of words" and do not retrieve facts. It asks you to fact-check all AI content before you publish.

So the question is not "Did AI write it?". The question is "Would an owner learn something true and useful here?".

## What is scaled content abuse?

Scaled content abuse is Google's name for many pages made mainly to manipulate search rankings and not to help users. Google added the policy with the March 2024 core update, on 2024-03-05. Google says it applies "no matter whether content is produced through automation, human efforts, or some combination".

Google's spam policy page lists examples. Two fit property management blogs well:

- Using AI tools "to generate many pages without adding value for users".
- Stitching content from different web pages "without adding value".

Here is how that looks in our field. A firm makes 200 city pages from one template: "Best property management in [city]". Each page swaps the city name and nothing else. The firm does not manage homes in most of those cities. That pattern fits the policy. Google says sites that break spam policies may rank lower or drop out of results.

A firm that writes 4 city pages is in a different place. Each page covers a city where it manages homes, with local fees and local rules. The count is small, and each page has facts only that firm knows.

## What must a human add to an AI-written post?

A person must add the parts AI cannot know and check the parts AI can get wrong. Google's "Who, How, and Why" questions are a good test. Google asks if content is "written or reviewed by an expert or enthusiast who demonstrably knows the topic well".

Use this split of work:

| Task | AI can do it | A person must do it |
|---|---|---|
| Outline from owner questions | Yes | Pick the questions your owners ask |
| First draft | Yes | Cut filler, fix tone |
| Local rules (deposits, notices, rent control) | No, it guesses | Check the statute or city page |
| Your fees and terms | No | Copy from your management agreement |
| Your numbers (days on market, renewals) | No | Pull from your software |
| A real story from a property | No | Write it, with owner consent |
| Fair Housing wording | It may slip | Read every line about tenants |
| Author name and review date | No | Add them |

Before you publish, run this 8-point checklist:

1. **Every number has a source** you opened, or it comes from your own books.
2. **Every legal rule** links to the statute or the government page.
3. **One local fact** appears that a writer outside your city would not know.
4. **One number from your own data** appears, such as average days to lease.
5. **No text steers renters** by any protected class.
6. **A named person** on your team is the author or the reviewer.
7. **The title and meta description** are true. Google says its review advice also applies to metadata.
8. **The post answers one owner question** in its first 2 sentences.

## How many AI blog posts a week is safe?

Google does not publish a safe number of posts per week. Its policy is about purpose and value, not volume. So the real limit is your review time.

Do the math for your team. A careful review of a 1,200-word post with local rules takes time: open each source, check each number, rewrite weak parts. If your reviewer has 3 hours a week and needs 1.5 hours per post, your limit is 2 posts a week. If you publish more than one person can check, quality drops first.

Fewer, deeper posts also fit how AI answers work. An AI engine cites a page when it holds a clear answer or a fact the engine needs. Ten generic posts on "5 tips for landlords" add little. One page with your real fee table can be quoted for months.

## Should you disclose that AI helped write a post?

Disclose AI help where a reader might ask how the post was made. Google says AI or automation disclosures are useful "for content where someone might think 'How was this created?'". Google also says that an AI author byline is probably not the best way to show that AI was part of the work.

A short line is enough: "Drafted with AI help. Checked and edited by [name], [role], on [date]." Put a real person's name in the byline.

We work the same way on this library. Agents draft the pages, and a person checks every fact before a page goes live. That is our own rule, not a Google rule.

## Does AI-written content help with ChatGPT and AI answers?

AI-written content helps with AI answers only when it adds facts that are hard to find elsewhere. AI engines pull from pages that search indexes hold. A post that repeats the top 10 results gives an engine no reason to pick you.

Content that tends to get quoted has specific facts: fees in dollars, local deadlines, door counts, service areas. Our [AI search checklist](/guides/ai-search-checklist-property-management-website) lists the pages owners and AI engines look for first.

Watch for this risk: AI drafts state wrong local rules with confidence. A wrong security deposit deadline on your blog can reach an owner through an AI answer, with your name next to it. Check every rule against the official text.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.
