## What is a pay-or-quit notice?

A pay-or-quit notice is a written demand for overdue rent. The tenant must pay in full by a deadline or give the unit back. If the tenant does neither, the landlord can file an eviction case in court.

The notice is not an eviction. The tenant still lives in the unit, and only a court can order the tenant out. But in most states, a court will not hear a nonpayment case without a correct notice first. A bad notice can sink a good case.

The rules come from state law, and they differ a lot. This guide uses 3 states as examples: California, Florida and New York. Check your own state's statute before you serve.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## What must a pay-or-quit notice include?

A pay-or-quit notice must name the tenant, the unit, the exact rent owed and the deadline. Most states add more items. The table shows what 3 states require.

| Item | California | Florida | New York |
|---|---|---|---|
| Statute or source | California Courts self-help guide | F.S. 83.56(3) | RPAPL 711(2) |
| Days to pay | 3, not counting Saturdays, Sundays, court holidays | 3, not counting Saturday, Sunday, legal holidays | At least 14 |
| Amount | Exact rent owed | Sum owed for rent | Rent demanded |
| Other money allowed? | No: no late fees, utilities or damages | Statute form names rent only | Statute names rent only |
| Payment details | How and where to pay: in person or mail, address, days, hours | Landlord name, address, phone | Not set in 711 |
| Fixed wording | No set form | Statute gives a form "in substantially the following form" | No set form |

The California Courts self-help guide lists the items a California notice must hold. These are the tenant's full names, the rental address, the exact rent owed, and the 3-day deadline to pay or move. The notice must also say how and where to pay. The same guide says the notice cannot ask for late fees, utilities or damages.

Florida Statutes 83.56(3) prints the notice text. It states the sum owed, the address and county, and a pay-or-leave date. It also lists the landlord's name, address and phone number. Use that text close to word for word.

New York RPAPL 711(2) asks for a written demand for the rent, with at least 14 days' notice. The demand gives the tenant a choice: pay the rent or give up the unit.

## How many days does the tenant get?

The tenant gets the number of days in your state's statute, counted the way that statute says. California and Florida both give 3 days, but they skip weekends and holidays. New York gives at least 14 days.

The count is where many notices fail. Here is a worked count for a Florida notice.

1. **Deliver the notice** by hand on Thursday, March 5.
2. **Count day 1** as Friday, March 6.
3. **Skip** Saturday, March 7 and Sunday, March 8.
4. **Count day 2** as Monday, March 9.
5. **Count day 3** as Tuesday, March 10, if it is not a court holiday.
6. **Write "on or before March 10"** as the deadline in the notice.

Florida's statute says that only court-observed holidays count as legal holidays for this rule. California's guide also skips court holidays. Check the court calendar for your county before you fill in the date.

Do not guess the count. A deadline that comes 1 day too early can make the notice fail.

## How do I serve a pay-or-quit notice?

You serve a pay-or-quit notice with a method that your state's law allows. Text, email and a note under the door are often not on the list. Keep proof of the date and method.

| State | Allowed methods (summary) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Florida | Mail, hand delivery, or leave a copy at the home if the tenant is absent | F.S. 83.56(4) |
| New York | Hand to tenant; or leave with a person of suitable age at the property; or post on a conspicuous part or under the door. Each of the last two needs mailing by certified or registered mail and first-class mail within 1 day | RPAPL 711(2), 735 |
| California | Follow the service steps in the CA Courts self-help guide | CA Courts self-help guide |

After you serve, write a short proof of service. Note the date, time, method and the name of the person who served. Take a photo of a posted notice. Keep a copy of the notice in the tenant file.

Florida Statutes 83.56(4) also says the lease cannot waive these notice rules. A lease clause that says "no notice needed" does not help you in Florida.

## What mistakes void a pay-or-quit notice?

Most void notices fail on 1 of 6 points. Use this checklist before you serve.

- **Wrong amount.** The notice asks for more than the rent owed, or adds fees the state does not allow in this notice.
- **Wrong deadline.** The count includes a weekend or a court holiday that the statute excludes.
- **Missing payment details.** In California, the notice must say how, where and when to pay.
- **Wrong names or address.** List every adult tenant on the lease and the full unit address.
- **Bad service.** The method is not on the state's list, or you have no proof of it.
- **Waived by rent.** You took rent after the notice in a way that waived it.

The last point needs care. Florida Statutes 83.56(5)(a) says a landlord who accepts rent with actual knowledge of a noncompliance waives the right to terminate for that noncompliance. If you take part of the rent, ask a local attorney first.

Florida gives one safety net. Florida Statutes 83.60(1)(a) says the landlord must get a chance to cure a deficiency in a notice or pleading before the court dismisses the case. Most states give no such rule, so do not count on it.

Also check local rules. California's self-help guide says cities and counties may require more details in a notice. Some cities also require a warning letter before other notice types.

## What happens after the deadline passes?

After the deadline passes, the tenant has either paid, moved out, or done neither. If the tenant paid the full amount in time, the notice ends and the tenancy goes on. If the tenant did neither, you can file an eviction case in the local court.

Do not change the locks, remove items or shut off utilities. The California Courts guide says a landlord cannot lock a tenant out or shut off gas, water or heat to make the tenant move. Only a court order and a sheriff or marshal can remove a tenant.
