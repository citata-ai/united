## Why do AI engines care about city pages?

AI engines care about city pages because owners ask city questions, and some engines open company sites to answer them. An owner types "best property manager in Mesa for a duplex". The engine searches, reads the pages it finds, and names companies.

In our 50-city test on 2026-10-19, we asked each engine for the best property manager in the 50 largest US cities (our data, [AI Property Manager Index 2026](/research/ai-property-manager-index-2026)). How often each engine linked a manager's own site:

| Engine | Answers that linked a manager's site |
|---|---|
| Gemini | 48 of 50 |
| Claude | 43 of 50 |
| ChatGPT | 15 of 50 |

So for Gemini and Claude, your city page is often the page they read. ChatGPT leans on directories, so the city page helps there only after your listings are right.

Google's guide on generative AI search says there is no special trick for AI features. Content that is "unique, compelling, and useful" is the factor it names as most likely to matter over time.

## What facts make a city page unique?

A city page is unique when it holds facts about your work in that city that no other page has. Property Manager Websites gives a quick test: remove the city name, and the page should still show which market it covers. If it reads the same for any town, it fails.

Use these facts, from strongest to weakest:

| Fact | Example of the form | Where to get it |
|---|---|---|
| Doors you manage in the city | "We manage 42 homes in Mesa." | Your software |
| Property types there | "Mostly 1950s duplexes and 3-bed houses" | Your portfolio |
| Local rules you handle | Rental registration, inspections, permits | City or county site |
| Local rent levels | Fair Market Rent for the metro | HUD data; our [Section 8 rent limits](/guides/section-8-rent-limits) |
| Your fees for that area | Same as your fee table, or the local difference | Your fee page |
| Average days to lease there | "Our homes leased in a median of 21 days" | Your records |
| A review from an owner in that city | Quote with first name and town, with consent | Google, Yelp |
| Your office or staff there | Address, who covers the area | Your team |

The table rows above show the shape of each fact. Use your own real numbers. Never write a number you cannot prove.

Google asks site owners a plain question: "Does the content provide original information, reporting, research, or analysis?" Your door count and lease times in that city are original data. No other site has them.

## How many city pages are too many?

You have too many city pages when you cannot fill each one with real facts. Property Manager Websites advises "Fewer, done well, beats many done thin." It warns that 20 near-identical pages can become "one thin page copied twenty times."

We saw this at Fridman Properties, a Chicago-area manager. Related party: the owner of Fridman Properties is CEO of MyRemotely, our partner. The site had 29 city pages, most for the northwest suburbs. Two of them, Palatine and Skokie, were 96% identical. Most of the company's units were on Chicago's South Side, which had no strong page.

Our baseline ran on 2026-10-16 (our data). AI named Fridman in 6 of 18 answers about the northwest suburbs. It named Fridman in 0 of 42 answers about the South Side. The rewrite of those city pages is a plan, not a result yet. Details are in the Fridman Properties case study on citata.ai.

A rule of thumb we use:

1. **Write a full page** for each city where you manage 10 or more doors, or plan to.
2. **List the rest** in an "areas we serve" section on your main page.
3. **Promote a town to its own page** when you have the facts from the table above.

The 10-door line is our working rule, not a Google rule.

## What is a doorway page?

A doorway page is a page made to rank for one place or phrase that sends visitors on to the real page. Google's spam policy defines doorway abuse as sites or pages "created to rank for specific, similar search queries" that "lead users to intermediate pages that aren't as useful as the final destination."

Google lists examples. Two fit property managers directly:

- "Having multiple domain names or pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page"
- "Creating substantially similar pages that are closer to search results than a clearly defined, browseable hierarchy"

Google's spam policy also names scaled content abuse: "many pages are generated for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users." A batch of 50 template city pages built in an afternoon fits that risk.

How to tell the difference:

| Doorway page | Real city page |
|---|---|
| Same text, city name swapped | Facts only true for that city |
| No doors or reviews there | Doors, owners and reviews there |
| Only goal: send to the contact form | Answers an owner's questions first |
| Not in the site menu | Linked from a locations page |

## How do you link city pages?

Link each city page into a clear tree, so people and crawlers can move up, across and down. Google contrasts doorway pages with "a clearly defined, browseable hierarchy." Build the hierarchy.

1. **Up.** Link each city page to your main property management page and your locations page.
2. **Across.** Link to your fee table, your reviews page and your rental analysis form.
3. **Down.** Link to neighborhood or property-type pages, if you have them.
4. **Between cities.** Link nearby cities only when it helps an owner, such as "We also manage in Tempe."
5. **From outside.** Put the city page URL on your Google Business Profile for that location, and on each directory listing for that city.

Use the same company name, address and phone number on every page. An engine that finds 2 phone numbers for one company has a reason to doubt both.

## What should a city page look like?

A good city page answers an owner's questions in the order they ask them. Use this outline:

1. **One-paragraph answer.** Who you are, how many doors you manage in the city, and what types.
2. **Fees.** One line and a link to the full fee table.
3. **Local rules.** The permits, inspections and notices you handle there, with links to the city site.
4. **Rents.** Local rent data with its source and date.
5. **Proof.** One owner review from that city and your days-to-lease number.
6. **FAQ.** 3 to 5 owner questions about that city.
7. **Next step.** A rental analysis form for that city.

Then check the site-wide items once: crawler access, schema and the same business facts on every page.
