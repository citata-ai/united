## What is stepped-up basis?

Inherited tax basis often starts from fair market value at the owner's death, rather than the owner's original purchase cost. IRS Publication 551 explains the general rule and exceptions. The adjustment can be downward as well as upward.

Ask the estate representative for the relevant valuation and tax records. Do not use an online estimate as the only basis evidence. Tell your preparer how the property passed to you and whether an alternate valuation or other special rule applies.

Separate land and building information for the rental review. Keep the valuation, title documents, estate papers, and any later project costs together. Your preparer needs these facts before calculating depreciation or a later sale result.

An assumed example shows why records matter. A relative paid $80,000, and the supported inherited basis is $300,000. Do not calculate your sale gain from $80,000 just because that was the old purchase price. The example assumes the general rule applies and ignores later adjustments and sale costs.

A basis adjustment does not pay repair bills or make rental income tax-free. Treat the tax history and the operating budget as separate work. Ask for a written explanation of the basis used so future records stay consistent.

## How should co-heirs share rent and costs?

Co-heirs should agree on ownership, decision rights, cash needs, and an exit plan before starting the rental. Ask counsel to check who can act for the estate or owners. Do not assume family agreement replaces legal authority.

Discuss the work as well as the income. Decide who handles the manager, reviews statements, pays urgent bills, and keeps records. If one person supplies labor or cash, state how the owners will treat it.

Use this agreement checklist:

| Issue | Decision to record |
| --- | --- |
| Authority | Who may sign leases and service contracts |
| Cash | Who funds work and future shortfalls |
| Payouts | When owners receive available cash |
| Repairs | Who approves scope and cost |
| Records | Who keeps accounts and shares reports |
| Exit | What happens if an heir wants to sell |

Ask a tax professional how income and costs should enter each owner's return. Do not simply divide bank deposits without checking ownership and expenses.

A manager can handle operations but cannot settle an unresolved family ownership dispute. Make the terms clear first. A predictable decision process protects both the property and the people who depend on it.

## What does the house need before you rent it?

The house needs a condition review, lawful rental use, insurance, and a service plan. A home that suited its former occupant may need work before another household moves in. Inspect before you promise a rent or move-in date.

Check heating, plumbing, wiring, roof, moisture, locks, alarms, and safe access with qualified help. Get complete quotes for identified work. Separate urgent defects from optional upgrades.

Confirm local registration, inspection, permit, and lease requirements with the relevant offices. Have local counsel review any existing occupancy or tenancy. Do not assume the inheritance makes the house vacant or removes earlier obligations.

Tell the insurer about the owner's death, vacancy, and proposed rental use. Ask who must appear on the policy during the ownership change. Keep coverage questions tied to the actual title and control of the home.

Set the rental-ready date from completed work and permission, not a hopeful calendar. IRS Publication 527 links rental depreciation to when property is ready and available for rent. Give your preparer the timeline and project records. Keep the receipts even if you later decide to sell instead.

## How do you compare rent with a sale now?

Compare net rental cash and net sale proceeds using the same property condition. Ask an agent for a supported sale range. Ask a local manager for supported rent and the full operating plan.

Subtract debt, taxes, insurance, management, maintenance, vacancy, and replacement funding from expected collections. Include initial repairs and leasing costs in the starting cash requirement. Use the [management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) for the service-cost review.

For a sale, subtract payoff, selling costs, preparation work, and a tax estimate. Ask whether an as-is sale and a repaired sale create different net results. Do not choose a large project only because the improved sale price sounds higher.

Also compare what each heir needs. One person may want long-term income while another needs cash soon. State those constraints before choosing a strategy that locks everyone's money into the house.

IRS Topic 414 explains rental income and expenses, but it does not decide the investment choice. Have your tax professional estimate the tax side of each route. Keep the rental plan conservative enough to work without a hoped-for rise in property value.

## What should you do before the final choice?

Gather the documents and price the work before you choose. A short period of fact gathering can prevent a rushed lease or an unnecessary renovation. Set a cash limit for that review.

Create a file with title, estate authority, valuation, debts, insurance, inspection, quotes, rent evidence, and sale estimates. List unresolved questions and assign each to the right adviser. Keep the co-heirs informed through the same record.

Review a difficult rental case with lower collections and a repair. Review a difficult sale case with a lower price or longer wait. Ask whether each owner can accept and fund the result.

Your next step is a written rent-versus-sale comparison and a clear co-owner decision. Keep the choice tied to the house's current condition and the owners' needs. Sentimental value can matter, but record it separately from the cash forecast so nobody mistakes one for the other.
