## What is the average sales cycle for a property management lead?

The average sales cycle for a property management owner lead is not known from public data. We looked for a study that tracks owners from first inquiry to a signed management agreement. We did not find one. What we found are agency claims, one large lead study that stops at the form, and a few client stories.

Here is every number we read on 2026-10-19, with what stands behind it.

| Source | What it says | Data behind it |
|---|---|---|
| Property Manager Websites (PMW) blog | Residential owners: 30 to 90 days. HOA boards: 6 to 12 months. Short-term rental owners: 14 to 45 days. | None cited for these ranges |
| PMW blog, same page | "Around three weeks" from lead to client | Cites a Promodo article; see the next row |
| Promodo blog | About 90 days lead to opportunity, plus about 20 days to close: about 110 days | Agency's own estimate, for real estate sales, not property management |
| Goodjuju lead source report 2026 | 2,525 owner leads from 35 companies | Counts form submissions only; no signing dates |
| Goodjuju website benchmark | Median company: 8.9 owner leads per 1,000 visitors | No lead-to-signed data |
| Goodjuju case, Hallmark | 3 owners signed from website leads in one month; 3 from a paid directory over 4 to 5 months | One client's account on a call |
| Goodjuju case, Traverse | Owner quotes a 38% "closing ratio" | The page does not define the ratio or the period |

Two lessons come out of this table. First, the most quoted number does not match its own source. The PMW page says "three weeks" and links to Promodo, but the Promodo page we read gives about 110 days. Second, the best lead data in the industry stops at the form. Goodjuju says so: its lead counts are form submissions, not qualified leads.

So if a vendor quotes you an average sales cycle, ask to see the data set.

## Why do some owners take months to sign?

Some owners take months to sign because the decision depends on events outside your sales process. The owner is not ready until one of those events happens. Common triggers:

- **A lease ends.** Many owners switch managers or start with one when a tenant moves out.
- **A contract with the current manager runs out.** The owner may wait for a notice period or a renewal date.
- **A sell-or-rent decision.** An owner with an inherited or vacant house may compare a sale with a rental first.
- **A first bad experience.** An owner who self-managed through one hard tenant may start to look, then stop.
- **A move.** An owner who takes a job in another city needs a manager on a fixed date.

PMW also says HOA boards take longer than single owners, because a board votes. That fits common sense, but PMW gives no data for it.

The point for you: a long cycle is often a timing problem, not a pitch problem. Your follow-up must still be there when the trigger happens.

## How should follow-up change over a long sales cycle?

Follow-up should be fast in the first hour and slow but steady after that. The best public data on speed is old and not from property management, but it is a real study.

The InsideSales.com and MIT Lead Response Management Study (2007) looked at 3 years of data from 6 companies. It covered over 15,000 web leads and over 100,000 call attempts. Its findings:

- The odds of contact dropped 100 times when the first call came at 30 minutes, not 5.
- The odds of qualifying the lead dropped 21 times over the same gap.
- After 20 hours, more call attempts hurt the odds of contact.
- The study did not measure close rates.

Use those findings for the first day only. After day 1, the owner sets the pace. Here is a follow-up plan you can adapt.

| Stage | Timing | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| New inquiry | Within 5 minutes | Call. If no answer, send a short text and email with your name. |
| Day 1 | Same day | Send the rental analysis or fee table the owner asked for. |
| Week 1 | Day 3 to 7 | One call to answer questions. Ask what date drives the decision. |
| Not ready yet | Each month | One useful email: a local rent change, a law change, a short owner tip. |
| Trigger date | 30 days before | Call. Offer a walk-through or a written proposal. |
| Lost to another manager | 6 months later | One check-in. Some owners are not happy with their first choice. |

The key field is the trigger date. If you know the lease end date, you know when to call. Ask for it on the first call.

## How do I measure my own sales cycle?

You measure your own sales cycle by logging two dates for every owner: the first inquiry and the signed agreement. The days between them are that owner's cycle. Most CRMs and property management tools can hold these as custom fields.

Steps:

1. **Add 5 fields** to each owner lead: first inquiry date, lead source, door count, signed date, and lost reason.
2. **Log every lead**, not only the ones that sign. Lost leads tell you where the cycle breaks.
3. **Compute days to sign** for each signed owner: signed date minus first inquiry date.
4. **Report the median**, not the mean. One owner who signs after a year pulls the mean up.
5. **Split by source.** Website, referral, directory and AI answers may have different cycles.
6. **Repeat each quarter.** One quarter of data is a small sample.

Worked example (sample numbers, not data). You signed 5 owners last quarter. Their days to sign were 9, 21, 34, 58 and 140.

| Measure | Math | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Median | Middle value of 9, 21, 34, 58, 140 | 34 days |
| Mean | (9 + 21 + 34 + 58 + 140) / 5 | 52.4 days |

The mean says 52 days. But 4 of 5 owners signed in 58 days or less. The median, 34 days, is the better planning number. Keep the 140-day owner in the log: that owner is the reason to keep monthly follow-up.

## Where is the data thin?

The data is thin at every step after the form. Here is what no public source we read measures:

- days from inquiry to signed agreement, by lead source
- the share of owner leads that sign, defined the same way across companies
- how cycle length changes with door count or property type

Goodjuju notes that its sample is its own clients, which "are likely stronger in Google and in AI answers than the average PM company." The Hallmark and Traverse numbers are client quotes on a vendor's site. The MIT study is from 2007 and covers other industries.

So treat every number on this page as a starting point. Your own log of 20 or 30 signed owners will tell you more than any of these sources. If owner inquiries are down, not slow, read [why owner leads are dropping](/guides/why-owner-leads-are-dropping) first.
