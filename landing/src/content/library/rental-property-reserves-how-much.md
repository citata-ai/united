## What is a reserve per door?

A reserve per door is cash you assign to each rental unit for future needs. Use that figure as a tracking measure, not a universal rule. Similar unit counts can hide very different repair and debt risks.

Separate operating cash, emergency cash, and planned replacement money. A roof you expect to replace belongs in the plan. An unexpected leak needs a separate response fund. Avoid counting the same dollars as full protection against every event.

Here is an assumed planning example, not an industry standard:

| Cash need | Assumption | Planned amount |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Fixed bills during vacancy | $1,500 for 3 months | $4,500 |
| Repair shock | Owner planning allowance | $2,500 |
| Insurance deductible | Assumed policy term | $2,000 |
| Combined scenario target | Sum of these assumptions | $9,000 |

The example adds the risks because it tests their overlap. Your target can differ. Use actual policy terms and repair quotes to replace each assumption. Add known replacements separately so an emergency does not consume money already assigned to major work.

## What do lenders require in reserves?

Lender reserve requirements depend on the loan, property, and borrower file. They are not a complete repair budget. Ask the lender for the required amount and which assets count.

Fannie Mae's Minimum Reserve Requirements guide measures reserves in months of the qualifying housing payment. Its Desktop Underwriter rules list 6 months for an investment property transaction. Additional financed properties can create additional requirements.

The lender's calculation can accept some assets you would not want to sell for an urgent repair. Ask about the documentation and availability of each account. Do not assume mortgage approval proves your emergency cash is enough.

Also separate closing cash from post-closing reserves. Buying the property, paying initial work, and funding the first lease can consume money before the first rent arrives. Keep those costs visible in the purchase plan.

If you refinance, ask how the new payment changes the reserve amount. Fannie Mae excludes subject-property cash-out proceeds from acceptable reserve sources. Get the lender's written calculation before you commit cash elsewhere. Treat the requirement as a financing condition and your own reserve as an operating decision.

## Where should reserve money sit?

Reserve money should be available when the property needs it. Choose the account by access, ownership, protection, and payment timing. A high advertised yield does not answer those questions.

Investor.gov defines liquidity as how easily or quickly a security can be bought or sold. For an owner reserve, also ask how fast cash reaches the contractor. A market sale, bank transfer, and vendor payment can follow different timelines.

Keep a list of accounts and authorized people. Confirm transfer limits, weekend access, and the steps for a large payment. Test the payment route before an emergency. Keep your account recovery information current.

If you choose a deposit account, verify the bank and the applicable deposit protection directly. If you choose an investment product, understand its value and access risks. Do not treat a product name as proof that the balance cannot decline.

Separate your rental reserve from tenant deposit records. Check local handling rules with counsel. A tenant deposit has a different purpose from owner emergency money. Your cash worksheet should make that distinction clear even when the manager's portal displays several balances together.

## How does a manager hold an owner reserve?

A manager-held owner reserve is money the manager retains under your agreement to pay property costs. Ask where it appears on the statement and what the firm can use it for. Read the replenishment terms.

Clarify whether the reserve applies per property or across your portfolio. Ask who authorizes transfers between properties. A balance labeled available may still sit beside pending bills, so request a clear explanation of payout timing.

Set a repair authority limit alongside the reserve. The money available and the manager's authority are separate terms. Having enough cash should not create unclear permission for an unrelated project.

Ask what happens when a bill exceeds the reserve. Confirm the contact process, payment deadline, and any manager advance fee. Keep your own accessible cash outside the manager's account for costs you pay directly.

Use the [management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to compare service costs. Do not count an owner reserve contribution as a management fee. IRS Topic 414 focuses on actual rental income and expenses. Ask your preparer how the underlying payments enter your books rather than deducting a transfer by its label.

## How often should you review the target?

Review the reserve when the property, debt, insurance, or lease changes. Use each review to replace old assumptions with real bills. A new roof can remove one planned project while a larger deductible adds a new cash need.

Check what happened during the last vacancy or repair. Did the reserve cover the bills and payment delays? Add a note where the actual result differed from the plan.

Before taking an owner payout, subtract committed work and near-term bills. Then compare the remaining cash with your target. If the balance is low, choose a clear funding plan rather than waiting for a repair to force a decision.

Write the target and its assumptions on a single page. Share the relevant part with your manager and financial adviser. A useful reserve plan explains what it covers, where the money sits, and who can act when you are unavailable.
