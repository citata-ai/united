You check the account on the 5th. Rent came in. The mortgage, the HOA and a plumber went out. The balance is lower than last month, again. That pattern is negative cash flow, and it needs a plan, not hope.

This guide gives the plan in four steps: find the leak, fix what you control, test a refinance, and decide whether to sell.

## How Do I Find Where the Money Goes?

Build a 12-month ledger from your bank account, not from memory. Put every dollar in one of the lines below. Then compare each line with a market number.

| Line | Your 12-month total | What to compare it with |
|---|---|---|
| Rent collected | | Rent for 3 similar homes nearby |
| Vacancy (rent lost) | | Days empty between tenants |
| Mortgage principal and interest | | A current refinance quote |
| Property tax | | Assessed value vs. market value |
| Insurance | | 2 or 3 new quotes, same coverage |
| HOA dues | | Fixed; check for special assessments |
| Repairs | | Your past 3 years |
| Big replacements (CapEx) | | Age of roof, HVAC, water heater |
| Utilities you pay | | What the lease says the tenant pays |
| Management | | Quotes from 2 or 3 managers |

IRS Publication 527 lists the common rental expenses: advertising, cleaning and maintenance, insurance, interest, legal fees, management fees, repairs, taxes and utilities. Use the same lines. Your tax return then matches your ledger.

Here is an example with invented numbers for one house:

| Line | Monthly |
|---|---|
| Rent | $2,100 |
| Vacancy (1 month a year) | −$175 |
| Mortgage P&I | −$1,560 |
| Tax | −$330 |
| Insurance | −$160 |
| Repairs and reserve | −$200 |
| Management | −$170 |
| Net cash flow | −$495 |

In this example, the leak is not one big line. It is rent below market, a high mortgage payment and a vacancy month together.

## Can I Raise Rent or Cut Costs Safely?

Yes, within the law and the lease. Start with the lines that move the most money with the least risk.

**Rent.** If your rent is below the market for 3 similar homes, raise it at renewal. State law sets the notice and, in some states, the size of the increase.

| State | Rent increase rule | Law |
|---|---|---|
| Washington | At least 90 days' written notice; the increase can't take effect before the lease term ends | RCW 59.18.140(3)(a) |
| Oregon | No increase in the first year; 90 days' written notice; once per 12 months; capped at the lesser of 10% or 7% plus CPI | ORS 90.323(2), 90.324(1) |

Oregon's state agency publishes the cap each year by September 30 for the next year, per ORS 90.324. Most states have different rules, and some cities add their own. Check before you send a notice.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

**Vacancy.** One empty month costs about 8% of a year's rent. Start renewal talks 90 days before the lease ends. A small rent increase that keeps a good tenant can beat a large one that leads to a vacancy.

**Insurance.** Shop the policy at renewal. The Texas Department of Insurance says different companies charge different rates for the same risk. It lists discounts some companies give, such as monitored alarms and impact-resistant roofs.

**Property tax.** If the assessed value is above market value, ask your county how to appeal.

**Repairs.** Fix small problems early. A slow leak becomes a floor replacement.

**Management.** Compare your fee with other quotes using the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator). Cutting a good manager can raise vacancy, so compare the full result.

## When Does a Refinance Help?

A refinance helps when the new payment, after closing costs, is lower than the old one for the time you will hold the loan. It does not help when today's rates are higher than your current rate.

On 2026-10-15, the Freddie Mac 30-year fixed average was 7.40%, per FRED. Your own quote may differ, so use your lender's numbers.

Run this test with invented numbers:

| Item | Current loan | Refinance quote |
|---|---|---|
| Balance | $225,000 | $225,000 |
| Rate and term | 7.40%, 30 years | 6.50%, 30 years |
| Monthly P&I | $1,558 | $1,422 |
| Monthly saving | | $136 |
| Closing costs | | $6,000 |
| Months to break even ($6,000 ÷ $136) | | 44 |

If you will keep the home more than 44 months, the refinance pays off in this example. If you might sell in 2 years, it does not.

A cash-out refinance does the opposite. It raises the balance and the payment. Fannie Mae's eligibility matrix caps cash-out on a 1-unit investment property at 75% LTV. Use cash-out only for a planned repair that raises rent.

## Do Taxes Change the Picture?

Yes. A cash loss and a tax loss are different things. Depreciation can create a tax loss even when cash flow is positive.

IRS Publication 925 lets owners who actively participate deduct up to $25,000 of rental loss against other income. The allowance phases out between $100,000 and $150,000 of modified adjusted gross income. Losses you can't use carry forward.

This is general information, not tax advice. Check the current law or ask a tax professional.

## When Is It Time to Sell?

It may be time to sell when three things are true:

1. **You fixed the controllable lines.** Rent is at market. Insurance and tax are checked. Vacancy is low.
2. **The loss continues.** The monthly gap is still negative after the fixes.
3. **Growth will not close the gap.** You do not expect rent or value growth large enough to make up for the monthly loss.

Before you list, run the after-tax numbers. The sale gain may include depreciation, taxed at up to 25%. Publication 925 says suspended passive losses are generally allowed in full when you sell your entire interest in a taxable sale to an unrelated buyer. That can lower the tax bill in the sale year.

## What Is the One-Page Action Plan?

- **This week:** build the 12-month ledger.
- **This month:** get 3 rent comps, 2 insurance quotes and 1 refinance quote.
- **Next renewal:** set rent at market, with the notice your state requires.
- **In 6 months:** review the ledger again.
- **If still negative:** decide to hold with a set monthly limit, or sell.
