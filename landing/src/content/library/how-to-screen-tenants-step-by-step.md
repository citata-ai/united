## What should be in written screening criteria?

Written screening criteria are the rules you use to say yes or no, set before the first showing. They protect you because every applicant meets the same bar. In Oregon they are a legal step: ORS 90.295 bars a screening charge unless the landlord first adopts written criteria and gives them to the applicant.

Put these items in your criteria:

| Item | Example wording |
|---|---|
| Income | "Gross monthly income of at least 3 times the rent, all adults combined." |
| Credit | "No open rental debt in collections." |
| Rental history | "No eviction judgment in the past 5 years." |
| Criminal history | Name the crime types and the look-back period that your state allows. |
| Occupancy | "Up to 2 people per bedroom, plus local code limits." |
| Fees | Amount, what it pays for, refund rules. |
| Tie-break rule | "First complete application that meets all criteria." |
| Your rights notice | Dispute rights and any appeal process. |

Oregon's list of required disclosures is a good model even outside Oregon. It includes the screening process, dispute rights, the nondiscrimination policy, the rent and deposits, and any renter insurance rule.

## In what order do I check credit, income and references?

Check the cheap and fast items first, and order the paid report last. That way you do not pay for a report on an incomplete file.

1. **Publish criteria with the listing.** Give a copy to every person who asks for an application.
2. **Take complete applications only.** Stamp the date and time on each one. Process them in that order.
3. **Check identity.** Match the photo ID to the application. Virginia, for example, lets a landlord ask for a Social Security number or an ITIN.
4. **Check income.** Pay stubs, an offer letter, benefit letters or bank statements. Count all lawful income the same way.
5. **Run the consumer report with written consent.** The FTC says written permission gives you a permissible purpose. You must certify that you use the report only for housing.
6. **Call rental references.** Ask the same 4 or 5 questions each time, and write down the answers.
7. **Decide and send notices.** Approve in writing, or send an adverse action notice if any report played a part.

## When do I owe an adverse action notice?

You owe a notice when a consumer report plays any part in a decision that goes against the applicant. The FTC lists denial, a co-signer demand, a larger deposit and higher rent. A notice is due "even if the information in the report played only a small part."

The notice must include:

- The name, address and phone number of the reporting company.
- A statement that the company did not make the decision and cannot give reasons.
- The right to a free copy of the report if asked for within 60 days.
- The right to dispute wrong information.

If you used a credit score, add the score and the key factors behind it. After you finish with the report, shred it or wipe the file. The FTC calls this the Disposal Rule.

## How long does screening take?

No law sets one national deadline. Most of the wait is for other people: employers and past landlords may take days to call back. Set a target, such as a decision within 2 business days of a complete application, and tell applicants. Two state rules to know:

- **Oregon:** a landlord may charge only one screening charge in any 60-day period, no matter how many units the applicant applies for (ORS 90.295).
- **Virginia:** if an applicant does not rent, the landlord must refund the application deposit above actual costs within 20 days, with an itemized list (Va. Code 55.1-1203).

## How much can I charge for an application?

The fee limit depends on the state. Two examples:

| State | Rule | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Virginia | Max $50, plus actual third-party screening costs. Max $32 for HUD-regulated units. | Va. Code 55.1-1203(C) |
| Oregon | No more than average actual cost, or the customary screening company charge. Receipt required. | ORS 90.295(2) |

Check your state before you set a fee. Some states set no cap. Others require a refund if you never run the report.

## How do I compare two qualified applicants?

Use the tie-break rule you wrote down before you listed the unit. The most common rule is the first complete application that meets every criterion. A second option is a lottery among qualified applicants who applied by a set date.

Do not compare on "fit," "feel" or who seems "like a good neighbor." Those words hide bias. Record which rule you used and why the second applicant did not get the unit.

## How do I stay consistent for Fair Housing?

Consistency is your best defense. HUD's rule at 24 CFR 100.75 bans statements to staff or renters that show a preference by a protected class. The same text in every ad and every reply helps you show equal treatment.

Watch for:

- **Blanket criminal bans.** The FTC page warns that refusing everyone with a criminal record may violate the Fair Housing Act. HUD withdrew its 2016 criminal records guidance, effective September 25, 2025 (Federal Register, July 17, 2026). The Act itself did not change.
- **State limits.** Oregon's ORS 90.303 bars a landlord from considering an eviction case that was dismissed or a judgment 5 or more years old. It also limits criminal history to set crime types.
- **Source of income.** Many states and cities protect it. Count voucher income the same as wages where the law requires.
