## Does your condo association allow rentals?

The current governing documents determine the association's rental rules, subject to applicable law. Obtain the declaration, bylaws, rules, amendments, and current rental policy. An old sales listing is not enough evidence.

NAIC warns that association rules can limit home-sharing. That warning is a reason to check the documents, not proof that every condo bans rentals. Annual leases and shorter stays may face different terms.

Ask the association manager for a written answer about your exact proposed use. Describe the lease length, whether you will furnish the unit, and whether you will remain an occupant. Ask which document supports the answer.

| Check | Question to resolve before listing |
|---|---|
| Rental permission | Is this unit eligible under the current rules? |
| Rental cap | Is a slot available, and is there a waitlist? |
| Lease term | Does the proposed agreement meet the permitted term? |
| Tenant review | Which forms, fees, and approvals apply? |
| Move-in access | Are elevator booking, keys, or deposits required? |
| Rule changes | Are pending amendments relevant to this lease? |

Do not treat a manager's casual phone answer as a legal opinion. If the documents conflict or an amendment affects you, ask a local attorney to review them. Save the final response with your lease file.

## How do HOA fees change cash flow?

Association dues reduce the cash available from rent, even when the unit is empty. Read the current budget and recent statements. Identify what the dues include so you do not count the same expense twice.

For example, water or building insurance may appear in association charges. That does not prove your individual unit has no extra utility or insurance bill. Ask for a clear allocation of owner and association duties.

Here is a hypothetical monthly budget, using invented inputs. Assume rent is $2,200, principal and interest are $1,100, and dues are $450. That leaves $650 before taxes, unit insurance, maintenance, vacancy, and management.

If those remaining costs total an assumed $600, only $50 remains. A further assumed $200 monthly assessment would make the result negative $150. The example shows cost order, not a forecast for your building.

Review known projects and assessment notices before setting a rent target. Ask how the association funds major work. A low current due does not prove the building has enough cash for its next project.

## What does the association master policy not cover?

The master policy covers only what its actual terms include. Travelers explains that the building and individual unit can have different coverage needs. Review the master policy together with your proposed unit policy.

Ask the insurer about unit interiors, fixtures, owner furniture, liability, deductibles, and assessment-related coverage. Describe the rental use accurately. A policy suited to an owner-occupied condo may not fit a home rented to others.

Travelers' landlord page describes property coverage and possible liability and loss-of-use protection, subject to terms. Its loss-of-use description concerns a home made unfit by a covered loss. Do not assume it covers ordinary vacancy or every unpaid rent event.

Ask who pays a master-policy deductible after damage involving your unit. The answer may depend on association documents, law, and policy language. Request the specific provision rather than relying on a general coverage summary.

Give your insurer the same lease plan you give the association. The approvals address different questions. Association permission does not itself confirm insurance coverage.

## Do you need HOA approval for a tenant?

Tenant approval depends on your association's current process and applicable law. Ask about forms, review steps, fees, timing, and required lease language. Finish this review before offering a fixed move-in date.

Clarify whether approval covers the lease, the occupant, or both. Ask what happens when a household member changes or a lease renews. Keep the process in your calendar so an administrative delay does not create a vacancy.

Use clear conditions in an attorney-reviewed agreement where needed. Do not promise access that the building cannot provide. Explain key pickup, parking, deliveries, elevator use, and any move-in booking process to the tenant.

Screen applicants with consistent, lawful criteria. Association review does not remove the owner's own duties. If a rule or requested decision appears unlawful, pause the application decision and ask counsel how to proceed.

Provide the tenant with the rules that apply to everyday use. A lease file full of documents is less useful when the tenant cannot find the contact for a water leak. Include a clear repair and building emergency process.

## Which records should you keep after the tenant moves in?

Keep association records beside the lease, insurance, and repair records. Save approvals, rule notices, dues statements, assessment details, and proof of payment. Record who handles each notice when you use a manager.

IRS Publication 527 discusses condominium rental expenses. Regular maintenance-related dues and capital assessments can receive different tax treatment. Give your tax professional the purpose of each charge rather than only a payment total.

Use this handover checklist before giving out keys:

- **Confirm permission** for the unit and lease you actually signed.
- **Save approval** and complete any move-in booking.
- **Bind coverage** for the correct owner and rental use.
- **Record condition** with dated photos and a key list.
- **Assign contacts** for unit repairs and building emergencies.
- **Update the budget** with dues, assessments, and actual quoted costs.
