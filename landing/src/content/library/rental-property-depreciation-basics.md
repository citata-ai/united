## Why does the IRS treat a rental as a 27.5-year asset?

The IRS treats a rental building as a 27.5-year asset because the tax law sets that recovery period for residential rental property. IRS Publication 527, Table 2-1, lists residential rental property at 27.5 years under the General Depreciation System (GDS).

Depreciation is how you recover the cost of the building. Pub 527 calls it a capital expense, taken over the expected life of the property. You do not deduct the price in the year you buy. You deduct a part of it each year.

Recovery periods from Pub 527, Table 2-1, for common rental items:

| Item | GDS years | ADS years |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| Residential rental building and structural parts (furnace, water pipes, venting) | 27.5 | 30 (placed in service after 2017) |
| Appliances, carpets, furniture used in the rental | 5 | 9 |
| Fences, shrubbery, roads | 15 | 20 |
| Additions and improvements, such as a new roof | Same as the building | Same as the building |

ADS is the Alternative Depreciation System. Most owners use GDS unless a rule requires ADS.

## What is the depreciable basis and why is land excluded?

The depreciable basis is the cost of the building, plus certain purchase costs, without the land. Land is excluded because, per Pub 527, it generally does not wear out, become obsolete or get used up.

Cost basis usually includes:

- **The price** paid for the property.
- **Some settlement costs**, such as legal fees, recording fees, surveys and title insurance (Pub 527 lists these under settlement fees).
- **Improvements** made before you rent it.

How to split land and building: Pub 527 says to divide the cost by the ratio of the fair market value (FMV) of each part at the time of purchase. If you are not sure of the values, you can use the ratio from the property tax assessment.

Worked split, with made-up numbers:

| Line | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Price plus allowed settlement costs | $300,000 |
| Assessor's value: land | $60,000 |
| Assessor's value: building | $180,000 |
| Building share ($180,000 / $240,000) | 75% |
| Depreciable basis ($300,000 x 75%) | $225,000 |

A former home has its own rule. Pub 527 says the basis for depreciation is the lesser of the FMV on the date you change it to rental use or your adjusted basis on that date. If the home lost value, you use the lower FMV.

## When does depreciation start?

Depreciation starts when the property is ready and available for rent. It does not wait for a tenant.

Pub 527 gives examples:

- **Ready on July 5, rented September 1.** The house is placed in service in July. Depreciation starts in July.
- **Moved out in July, repaired in August and September, listed October 1, rented December 1.** The house is placed in service in October, when it was listed.

Residential rental property uses the mid-month convention. IRS Publication 946 says this treats the property as placed in service at the midpoint of the month. So a house placed in service in any month gets half a month for that month.

## Can you show a worked example?

Yes. This example is from IRS Publication 527, with one more year added.

| Line | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Depreciable basis | $160,000 |
| Placed in service | February, Year 1 |
| Recovery period (GDS) | 27.5 years |
| Year 1 rate (Pub 527, Table 2-2d, February) | 3.182% |
| Year 1 depreciation | $5,091 |
| Full-year depreciation ($160,000 / 27.5) | $5,818 |
| Years 2 through 27 | $5,818 per year |

The first year is lower because the house was in service for only 10.5 months under the mid-month rule.

Now the effect on a sale, with made-up numbers. Say the owner sells after 5 full years.

| Line | Amount |
|------|--------|
| Total basis (land $40,000 + building $160,000) | $200,000 |
| Depreciation, about 5 years | −$28,363 |
| Adjusted basis | $171,637 |
| Sale price, net of costs | $260,000 |
| Gain | $88,363 |

Without depreciation, the gain would be $60,000. The extra $28,363 of gain matches the deductions taken. That is why depreciation is a deferral, not a gift.

## Must I take depreciation (allowed or allowable)?

You should take it, because the IRS lowers your basis either way. IRS Publication 946 says you must reduce the basis of property by the depreciation allowed or allowable, whichever is greater.

- **Allowed** is depreciation you actually deducted.
- **Allowable** is depreciation you were entitled to deduct.

Pub 946 states that if you do not claim depreciation you are entitled to, you must still reduce the basis by the full allowable amount. So an owner who skips depreciation pays tax at sale as if they took it, without the yearly deductions.

The home sale exclusion does not erase it either. IRS Pub 523 says you cannot exclude the part of your gain equal to depreciation allowed or allowable after May 6, 1997.

If you missed depreciation in past years, Pub 946 describes ways to correct it, such as an amended return or a change in accounting method. Ask a tax professional.

## Depreciation checklist

- **Find your basis:** price, settlement costs and improvements.
- **Split land and building** using FMV or the assessment ratio.
- **Write down the date** the home was ready and available for rent.
- **Use Form 4562** in the first year (Pub 527).
- **Track improvements separately.** Pub 527 says improvements are depreciated as if they were separate property.
- **Keep the records until you sell**, and after.
