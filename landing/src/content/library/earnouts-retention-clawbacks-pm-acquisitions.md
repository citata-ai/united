## What is a retention clawback?

A retention clawback is a contract term that links purchase value to keeping specified client accounts. The agreement may reduce a later payment or require repayment, depending on its wording. Do not assume those structures have the same cash or legal effect.

An earnout is related but different. It makes a later payment depend on an agreed result. That result might use retained fee revenue rather than a count of managed homes. The final agreement needs to name the measure.

The SBA recommends reviewing contracts, financial statements, tax returns and purchase price adjustments when buying a business. Its guidance also recommends help from an attorney and accountant. Use that review to connect the deal formula with records you can inspect.

| Term | Define before signing | Risk if left unclear |
|---|---|---|
| Eligible account | Agreed closing schedule | Disputes over which homes count |
| Retention event | Active agreement, fees or another test | Different meanings of retained |
| Payment date | Calculation and payment calendar | Uncertain cash receipt |
| Exclusion | Events that do not reduce value | Seller bears events outside control |
| Dispute process | Records, review and resolution | No practical way to check the result |

Keep a signed account schedule with the agreement. Include the client identifier and relevant fee terms. Protect personal data during review and share only what the parties need.

## How long is a typical earnout period?

There is no verified typical period in this guide. Choose a period after you inspect the business cycle, contract renewal dates and the buyer's transition plan.

A longer period gives more time to observe retention, but it also delays payment. It may expose the seller to decisions made after the seller loses control. A short period can miss renewal events that the buyer wants to test.

Ask what the proposed period measures. If the stated goal is a smooth account transfer, identify the transfer milestones. If the goal is revenue growth, identify the staff, pricing and budget decisions that support growth.

Model the cash timing without assuming every payment arrives. Show cash at close, deferred fixed payments and contingent payments separately. Include the seller's work duties and any cost of staying through the measurement period.

Do not call an earnout guaranteed money in your personal planning. Ask advisers how security, guarantees, setoff rights and buyer credit risk affect collection. The best headline price may include more uncertain cash than a smaller offer.

Have your accountant examine the tax treatment of each payment type. This is general information, not tax advice. Check the current law or ask a tax professional.

## How should lost doors be counted?

Lost doors should be counted against a fixed opening schedule using a defined loss event. A door means a managed rental unit here. An owner with several homes can create a large change from a single cancellation.

Separate door retention from revenue retention. A low-fee home and a high-fee home have different effects on income. If the agreement uses revenue, define refunds, fee changes, waived fees and pass-through amounts.

For an invented example, assume the closing schedule contains 100 eligible homes. If 8 leave and no exclusions apply, 92 remain. The retained share is 92%. This calculation does not set a purchase price or clawback.

If the parties use a payment pool, state whether the retained share multiplies the whole pool or only changes payment below a threshold. Those formulas can yield different amounts from the same loss count. Ask advisers to test both with the same sample data.

Discuss homes sold, homes withdrawn for owner use and accounts ended by the buyer. Also discuss buyer fee increases and service changes. Decide which events count rather than arguing about fairness after they occur.

Keep a dated event log with the cancellation notice and stated reason. Give both parties a way to inspect supporting records without exposing unrelated client data.

## Which terms can protect the seller?

Seller protections should connect payment risk with control and access to evidence. They need legal review and a buyer willing to accept them.

Ask for a consistent calculation method and regular reports. Define which records the buyer must retain and when the seller can inspect them. A right to dispute a number has little use without access to its source.

Discuss how the buyer may change fees, staffing and service during the measurement period. Do not assume the buyer will keep the seller's old operating model. If a change affects payment, the agreement should address the effect.

List exclusions and avoid vague phrases such as ordinary loss without a definition. State how replacements, merged owner accounts and acquired homes affect the opening schedule. Make sure the same account cannot create several deductions for the same event.

Review the process for a disputed amount. Ask counsel about deadlines, independent review, cost allocation and whether the buyer can withhold unrelated sums. Put the agreed process in the contract.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## How can I compare offers with different structures?

Compare offers with a payment table and a shared set of assumptions. Keep price, timing, risk and required work in separate columns.

Create a full-payment case and a downside case using clearly labeled planning assumptions. Change retention and revenue inputs rather than treating the advertised price as cash. Show which party controls each assumption.

Review your recurring fee quality before you negotiate. The [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) can help inspect fee economics, but it does not value a company. Ask the buyer to explain how account margin, concentration and transfer risk affect its offer.

Before you sign a letter of intent, give your advisers the proposed calculation and account schedule. Ask them to calculate a sample payment independently. Resolve any difference in interpretation while the deal terms remain open.
