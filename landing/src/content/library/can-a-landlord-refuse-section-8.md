## Does federal law require landlords to accept vouchers?

Federal law does not require most private landlords to accept Housing Choice Vouchers. The voucher program is a contract between the owner and the public housing agency (PHA). The federal rule on owner approval, 24 CFR 982.306(e), even says the rule does not give any owner a right to take part. Participation is a choice on both sides.

The federal Fair Housing Act does not list source of income as a protected class. 42 U.S.C. 3604 covers race, color, religion, sex, disability (the statute says "handicap"), familial status and national origin.

There are exceptions. PRRAC's compilation (updated March 2026) lists federal programs whose owners cannot refuse a renter only because the renter holds a voucher:

- **Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC) units.** 26 U.S.C. 42(h)(6)(B)(iv) bars a refusal based on voucher status.
- **HOME-assisted rental units.** 24 CFR 92.252(d) has the same rule.
- **Other programs.** PRRAC also lists Mark-to-Market, the Capital Magnet Fund, the National Housing Trust Fund and some properties bought from HUD.

If your building has any of these funds, the voucher question is already settled for you.

## What do source-of-income laws change?

A source-of-income law makes it illegal to refuse a renter because of a lawful source of money, such as a voucher. PRRAC's March 2026 list names 21 state-level entries with such laws: 19 states plus the District of Columbia and Guam. PRRAC estimates that over 60% of voucher holders now live where such a law protects them.

| Jurisdiction | Law (as PRRAC cites it) | Voucher note |
|---|---|---|
| California | Cal. Gov't Code 12955 | Covers vouchers since 2020 |
| Colorado | Colo. Rev. Stat. 24-34-502 | Covers vouchers since 2021 |
| Connecticut | Conn. Gen. Stat. 46a-64c | In place since 1989 |
| Illinois | 775 ILCS 5/1-103 | Human Rights Act amendment |
| Michigan | MCL 554.601c | Landlords with 5 or more units |
| New Jersey | N.J. Stat. 10:5-12(g) | Names Housing Choice Vouchers |
| New York | State law since 2019 | Courts have upheld it |
| Washington | RCW 59.18.255 | In effect since 2018-09-30 |

PRRAC also names Maine and Minnesota as states where court rulings weakened the law. It lists Wisconsin's law as one that does not apply to vouchers. Seven states, including Florida, Texas and Indiana, have laws that block cities from passing their own protections.

Local laws add another layer. PRRAC lists cities such as Chicago, Los Angeles, San Diego, Denver and Phoenix. A city law can apply even where the state has none.

## Is "no Section 8" in an ad legal?

In a place with a source-of-income law, a "no Section 8" ad is likely illegal. Washington's RCW 59.18.255(2) is a clear example. It says a landlord may not publish any notice or ad that "indicates a preference, limitation, or requirement based on any source of income."

In a place without such a law, the ad may be legal under federal law. But it is still a risk. Ads also fall under 42 U.S.C. 3604(c), which bars any ad that shows a preference based on a protected class. If your words or your pattern of refusals point to one of those classes, the ad can cause a fair housing claim.

## Can I use the same screening rules for all applicants?

Yes. The federal rule gives screening to the owner. 24 CFR 982.307(a)(2) says the owner is responsible for screening and selection. The PHA must tell you so at or before it approves the tenancy.

Under 24 CFR 982.307(a)(3), you may look at a family's history on these points:

1. **Rent and utility payments.** Did the family pay on time?
2. **Care of the unit.** Did the family damage past units?
3. **Respect for neighbors.** Did the family disturb other residents?
4. **Criminal activity.** Drug-related or other criminal activity that threatens health, safety or property.
5. **Other lease terms.** Compliance with other essential conditions of tenancy.

Apply one written standard to every applicant. Some states also limit income tests. Washington's RCW 59.18.255(3) says that if you use an income threshold, you must subtract the voucher amount from the rent before you apply it. In practice, you test the renter's income against the renter's share, not the full rent. Our [Section 8 rent calculator](/tools/section-8-rent-calculator) shows how that share is set.

## What are the risks of a refusal?

The main risk is a discrimination claim under state or local law. Penalties differ by place. PRRAC notes, for example, that Hawai'i caps fines at $2,500 and does not allow attorney's fees. Michigan's law allows injunctive relief, damages and attorney's fees.

Other risks are less visible:

| Risk | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| State or city claim | A complaint to a civil rights agency or a lawsuit |
| Fair housing claim | Refusals that fall harder on a protected class |
| Lost demand | A smaller pool of qualified applicants |

A refusal for a lawful, neutral reason is a different matter. If you apply the same criteria to all applicants and an applicant fails them, the voucher is not the reason. Keep a record of each decision.

Before you decide to take part, check what the program pays in your area. Our [Section 8 rent limits guide](/guides/section-8-rent-limits) lists HUD's FY2027 Fair Market Rents for the 50 largest metros.
