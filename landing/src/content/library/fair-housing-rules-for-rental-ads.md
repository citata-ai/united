## What does federal law say about rental ad wording?

Federal law bans any rental ad that "indicates any preference, limitation, or discrimination" based on a protected class. That is the text of 42 U.S.C. 3604(c). The 7 federal classes are race, color, religion, sex, handicap (disability), familial status and national origin.

HUD's rule at 24 CFR 100.75 explains what counts. Discriminatory ads include:

1. **Words, phrases, photographs, illustrations, symbols or forms** that convey a unit is or is not available to a group.
2. **Statements to agents or staff** that show a preference for or against a group.
3. **Choice of media or location** that keeps parts of the market from hearing about the unit.
4. **Different ad terms** based on a protected class.

The rule covers more than listings. It names applications, flyers, signs, banners and "any documents used" in renting.

## Does the ad rule apply to small landlords?

Yes. The ad rule applies to almost every landlord, even ones exempt from other parts of the Act. Section 3603(b) exempts some owners who rent up to 3 single-family houses without an agent, and some owner-occupied buildings with up to 4 units. But the exemption says it covers section 3604 "other than subsection (c)." Subsection (c) is the advertising ban.

So a small owner who posts on a listing site must follow the ad rule like a 500-door manager.

## What changed with HUD's ad guidance?

HUD withdrew its April 29, 2024 guidance on ads through digital platforms. The withdrawal took effect September 17, 2025, and HUD published the notice in the Federal Register on April 6, 2026. The notice says withdrawn documents "should not be relied upon as authoritative."

The statute and the rule did not change. The same notice says HUD will still enforce actions that do not comply with the Act's text. Renters can still sue within 2 years. So read the law itself, and keep your ads plain.

## Can an ad say "no kids" or "adults only"?

No, not for normal rental housing. Familial status is a protected class. "No kids," "adults only," "perfect for a single person" and "not suitable for children" all convey a limit on families.

There is one exception: housing for older persons under 42 U.S.C. 3607(b). It covers housing for people 62 or older. It also covers 55-and-older housing where at least 80% of occupied units have at least one person aged 55 or more. That housing must publish its policies and verify ages. If your building does not meet that test, do not use age words.

What to do: describe the space. "2 bedrooms, 850 square feet, second floor" gives renters the facts without a limit on who may live there.

## Can I say "near churches" or "walk to school"?

A location fact is different from a preference for a group. "2 blocks to Lincoln Elementary" names a place. "Great for families with kids" names a kind of renter. The first describes the property. The second conveys a preference based on familial status.

The same test works for religion. "Across from St. Mark's" is a landmark. "Christian neighborhood" or "ideal for a church family" states a preference. If you are not sure, name the street or the transit line instead.

## Word swap table for rental ads

Use this table when you review a draft. The right column describes the unit, not the renter.

| Risky phrase | Why it is a risk | Plain swap |
|---|---|---|
| "No kids" / "adults only" | Familial status | "1 bedroom, 600 sq ft" |
| "Perfect for a young professional" | Conveys a preference about who lives there | "10-minute walk to the Blue Line" |
| "Ideal for a couple" | Familial status | "Fits a queen bed and a desk" |
| "Christian home" | Religion | Leave it out |
| "No wheelchairs" / "must climb stairs" | Disability | "Third-floor walk-up, no elevator" |
| "English speakers only" | National origin | Leave it out |
| "Exclusive neighborhood" | Can suggest exclusion | "Quiet street, off-street parking" |
| "No Section 8" (where source of income is protected) | State or local class | Leave it out |

## Are photos of people in a rental ad a risk?

Yes, photos can break the rule. 24 CFR 100.75 lists "photographs, illustrations, symbols" next to words. A campaign that shows only one race, or only young adults, can convey who is welcome.

Two safe options:

- **Show the unit only.** Rooms, kitchen, yard, building front.
- **If you show people, show a broad mix** across all your ads, not only in one.

## How do I describe accessibility features?

Describe the feature, not the renter. "Step-free entry," "grab bars in the bathroom," "elevator to all floors" and "ground-floor unit" are facts about the property. They help renters who need them, and they exclude no one.

Do not write "no wheelchairs," "must be able to climb stairs" or "not for disabled persons." If the unit has stairs, say so as a fact: "third-floor walk-up."

## Do state and local laws add protected classes?

Yes, and your ads must follow them too. Two examples:

- **Oregon:** ORS 659A.421 adds sexual orientation, gender identity, marital status and source of income to the federal list. It also bans ads that show a preference by any of those classes.
- **Colorado:** the Colorado Attorney General says landlords must accept lawful sources of income, including housing vouchers, with limited exceptions. In 2026 the office settled with a manager that told renters it did not accept vouchers.

Check your state and city before you post. A "No Section 8" line can break state law even where federal law allows it.

## Checklist before you post a rental ad

- Describe the unit: beds, baths, size, floor, rent, deposit, pets policy.
- Name places only as landmarks, not as signs of who lives nearby.
- Remove words about age, family, religion, origin, sex or disability.
- Check state and local classes, such as source of income.
- Use the same ad text on every site and in every language you use.
- Keep a copy of each ad with the date you posted it.
