## Which numbers belong on the scorecard?

A weekly scorecard needs numbers that can change a management decision. Start with company cash, owner changes, leasing work and unresolved service issues. Do not fill the page with totals that nobody will act on.

Buildium's accounting guide recommends choosing measures that match business goals and using consistent reports. Use that principle without copying every property metric into your company review. A building's operating result and the management company's cash position are different subjects.

Track activity and outcomes separately. New signed agreements show a different stage from homes that have started billing. A large sales total can hide a slow onboarding queue.

Use counts with their underlying records available. If a report shows overdue owner replies, staff should be able to open the unanswered requests. A summary without a source list can turn the meeting into an argument about data.

Avoid replacing meaningful definitions with color alone. The team needs to know why a number is outside its target and which work contributes to it. Keep prior results visible so staff can distinguish a trend from a reporting error.

Exclude sensitive resident details from the leadership sheet. Link to controlled records for case-level work rather than copying private information into a broad report.

## Who reports each number?

Each metric needs a person who owns its definition, source and follow-up. The person can collect data from others, but should be able to explain changes. Give every metric a backup.

## What targets should I set?

Set targets after checking the baseline, data quality and work capacity. Choose a result the team can influence and explain what will change when it misses. A target with no response plan adds little value.

For a new metric, collect comparable results before attaching rewards or penalties. Check whether the definition produces the cases you expect. Otherwise staff may learn to improve the report instead of the work.

Use a service promise as a target only when its measurement matches the promise. For example, distinguish a useful owner reply from an automatic receipt. Keep urgent cases out of a routine service-hours average.

For company cash, ask the accountant to confirm what the balance excludes. IRS Publication 583 discusses business records and accounting methods. Use that source for the record system, while keeping management targets as your own choices.

Set separate warning and action conditions when needed. A rising queue may call for a staffing review before it becomes a missed promise. Record the rule so the response does not depend on the loudest voice.

Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) when fee income is part of a planning discussion. Do not treat an estimated fee as cash already received.

## How do I run the weekly meeting?

Run the meeting around exceptions and decisions rather than reading every line aloud. Ask reporters to update the sheet before the review. Start by checking unresolved actions from the last meeting.

Let the metric owner explain a missed target briefly. Ask what changed, what evidence supports the cause, and what action the team should take. Move detailed case work to a smaller follow-up group.

Record the action with a named person and due date. Avoid actions such as improve leasing that nobody can close. A useful action describes the specific blocked work and the evidence needed.

Keep reporting errors visible. If the source is wrong, correct the data and mark the affected periods. Do not quietly change history or count the correction as an operating gain.

Use a parking list for topics outside the scorecard. Sales ideas and long policy debates may matter, but they should not consume the service review. Give each parked topic its own route.

End by checking that every decision has an owner. Staff should leave knowing which work changed, which target still applies and where to record completion.

## When should I change the scorecard?

Change a metric when it no longer supports a useful decision or its definition has failed. Keep a brief record of the change and when it takes effect. Preserve old definitions with old results.

Review whether staff can reproduce the numbers without the usual reporter. That test reveals hidden spreadsheets and manual steps. Repair those gaps before adding more measures.

If a metric stays healthy while complaints rise, read the underlying cases. You may be measuring the wrong stage or excluding difficult requests. Ask what the report fails to show.
