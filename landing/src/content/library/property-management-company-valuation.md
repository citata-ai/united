## What SDE and EBITDA multiples apply to a property management company?

A property management company usually sells for a multiple of its earnings. The size of the firm decides which earnings figure the buyer uses.

- **SDE (seller's discretionary earnings):** profit before the owner's pay and perks. Buyers use SDE for small firms where the owner does much of the work.
- **EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization):** profit after a market salary for every role. Buyers use EBITDA for firms with a management team.

The published ranges do not fully agree. Here is what three sources say:

| Source | Owner-run firm | Mature firm | Other basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| CT Acquisitions (2026) | 2.5x–3x SDE | 4x–8x EBITDA | 0.75x–1.5x management-fee revenue |
| Second Nature (2024) | 1.5x–2x SDE (weaker), 2.5x–3x SDE (strong) | 6x EBITDA (growth example) | Revenue multiples are less common |
| PMAssist (2025) | — | "3–6x EBITDA" heard often | "1 to 1.5 times revenue" heard often |

Read the PMAssist row with care. Todd Ortscheid, who wrote that post, calls those figures conventional wisdom, not data. CT Acquisitions is an M&A adviser, and Second Nature sells to property managers. Each one has a reason to publish these numbers. None of the three shares a full deal dataset.

## What price per door do buyers pay?

Buyers pay a price per door that comes out of the earnings multiple. They do not start from the per-door number. CT Acquisitions (2026) says buyers "cross-check" a per-door value of about $200 to $500 for residential books. Higher-fee, higher-retention or HOA books reach $500 to $2,000 per door.

PMAssist (2025) says the industry once used "about $1,000 per door" as a rule. The same post says that rule "doesn't really matter anymore." It also says a roll-up once paid more than $8,000 per door in one deal.

The spread is wide because two books of the same size can earn very different profit. A door at $1,200 rent with a 7% fee earns less than a door at $3,000 rent with the same fee. Price per door hides that.

## A worked valuation for a 300-door firm

The numbers below are an example, not data. Change them to your own figures.

Assume a 300-door residential firm with these results for the last 12 months:

- Management-fee revenue: $300,000
- Total revenue (fees plus leasing, renewals and other income): $420,000
- SDE: $150,000

Now run the three checks from CT Acquisitions:

| Method | Range | Value |
|---|---|---|
| SDE multiple | 2.5x–3x × $150,000 | $375,000–$450,000 |
| Management-fee revenue multiple | 0.75x–1.5x × $300,000 | $225,000–$450,000 |
| Per-door cross-check | $200–$500 × 300 doors | $60,000–$150,000 |

The SDE and revenue checks overlap near $375,000 to $450,000. The per-door check comes out far lower. In this example, a buyer who quotes "$400 a door" offers $120,000 for a firm that the earnings math puts near $400,000. That gap is the reason to run all three checks and show them to the buyer.

Two steps make the SDE figure honest:

1. **Add back only true owner costs.** Second Nature quotes Jock McNeill of PURE Property Management: buyers remove "seller benefits" such as vehicle leases and personal expenses.
2. **Keep one-time income out.** A large one-time fee inflates SDE, and the buyer's diligence will find it.

## How does door count change the multiple?

Door count raises the multiple once a firm is large enough to support its own local team. PMAssist (2025) summarizes an M&A report by Peter Lohmann built from 89 closed deals over five years. The report found a tipping point near 350 doors. Above it, the expected price was about 20% higher.

Ortscheid gives a reason: a buyer who enters a new market needs enough revenue to pay a senior local manager. At 200 doors that is hard. At 350 it works.

The same report found two other patterns:

- **Margin:** firms with profit margins above 20% got a higher profit multiple.
- **Buyer type:** smaller local buyers paid higher multiples on average than the big roll-ups.

The report has self-selection bias. Deal makers chose to submit their own deals. Treat the 350-door line as a signal, not a law.

## Which factors raise or lower value?

Value goes up when the doors are likely to stay after the sale. Each factor below changes that likelihood.

| Factor | Raises value | Lowers value |
|---|---|---|
| Churn | Low, steady door losses | High churn or a shrinking book |
| Owner concentration | No owner above 10% of doors (CT) | One owner above 20% often means an earnout (CT) |
| Owner role | A team runs the work | The founder holds every relationship |
| Fees | Clear fee table, ancillary revenue | Under-priced fees, no add-on revenue |
| Agreements | Long terms, auto-renewal | Short notice periods |
| Records | Clean accrual books, reconciled trust accounts | Commingled funds, missing ledgers |
| Revenue per door | High | Low, often from low rents and high labor cost (Second Nature) |

CT Acquisitions also lists the deal killers it sees in diligence. The list includes high churn, owner concentration, founder dependency, thin fees, weak software, short-notice agreements, trust accounting problems and sloppy financials.

## How to raise your number before you sell

You can improve most value drivers in 12 to 24 months, according to CT Acquisitions. Start with the ones a buyer can measure.

- [ ] Track door count, doors gained and doors lost every month.
- [ ] Split lost doors into "owner sold" and "owner switched managers."
- [ ] Publish one fee table and charge it the same way to every owner.
- [ ] Move owner relationships from you to a named manager.
- [ ] Reconcile each trust account every month and keep the records.
- [ ] Prepare accrual financials with your own pay shown at a market rate.

If your fees are below the local market, the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) shows what a change in fee does to revenue per door.
