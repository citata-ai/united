## When do leasing fees peak?

Leasing fees peak when your own completed leases produce earned and collected fees. The busiest showing month may not be the strongest cash month. Trace the billing and payment dates before calling a period your peak.

Buildium publishes leasing-season marketing guidance, but that does not establish a universal cash calendar for every manager. Use the local pattern as a question to test. Compare your own property types and service areas.

Pull completed leases, invoices and receipts into a consistent view. Separate new placements, renewals and one-time project income. A single large onboarding event can make a month look seasonal when it is not.

Keep a note of unusual events. A portfolio transfer, fee change or staffing gap can distort the pattern. Do not forecast the same event again unless you have a reason to expect it.

Review cash timing with the staff who prepare invoices. A delayed approval or manual billing step may explain the gap better than the rental market. Fixing the process may improve cash without changing prices.

Ask sales staff when signed work will actually begin billing. A promising pipeline should appear as an assumption, not as cash already available for payroll.

## Which costs continue in slower months?

Company costs can continue even when new leasing income falls. List payroll, insurance, software, debt payments and other commitments by payment date. Then identify which costs can change and which require notice or a contract decision.

Separate company costs from owner property bills. A repair paid from an owner ledger should not become a company expense merely because staff processed it. Confirm the ledger treatment with the accountant.

Use this proposed forecast structure:

| Cash line | Forecast basis | Check before relying on it |
|---|---|---|
| Opening company cash | Reconciled operating balance | Excludes client funds |
| Recurring fee receipts | Active agreements and expected collections | Vacancy and owner-exit assumptions |
| Leasing receipts | Expected completion and payment dates | Probability and billing conditions |
| Staff payments | Payroll schedule and planned coverage | Leave, extra work and hiring costs |
| Fixed bills | Signed commitments | Actual due dates |
| Other outflows | Approved plans and known liabilities | Whether payment can move |
| Closing company cash | Opening cash plus receipts less outflows | Compared with your reserve decision |

SBA's finance guidance supports tracking business finances and planning cash needs. The table is a proposed planning format, not a required accounting report.

Add annual payments to the actual period when cash leaves. Spreading a cost in a budget can be useful, but it does not change the bank payment date.

## How large should the company reserve be?

A reserve should cover the cash shortfall your company could face under stated stress assumptions. Start with a forecast, then decide how much uncertainty to add. Do not copy a month-count rule without testing it.

Create a base case from expected receipts and known costs. Then reduce uncertain leasing receipts, delay collections and remove income from a large owner. Use assumptions your leadership team can explain.

Find the lowest projected unrestricted cash balance. Ask what actions would be available before that point and how quickly they could work. A planned cost cut may take time or harm service.

Define which cash belongs to the company before naming a reserve. California DRE's trust-fund guidance requires records that identify funds held for beneficiaries and support reconciliation. That is a California regulatory example, not a full national trust-account guide.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

Keep client-held funds outside the company's available-cash calculation. Ask the accountant and broker to approve the separation. A large bank balance can hide money that does not belong to the management business.

Write the reserve policy in plain terms. State when leadership can use the reserve, who approves use and how the company rebuilds it.

## How do I forecast cash flow?

Forecast receipts and payments by the dates you expect them to hit the company account. Use a near-term view for immediate decisions and a broader view for seasonal planning. Keep both based on the same records.

Update actual receipts and payments before changing assumptions. Compare the last forecast with the outcome. Ask which difference came from timing and which came from a wrong estimate.

Give uncertain receipts a clear status. Signed work, probable placements and early sales leads should not receive the same treatment. Keep a note explaining why each forecast receipt belongs in the plan.

Assign someone to review the cash view who did not build every formula. Ask them to trace a receipt and a bill back to the source. This checks the model without turning it into a complex software project.

Use the [fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) for proposed fee changes, then apply the actual expected start and collection dates. A price increase does not solve a cash gap until it becomes earned and collected income.

Keep financing separate from operating results. If a credit line forms part of the plan, record its available terms and repayment effect. Do not present borrowing as improved trading performance.

## What should trigger action?

Action should begin when the forecast shows a future cash gap, rather than after a failed payment. Agree on the warning conditions and who reviews them. Link each condition to a practical response.

Possible responses include delaying optional spending, resolving billing delays and changing the pace of hiring. Check the service effect before cutting coverage or account review. Protect controls that keep client money and records safe.

Explain seasonal plans to team leads early enough for them to adjust work. Avoid promising new staff or tools without checking the payment dates. Keep owner service promises aligned with capacity.

Record what the slow period taught you. Update fee assumptions, workload estimates and the reserve decision before the next cycle. Use the [guide library](/guides) for related planning work, then build the forecast from your actual company bank records.
