## What is a self-help eviction?

A self-help eviction is any step a landlord takes to force a tenant out without a court order. The landlord acts alone, outside the court process. Most state landlord-tenant laws ban it, and the penalties are often larger than the unpaid rent.

Common self-help acts:

- **Lock change.** The tenant comes home and the key no longer works.
- **Utility shutoff.** Water, power, gas or heat stops, even if the account is in the landlord's name.
- **Removed doors or windows.** The unit becomes unlivable on purpose.
- **Removed belongings.** The tenant's things go to the curb or to storage.
- **Pressure tactics.** Threats, force, or a pattern of conduct that makes the home unbearable.

The rules differ by state. The table below shows 3 states with clear statutes.

| State | What the law bans | Tenant's remedy | Statute |
|---|---|---|---|
| Florida | Utility interruption, lock changes or bootlocks that block access, removing doors, locks, roof, walls or windows (except for repair), removing the tenant's belongings except after surrender, abandonment or a lawful eviction | Actual and consequential damages or 3 months' rent, whichever is greater, plus costs and attorney's fees | Fla. Stat. 83.67 |
| Texas | Utility interruption (except repairs, construction or emergency); exclusion from the unit except by judicial process, with a narrow, strict lock-change rule for unpaid rent | Possession or lease end, plus 1 month's rent plus $1,000, actual damages, costs and attorney's fees | Tex. Prop. Code 92.008, 92.0081 |
| New York | Force or threats, cutting essential services, removing possessions, removing the entrance door, disabling or changing the lock without a key | Class A misdemeanor; civil penalty of $1,000 to $10,000 per violation, plus up to $100 per day if the owner does not restore the occupant | RPAPL 768 |

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## Can I change the locks on a tenant who does not pay?

In most states, a lock change on a tenant who does not pay rent is illegal. Florida Statute 83.67 says a landlord may not prevent reasonable access by changing the locks or using a bootlock. New York RPAPL 768 lists "changing the lock without giving the occupant a key" as unlawful eviction. The New York rule protects anyone who has lived in the unit for 30 days or more, or who signed a lease.

Texas is the main exception, and it is narrow. Texas Property Code 92.0081 allows a lock change for unpaid rent only when all of these are true:

1. The lease states the landlord's right to change the locks for late rent.
2. The tenant owes all or part of the rent.
3. The landlord gave written notice: mailed at least 5 calendar days before, or hand-delivered or posted inside the main entry door at least 3 calendar days before.
4. The notice states the earliest lock-change date, the amount due, where to pay, and, in bold or underlined print, the tenant's right to a new key at any hour.

Even then, the landlord must give the tenant the new key at any hour, whether or not the tenant pays. The landlord may not change the locks while the tenant is inside, or more than once in a rent period. A violation costs 1 month's rent plus $1,000, plus actual damages and attorney's fees. So the Texas rule is a pressure notice, not an eviction.

## Can I shut off utilities?

A landlord may not shut off utilities to push a tenant out. Florida Statute 83.67 bans causing, "directly or indirectly," the interruption of water, heat, light, electricity, gas, elevator, garbage collection or refrigeration. That ban applies even when the landlord does not control or pay for the utility.

Texas Property Code 92.008 bans a landlord from interrupting utilities the tenant pays for directly, and utilities the landlord furnishes, unless the cause is bona fide repairs, construction or an emergency. The tenant may recover actual damages, 1 month's rent plus $1,000, attorney's fees and court costs. Texas allows one narrow case: a landlord who submeters electricity may cut power for an unpaid electric bill, after a long list of notice steps in the same section.

New York RPAPL 768 treats "interruption or discontinuance of essential services" as part of an unlawful course of conduct.

What to do instead: keep every utility in your name paid while the case runs. Bill the tenant for the cost under the lease, or add it to your court claim.

## Can I remove the tenant's belongings?

You may not remove a current tenant's belongings to force them out. Florida Statute 83.67 allows removal only after the tenant surrenders or abandons the unit, after a lawful eviction, or after the last tenant dies. Texas Property Code 92.0081 allows exclusion to remove contents only from premises the tenant abandoned. New York RPAPL 768 names "removal of the occupant's possessions" as unlawful eviction.

Abandonment has its own rules in each state. Before you touch anything, confirm the unit meets your state's test for abandonment and follow its notice and storage steps.

## What penalties apply?

Self-help eviction penalties stack up fast. Here is a worked example for one Florida lockout.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Monthly rent | $1,800 (example) |
| Statutory minimum damages: 3 months' rent | $5,400 |
| Tenant's attorney's fees and court costs | Added on top, set by the court |
| Unpaid rent you were owed | Still owed, but now in a weaker case |

Florida also counts each later, separate violation as a new claim. A second lockout a week later can mean a second award.

In New York, the same lockout is a class A misdemeanor. The civil penalty is $1,000 to $10,000 per violation. If the occupant asks to get back in and the owner does not restore access, the owner can owe up to $100 more per day, for up to 6 months.

In Texas, a lockout outside the 92.0081 rules costs 1 month's rent plus $1,000. If the landlord also refuses the new key, the tenant can recover 1 more month's rent.

## What is the legal path instead?

The legal path to remove a tenant has 4 steps. It takes longer than a lock change, but it ends with a clean result.

1. **Serve the right notice.** Use your state's form and time period, for example a pay-or-quit notice for unpaid rent.
2. **File the eviction case.** File in the court your state uses for possession cases.
3. **Win the judgment.** Bring the lease, the ledger and proof of notice.
4. **Let the officer act.** The sheriff, constable or marshal carries out the court order. You change the locks only after the officer returns possession.

Watch for: a tenant who stops paying often asks for repairs or files a complaint at the same time. Keep up repairs during the case. A judge looks at your conduct, not only the tenant's.

If you manage many units, put the no-self-help rule in writing for every staff member and vendor. One locksmith call from a frustrated leasing agent can cost more than a year of the unit's profit.
