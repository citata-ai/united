## Which revenue goes into lifetime value?

Lifetime value counts all revenue one door brings your company, not the rent it collects. Rent and vendor bills pass through to the owner. Your LTV only uses what you keep.

| Revenue line | In LTV? |
|---|---|
| Monthly management fee | Yes |
| Leasing or tenant placement fee | Yes, spread over the years |
| Lease renewal fee | Yes |
| Maintenance coordination fee or markup you keep | Yes |
| Other fees you keep (inspection, late fee share) | Yes |
| Rent paid to the owner | No |
| Vendor invoices paid for the owner | No |

Pull 12 months of income from your general ledger. Divide by the average doors you managed in those 12 months. That is annual revenue per door.

Rentvine's example uses $2,200 of revenue per unit per year (Rentvine, 2025). Property Manager Websites uses $220 per unit per month, or $2,640 a year (PMW, 2025). Both are examples, not benchmarks. Use your own number.

### Revenue LTV and profit LTV

Revenue LTV tells you what a door brings in. Profit LTV tells you what it leaves after the cost to serve it. A 2022 Fourandhalf podcast with Ray Hespen of Property Meld used a 40% margin on $2,200 of yearly revenue as its example. We use the same numbers below.

## How do I calculate the lifetime value of a door?

The lifetime value of a door is annual revenue per door divided by annual unit churn. Peter Lohmann gives this formula in his January 2026 newsletter (issue #173):

**Door LTV = annual revenue per door ÷ annual churn rate**

This works because average tenure is 1 divided by churn (Lohmann, issue #74). At 20% churn, a door stays 5 years on average.

Rentvine uses a longer form at the client level (Rentvine, 2025):

**Client LTV = annual revenue per unit × tenure in years × units per client**

Its example: $2,200 × 4 years × 1.2 units = $10,560.

### Worked example

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| Revenue per door per year | $2,200 |
| Annual unit churn | 20% |
| Average tenure | 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5 years |
| Revenue LTV | $2,200 ÷ 0.20 = $11,000 |
| Profit margin | 40% |
| Profit LTV | $11,000 × 0.40 = $4,400 |

## How does churn change lifetime value?

Churn changes lifetime value more than any other input, because you divide by it. Keep revenue at $2,200 a year and 40% margin, and move only churn:

| Annual churn | Average tenure | Revenue LTV | Profit LTV |
|---|---|---|---|
| 25% | 4.0 years | $8,800 | $3,520 |
| 20% | 5.0 years | $11,000 | $4,400 |
| 15% | 6.7 years | $14,667 | $5,867 |
| 10% | 10.0 years | $22,000 | $8,800 |

Going from 20% to 15% churn adds $3,667 of revenue to every door. On 500 doors, that is about $1.8 million of lifetime revenue.

The 2022 Fourandhalf podcast with Hespen made the same point. Your acquisition cost is the same whether an owner stays 1 year or 5. For a 500-door company, the podcast says, the gap between 3-year and 5-year customers can reach a million dollars in profit (Fourandhalf, 2022).

Watch for: units per client. If you manage many small multifamily buildings, one lost client takes several doors. Lohmann averages 2.4 units per client (issue #74). Rentvine's example uses 1.2.

## How much can I spend to win a door?

You can spend up to a fixed share of LTV to win a door, and the share depends on the margin you need. Property Manager Websites gives one rule: aim for a 4:1 ratio of lifetime revenue to acquisition cost (PMW, 2025).

| Method | Math | Max spend per door |
|---|---|---|
| 4:1 revenue rule (PMW) | $11,000 ÷ 4 | $2,750 |
| Half of profit LTV | $4,400 ÷ 2 | $2,200 |
| Payback in 12 months of profit | $2,200 × 0.40 | $880 |

The three methods give very different caps. Pick one and use it every quarter. The 12-month payback cap is the safest when cash is tight. The 4:1 rule fits a company with low churn and cash to wait.

To turn the cap into a lead budget, divide by your close rate. PMW's example: at a 20% close rate you need 5 leads per client. A $3,000 cap per client means no more than $600 per lead (PMW, 2025).

## How does lifetime value change a buy price?

Lifetime value sets the most you can pay for a door you buy. Compare the asking price per door with profit LTV, not with revenue LTV.

Lohmann's January 2026 newsletter listed companies for sale. Two give a door count:

| Listing (issue #173) | Asking price | Doors | Price per door |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sacramento and Bay Area, CA | $225,000 | 36 | $6,250 |
| Palm Beach County, FL | $1,200,000 | 278 | $4,317 |

These are asking prices, not sale prices. Now test them against the worked example. At $4,400 of profit LTV per door, a $4,317 price uses up 98% of the lifetime profit. A $6,250 price is above it. That deal only works if those doors bring more revenue, churn less, or cost less to serve than our example.

Run three checks before you make an offer:

1. **Ask** for the seller's revenue per door and 3 years of churn. Calculate their LTV, not yours.
2. **Plan** for higher churn in year 1 after the sale. Owners get a new manager they did not choose. This is a hypothesis; we found no published number.
3. **Subtract** transition costs: software migration, trust account transfer, staff time.

Lohmann adds one more warning. A purchase does not change your monthly adds or your churn rate. If the deal takes you above your growth ceiling, you shrink back toward it (issue #74).
