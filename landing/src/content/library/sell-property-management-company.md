A buyer of a property management company buys one thing: management agreements that keep paying. Everything else is proof that they will keep paying after you leave. Your books, your trust account, your team and your processes are that proof.

This guide is a prep plan. It does not give a price. Valuation numbers you see online come from deals with very different terms, and we could not verify a current, public benchmark. Get a valuation from a broker or a CPA who knows your market.

## How early should I prepare?

Start 1 to 3 years before you want to close. Mike Kalis, CEO of Marketplace Homes, gives that range on the Buildium blog. He compares it to renovating a house before a sale.

Use the time in three stages.

| Stage | When | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Clean up | 24 to 36 months out | Separate personal costs from company costs. Write off bad debt. Reconcile every trust account. Move to electronic payments. |
| Build the team | 12 to 24 months out | Hire or promote people for leasing, maintenance, accounting and owner care. Write each process down. Step back from daily work. |
| Package | 6 to 12 months out | Build the data room. Get a valuation. Pick a broker or a short list of buyers. Line up your attorney and CPA. |

Kalis gives a blunt test. If you personally do all the business development, marketing, bookkeeping and evictions, "you are the business". When you leave, there is nothing left to buy.

## Which numbers will buyers ask for?

Buyers ask for numbers that show how stable your revenue is. Have these ready in one shared folder.

**Financial**

- [ ] Profit and loss statements for the last 3 years and this year to date.
- [ ] A list of owner add-backs: your car, phone and personal costs paid by the company.
- [ ] Revenue by type: management fees, leasing fees, renewal fees, maintenance markup, other.
- [ ] Bad debt written off, not carried as an asset.
- [ ] Cash-basis and accrual-basis views, and an explanation of any big gap.

**Portfolio**

- [ ] Door count by month for 3 years.
- [ ] Doors gained and lost each year, with reasons for losses.
- [ ] A list of owners with door counts, fee rates and contract start dates.
- [ ] Share of revenue from your largest 5 and 10 owners.
- [ ] Copies of management agreements, with their termination and assignment terms.

**Trust and compliance**

- [ ] Monthly three-way reconciliations for at least the last 12 months.
- [ ] Your state license and any regulator audits or complaints.
- [ ] Insurance policies and claims history.

**Operations**

- [ ] Staff list with roles, pay and tenure.
- [ ] Written processes for leasing, maintenance, move-out and owner reporting.
- [ ] Software list and contracts, including end dates.

Kalis says buyers will ask "tons of questions" about your financials. "I don't know" is not an acceptable answer. Go through the P&L line by line with your CPA first.

The trust account deserves extra care. The California DRE trust fund guide (RE 13) says the money in the account must always equal what you owe all owners and tenants. A buyer who finds a shortage will either walk away or cut the price.

## Broker or direct sale?

Both routes work. The right one depends on who your likely buyer is.

| Question | Business broker | Direct sale |
|---|---|---|
| Do you already know a buyer? | No, or you want more than one bid | Yes, a local firm or a competitor |
| Do you need confidentiality? | The broker screens buyers before they see your name | You manage it yourself with an NDA |
| Who prepares the sale package? | The broker | You, your CPA and your attorney |
| Cost | A success fee to the broker, set in the engagement letter | No broker fee; more of your own time |
| Price pressure | More bidders can raise price and improve terms | One buyer sets the pace |

Always hire your own attorney and CPA, whatever route you pick. The buyer's advisors work for the buyer.

## How do earnouts work?

An earnout is part of the price paid after closing, tied to results. In a property management sale, the measure is often how many doors or how much revenue stays for a set period.

A worked example, with made-up numbers:

| Term | Example |
|---|---|
| Agreed headline price | $600,000 |
| Paid at closing | $360,000 (60%) |
| Earnout | $240,000 (40%), paid at 12 months |
| Measure | Share of acquired management revenue still under contract at month 12 |
| Result | 85% of revenue retained |
| Earnout paid | $240,000 × 85% = $204,000 |
| Total received | $564,000 |

Negotiate these points in writing:

1. **The measure.** Doors, revenue or gross profit. Revenue is easier to check.
2. **The period.** Shorter periods mean less risk for you.
3. **Who controls the result.** After closing, the buyer runs the company. Ask for protections if the buyer raises fees or changes service in a way that drives owners away.
4. **Reporting.** Monthly reports and the right to check the numbers.
5. **Disputes.** A named accountant or a clear dispute process.

Earnouts and seller notes also change your taxes. The IRS says a sale where you get at least one payment after the year of sale is an installment sale. You report it under the installment method unless you elect out. Ask a tax professional how this applies to your deal.

## How does the IRS treat the sale?

The IRS treats the sale of a business as the sale of each asset, not one asset. Its "Sale of a business" page says the buyer and seller must both use the residual method to allocate the price to each asset. That allocation decides how much is goodwill.

Both sides file Form 8594, the Asset Acquisition Statement, when goodwill or going concern value could attach. Agree on the allocation in the purchase contract, so both returns match. A sale of stock or a partnership interest follows other rules.

This is general information, not tax or legal advice. Ask a tax professional and a local attorney.

## What happens to my staff?

Your staff decide much of the deal's value. A buyer wants the people who know the owners, the tenants and the vendors.

- **Tell key staff at the right time.** Usually after a letter of intent, with the buyer's plan in hand.
- **Explain what changes and what does not.** Kalis suggests telling staff about the better tools and job security a larger owner can bring.
- **Ask the buyer for job offers in writing** for the people you want protected.
- **Consider stay bonuses** for key staff through the earnout period. This protects your earnout too.

## What do I do after closing?

Many buyers ask the seller to stay for a transition period. Introduce the buyer to your largest owners in person or by phone. Send a joint letter to all owners and tenants. Hand over keys, vendor lists and software access on a checklist.
