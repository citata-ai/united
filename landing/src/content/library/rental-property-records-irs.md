## Which receipts and statements does the IRS expect?

The IRS expects records that prove each number on your Schedule E: every dollar of rent, every expense, and the cost basis behind your depreciation. If you cannot show a record, an auditor can deny the deduction.

Group your records by what they prove:

| What it proves | Records to keep |
|----------------|-----------------|
| Rental income | Leases, rent ledger, bank deposits, Form 1099-MISC from your manager |
| Deposits | Deposit received, where it is held, and what you kept and why |
| Operating expenses | Invoices, receipts, canceled checks or card statements |
| Mortgage interest | Form 1098 from the lender |
| Property tax | County tax bills and payment proof |
| Insurance | Policy and payment proof |
| Travel and mileage | Mileage log with date, miles and purpose |
| Purchase and basis | Closing statement, purchase contract, land and building split |
| Improvements | Invoices, contracts, permits, date placed in service |
| Depreciation | Your depreciation worksheets and Form 4562 |

A few IRS rules that affect your files:

- **Security deposits.** IRS Publication 527 says you do not include a deposit in income if you plan to return it. If you keep part of it, include that amount in income that year. Keep the move-out record that shows why.
- **Expenses a tenant pays.** If a tenant pays a bill for you, such as a repair, Publication 527 treats it as rental income. You can then deduct the expense. Keep the receipt.
- **Form 1098.** Publication 527 says you should get a Form 1098 if you paid $600 or more of mortgage interest to one lender.
- **Form 1099-MISC.** If you use a manager, the manager must report the rent it paid over to you on Form 1099-MISC (IRS instructions).
- **Depreciation worksheets.** Publication 527 says you do not attach your own worksheets to the return, but you should keep them in your records.

## How long should I keep records (IRS guidance)?

Keep most rental records for 3 years after you file. Keep property records much longer. The IRS bases the time on the period of limitations: the time you can amend a return, or the IRS can assess more tax.

| Situation (IRS) | Keep records for |
|-----------------|------------------|
| Normal case | 3 years |
| You file a claim for credit or refund after you file | 3 years from filing, or 2 years from paying the tax, whichever is later |
| You leave out income that is more than 25% of the gross income on your return | 6 years |
| You claim a loss from worthless securities or a bad debt deduction | 7 years |
| You do not file a return | Indefinitely |
| You file a fraudulent return | Indefinitely |
| Employment tax records, if you have employees | At least 4 years |
| Records about the property | Until the period of limitations ends for the year you dispose of the property |

The property rule is the one landlords miss. The IRS says you need these records to figure depreciation and the gain or loss when you sell. So the closing statement from a 2012 purchase, sold in 2030, stays in your file until at least 2034 in the normal case.

1031 exchanges add more time. Say you received the property in a nontaxable exchange. The IRS says to keep the records on the old and the new property. Keep them until the period ends for the year you dispose of the new one.

## Should I use a separate bank account?

Yes, use a separate bank account for each rental or for the rental business. The IRS does not require it for an individual landlord. But IRS Publication 583 tells business owners to keep a business account separate from a personal one. It calls that account the base for recording expenses.

Why it helps a landlord:

1. **One place for income.** Every rent payment lands in one account, so the ledger matches the bank.
2. **Clean expenses.** No sorting of hardware store receipts between your kitchen and the rental.
3. **Easy proof.** A statement shows the date and payee of each cost.
4. **Deposits stay apart.** Some states require tenant deposits in a separate account. Check your state's rules.
5. **Easy handoff.** A manager or bookkeeper can take over without digging through personal spending.

If you own more than one rental, use one account per property, or one account with a tag for each property on every transaction.

## What is a simple record system for one rental?

A simple record system for one rental is one bank account, one folder per year, one permanent folder, and a 15-minute monthly habit. You do not need special software to start.

Set up these folders (paper or cloud):

```text
/Rental - 123 Main St
  /Permanent (keep until 3+ years after you sell)
    Closing statement and purchase contract
    Land and building value split
    Improvement invoices, by date
    Depreciation schedule
    Loan documents
  /2026
    /Income     leases, rent ledger, 1099-MISC
    /Expenses   receipts and invoices, by month
    /Statements bank, mortgage (1098), tax bill, insurance
    /Mileage    trip log
    Schedule E and Form 4562 as filed
  /2025
    ...
```

The monthly habit:

- [ ] Download the bank statement for the rental account.
- [ ] Match each deposit to a tenant and month.
- [ ] Save each receipt in the month's folder, named "date-vendor-amount".
- [ ] Tag each expense as repair or improvement.
- [ ] Add trips to the mileage log.
- [ ] Move any improvement invoice to the permanent folder.

At year end, total each category for Schedule E. Then you or your tax preparer can file without a shoebox.

Why the repair or improvement tag matters: IRS Publication 527 tells owners to separate repair and improvement costs and keep accurate records. You need the cost of improvements when you depreciate or sell. Publication 551 adds that your basis includes settlement costs such as title fees, recording fees, surveys and transfer taxes. Those numbers come from the closing statement in your permanent folder.

If you hire a manager, ask for monthly statements and invoices in a format you can save. Compare the year's statements with the Form 1099-MISC total. For more on what to expect from a manager, see [/property-management](/property-management).
