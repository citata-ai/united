## Which fee questions come first?

Fee questions come first: an owner wants the total cost before anything else. The trouble is that most managers answer with one number, "8% of rent", and the owner learns about the leasing fee later. That feels like a trick, even when it is in the contract.

A good answer has three parts:

1. **The full list.** Every fee, in one table, with what it covers.
2. **A worked example.** The total for one year on a home like theirs.
3. **What is not included.** Repairs, legal fees, and anything billed at cost.

Here is a sample fee answer for a $2,000 a month home. The fee levels are an example, not a market rate.

| Fee | Example rate | Year 1 cost at $2,000 rent |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly management | 8% of collected rent | $1,920 (12 x $160) |
| Leasing | 50% of 1 month's rent | $1,000 |
| Lease renewal | $250 flat | $0 in year 1 |
| Maintenance markup | 10% on vendor bills | $100 on $1,000 of repairs |
| Setup | $0 | $0 |
| **Total** | | **$3,020** |

The math: 8% of $2,000 is $160 a month. 12 months is $1,920. Add the $1,000 leasing fee and $100 markup. Total $3,020, or about 12.6% of the $24,000 a year in rent.

Owners can run their own numbers in our [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator). Send them the link before the call.

Other fee questions to expect:

- "Do you charge when the home is empty?"
- "Do you mark up repairs?"
- "What does a lease renewal cost me?"
- "Is there a fee to cancel?"

## How do I explain tenant screening?

Explain tenant screening with a written list of criteria that you apply the same way to every applicant. Owners want to hear that you are careful. They also need to hear that you are fair and legal, because screening mistakes put the owner at risk too.

Cover these points:

| Owner question | What a good answer shows |
|---|---|
| "What do you check?" | Your written criteria: income to rent ratio, rental history, credit, background where local law allows |
| "Who decides?" | One person applies the criteria; the owner does not pick by personal taste |
| "Can I meet the tenant?" | Yes, if your process allows it for every applicant the same way |
| "What if you deny someone?" | You send an adverse action notice when a consumer report played a part |
| "Do you take vouchers?" | Your policy, and the source of income law in your state or city |

The FTC says a landlord must send an adverse action notice when a consumer report played any part in the decision, "even if information in the consumer report wasn't the primary reason" (FTC). The notice names the consumer reporting agency, says the agency did not make the decision, and tells the applicant they may get a free copy of the report within 60 days and dispute errors (FTC). The FTC also says you must securely dispose of reports when you are done.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

Do not describe your tenants by family status, religion, national origin or any other protected class. "Quality tenants" is fine. "Young professionals" is not.

## How do I answer "what if the tenant does not pay"?

Answer the late rent question with a timeline in days, not a promise. Owners fear months of lost rent. A clear process calms that fear better than "we rarely have problems".

A sample answer pattern:

1. **Day 1.** Rent is due. Your software sends a reminder.
2. **Grace period ends.** The late fee in the lease applies. State law may cap late fees and grace periods.
3. **Notice day.** You serve the notice your state requires, such as a pay-or-quit notice.
4. **Owner call.** You call the owner, share the ledger, and agree on the next step.
5. **Filing.** If the tenant does not pay or leave, you start the court process with a local attorney.

Name what changes by state. Notice periods, late fee limits and eviction steps differ in each state, and some cities add rules. Do not quote a national number.

Also say what you do before rent is late: screening, move-in inspection, and how fast you answer repair requests. Owners care about prevention as much as the cure.

## Which other questions do owners ask?

Owners ask about money flow, communication and how to leave. Prepare one short answer and one document for each.

| Question | Short answer to prepare | Document to show |
|---|---|---|
| "When do I get paid?" | The day of the month you send owner draws | Sample owner statement |
| "How do I see what is going on?" | Owner portal, monthly statement, who to call | Portal screenshot |
| "Who handles repairs?" | Your approval limit and vendor process | Maintenance policy |
| "Do you hold my money in a trust account?" | Yes or no, and the state rule | License and trust account details |
| "How many doors does each manager handle?" | Your real ratio | Team page |
| "How do I cancel?" | Notice period and any fee | Management agreement clause |
| "What happens to my tenant if I leave?" | Lease stays in place, deposit moves with it | Offboarding checklist |
| "Are you licensed?" | License type, number and state | Link to the state license lookup |

If you do not know an answer, say so and follow up in writing. That builds more trust than a fast guess.

## Which answers belong on my website too?

Put the answers to the first 4 questions on your website: fees, screening, late rent and cancel terms. These are the questions that stop owners from calling at all.

Two reasons to publish them:

- **Owners read before they call.** A fee table and a cancel clause answer the doubts that make an owner leave the site.
- **AI answers quote public facts.** When an owner asks ChatGPT or Gemini about local managers, the answer can only quote what is public. In our own research, Expertise.com was a source in 93 of 150 AI answers about property managers (our data, 2026-10). If your fees are not on your site, AI answers use a directory or a guess. Our guide on [how owners choose a property manager with AI](/guides/how-owners-choose-a-property-manager-with-ai) shows this in more detail.

Owner traffic is small, so each visit counts. In the Goodjuju fall 2026 study of 35 companies, the average site turned 0.9% of visitors into leads (Goodjuju, 2026).

Checklist for the website:

- [ ] Fee table with every fee and a worked example
- [ ] Written tenant screening criteria
- [ ] Late rent timeline in days, with a note that state law applies
- [ ] Cancel terms quoted from the agreement
- [ ] Owner draw date and a sample statement
- [ ] License number and a link to the state lookup
- [ ] An FAQ section with the 12 questions above

Start with the fee table. It answers the question every owner asks first.
