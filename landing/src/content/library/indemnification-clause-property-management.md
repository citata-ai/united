## Who protects whom in an indemnity clause?

An indemnity clause should identify the party making the promise, the party receiving protection and the claims covered. Start with the business risks before asking counsel to draft language. A broad heading cannot explain the actual duties.

Discuss an owner-controlled building condition, a manager's instruction error and a contractor's damage separately. Ask who should bear each risk and why. Record that intended allocation before reviewing a standard contract.

Terms such as indemnify, defend and hold harmless may create different duties under the governing law. Ask counsel to explain those effects in your agreement. Do not assume the words are interchangeable or harmless boilerplate.

Use this review table:

| Question | Business fact to provide | Professional reviewer |
|---|---|---|
| Which claims belong to the owner? | Ownership and property-control duties | Local attorney |
| Which claims belong to the manager? | Actual service scope and decisions | Local attorney |
| Who pays for a defense? | Proposed notice and defense process | Attorney and insurer |
| Which losses may be insured? | Policy forms and claim scenarios | Insurance broker |
| Who else receives protection? | Entities, staff and contractors involved | Attorney and insurer |

Oregon's Real Estate Agency says management agreements must state owner and manager duties. Its guidance supports a clear scope discussion, but does not supply a universal indemnity clause.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## What does the clause not cover?

The clause covers only what its wording and applicable law allow. Have counsel identify exclusions and duties that cannot be transferred. Avoid telling owners that a signed agreement removes every risk.

Review conduct categories separately. Ordinary mistakes, serious misconduct, intentional acts and criminal conduct raise different questions. Ask counsel which terms the clause uses and whether those terms match local law.

Check the types of claims too. A third-party injury claim is different from an owner demanding repayment of a fee. The clause should make clear whether it addresses outside claims, disputes between the parties, or both.

Ask when the duty begins. Does it depend on an allegation, a finding of fault or a settlement? Who selects counsel and approves a settlement? These are practical decisions with a direct effect on cash and control.

Review notices and cooperation duties. Staff need to know who receives a claim and what records to preserve. A clause that depends on prompt reporting needs a usable reporting route.

Do not copy an exclusion from an unrelated industry without review. Have counsel compare the agreement with your actual management work and property types. Keep a plain explanation for staff beside the approved contract.

## How does indemnity work with errors and omissions insurance?

Indemnity and errors and omissions insurance need separate review because the contract and policy answer different questions. The contract allocates duties between parties. The policy states when the insurer may respond.

HUB's property management insurance page discusses professional liability and the need to clarify whether the owner's policy, the manager's coverage or both respond. Use that as a prompt for a written coverage review. It is not proof that your policy covers a particular claim.

Give the broker the full agreement, not just the indemnity paragraph. Service scope, insurance terms and notice duties can interact. Ask about coverage for contractually assumed duties and exclusions that could leave a gap.

Provide realistic scenarios from your work. Include missed maintenance instructions, an owner who withholds approval and a contractor dispute. Ask the broker to identify the relevant wording for each scenario.

Check who must receive notice and which claim-response steps require insurer approval. Do not let operations staff promise a settlement or select outside counsel without checking the policy process.

Keep the approved policy documents and review notes in a controlled location. A salesperson's summary is useful context, but the issued wording needs to support the decision.

## Should the owner name me as an additional insured?

Additional-insured status may help for specified risks, but only the actual endorsement shows what it provides. Ask your broker what coverage the company needs for its role. Do not treat the label as complete protection.

Confirm the exact legal entity that performs management work. A trading name, parent company and operating company may differ. Ask counsel and the broker to align names across the contract and coverage documents.

Request the relevant endorsement and have the broker review the covered operations. Check exclusions, limits and any conditions that matter to the property. A certificate alone should not end the review.

Discuss how additional-insured protection relates to your own professional liability coverage. The risks may differ. Do not cancel a policy because an owner says their building coverage includes the manager.

Create an evidence process for renewal and owner changes. Staff should know what to request, who checks it and how an unresolved gap reaches management. Avoid keeping expired certificates as though they prove current coverage.

If an owner cannot provide the intended coverage, seek a reviewed alternative before accepting the risk. A rushed exception should not become an undocumented business practice.

## How should I review the clause before signing?

Review the clause with the scope, insurance requirements and claims procedure in the same meeting. Ask the attorney to explain the effect using a plausible property event. Ask the broker to explain the likely coverage question.

Keep a written list of unresolved terms. Assign each to the owner, counsel or insurance adviser. Do not mark the agreement ready merely because the fee schedule is complete.

Teach staff how to report a claim without admitting fault or promising payment. Give them a factual incident form and a named escalation contact. The claims lead can handle the next professional review.

Revisit the terms when you add a new property type or service. Short-term rentals, commercial work and major project coordination may need a fresh assessment.
