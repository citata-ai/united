## What are the steps of an eviction?

An eviction has 5 steps, and every state uses the same basic order. The names of the papers and the deadlines change from state to state.

1. **Serve a written notice.** The notice tells the tenant to pay, fix a lease problem or move out.
2. **File the case.** If the deadline passes, you file a complaint and summons with the local court.
3. **Wait for the answer.** The tenant can answer in writing, or do nothing.
4. **Get a judgment.** The judge decides at a hearing, or enters a default if the tenant did not answer.
5. **Get a writ and a lockout.** The court issues a writ. A sheriff or marshal posts a final notice, then removes the tenant.

The California courts say the whole process "can take 30 to 45 days or more". The clock starts when the tenant gets the court papers.

## What notice starts an eviction?

A written notice starts almost every eviction. For unpaid rent, the notice length is set by state law.

| State | Rent notice | What counts | Law |
|---|---|---|---|
| California | 3 days to pay or quit | Saturdays, Sundays and court holidays do not count | California Courts Self-Help Guide |
| Florida | 3 days | Saturdays, Sundays and legal holidays do not count | Fla. Stat. 83.56(3) |
| Arizona | 5 days | Written notice of nonpayment and intent to end the lease | A.R.S. 33-1368(B) |
| New York | 14 days | Written demand for the rent | RPAPL 711(2) |

Watch for: the California notice can ask for rent only. The California courts say you "can't ask for other money (late fees, utilities, damages)" in a 3-day notice to pay rent or quit. A mistake in the notice can lose the case.

Other notices exist for other reasons. California lists a 3-day notice to fix a lease breach. It lists a 30-day or 60-day notice to end a month-to-month tenancy, and a 90-day notice for Section 8 tenancies. It also lists a 30-day notice for properties covered by the federal CARES Act. Some cities add more rules on top of state law.

In Arizona, the tenant can stop the eviction before you file. A.R.S. 33-1368(B) reinstates the lease if the tenant pays all past-due rent and a reasonable late fee from a written lease.

## How does the landlord file the case?

The landlord files a complaint and a summons at the court for the area where the rental is. A court officer or a process server then delivers the papers to the tenant.

The answer deadline is short in an eviction case:

- **California:** 10 court days if the tenant got the papers in person. Substituted service or posting and mailing gives 10 calendar days plus 10 court days (California Courts Self-Help Guide).
- **Florida:** 5 days after service, under the summary procedure in Fla. Stat. 51.011.
- **Arizona:** the summons names a trial date "not more than six nor less than three days" from the date of the summons (A.R.S. 33-1377(B)).

Florida adds one more rule. Under Fla. Stat. 83.60(2), a tenant who raises a defense other than payment must pay the rent owed into the court registry. A tenant who does not pay it in 5 days waives those defenses.

## What happens at the court hearing?

The judge hears both sides and decides who gets possession. If the tenant did not answer, you ask for a default judgment, and the judge can decide without a trial.

Bring these to the hearing:

- The lease and any addendums.
- A copy of the notice and proof of how you served it.
- A rent ledger that shows each charge and each payment.
- Photos, emails and texts that support your claim.

If the tenant files a motion instead of an answer, get legal help. The California courts say a demurrer "can delay the case for a few weeks". In California, either side can ask for a jury trial, and the jury fee is $150.

If you win, the judge signs a judgment for possession. The judgment can also include past-due rent, damages and court costs.

## What is a writ of possession?

A writ of possession is the court order that lets a sheriff or marshal remove the tenant. States use different names for it and set a different wait before it issues.

| State | Name of the writ | Wait or final notice | Law |
|---|---|---|---|
| California | Writ of execution | Sheriff gives the tenant 5 days to move | California Courts Self-Help Guide |
| Florida | Writ of possession | Sheriff posts 24 hours' notice on the property | Fla. Stat. 83.62(1) |
| Arizona | Writ of restitution | No writ until 5 calendar days after judgment | A.R.S. 12-1178(C) |
| New York | Warrant of eviction | Officer gives at least 14 days' written notice | RPAPL 749 |

## Who does the lockout?

A court officer does the lockout, not the landlord. In California, the sheriff posts a notice to vacate. If the tenant stays past the deadline, the sheriff comes back, removes them and locks them out.

Self-help eviction is illegal. The California courts say you cannot "lock a tenant out of their home". You also cannot "shut off gas, water, or heat to make them move out". Other states have similar rules. Change the locks only after the officer gives you possession.

After the lockout, the tenant may still try to stop or undo the eviction. The California courts list a request for more time, a motion to set aside the judgment and an appeal.

## How long does each step take?

Each step has its own clock. Here is a rough timeline for an uncontested rent case, built from the deadlines above.

| Step | California | Florida | Arizona |
|---|---|---|---|
| Notice | 3 court days | 3 days, no weekends or holidays | 5 days |
| Answer or trial | 10 court days to answer | 5 days to answer | Trial 3 to 6 days after summons |
| Writ | Sheriff gives 5 days | Sheriff posts 24 hours | 5 calendar days after judgment |

Court calendars, service delays and holidays add days to every step. A contested case takes longer. Plan for the lost rent before you file. A property manager who files often can help; see [how a property management company runs these cases](/property-management).

Fair Housing applies to every step. You cannot evict for race, religion, disability, family status or another protected class, and you cannot evict in retaliation for a repair request.
