## Why do property managers add ancillary revenue?

Property managers add ancillary revenue because the management fee alone often does not grow fast enough to cover costs. The National Apartment Association summary of AppFolio's 2025 Property Management Benchmark Report (more than 2,000 respondents) shows how fee managers planned to respond:

| Plan for 2025 (fee managers) | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Offer additional services to owners | 31% | 36% |
| Charge owners for maintenance | 31% | 36% |
| Implement additional fees for residents | 28% | 32% |
| Offer additional services to residents | 17% | 28% |
| Implement additional fees for owners | 25% | 24% |

Note the last row. Fewer fee managers planned new owner fees, while more planned new owner services. Owners accept a charge for a service more easily than a charge with no service attached.

## Which add-on services do owners pay for?

Owners pay for services that save them time or protect the asset. All Property Management lists common charges on top of the monthly fee:

- **Tenant placement:** about 50% of the first month's rent per new tenant, or 50–100% of one month's rent for leasing-only service.
- **Evictions:** about $200–$500 per eviction, plus court costs.
- **Late payment fees:** on average 25–50% of the late fees collected.
- **Set-up fee:** a one-time charge for new clients at some firms.

Other owner-side services that many firms sell:

- **Lease renewal fee:** a flat fee or a percent of rent when a tenant renews.
- **Periodic inspections:** a fee per visit, with photos and a short report.
- **Project management:** a percent of the cost of a large repair or a turn.
- **Annual tax packet or owner report:** a flat fee for extra reports.

Price each one as a service. Write what the owner gets: number of visits, number of photos, turnaround time.

## Which tenant-side fees are allowed?

Tenant-side fees are allowed only within state and local law, and the limits differ a lot. Two examples show the range.

**New York.** Real Property Law § 238-A bans most payments, fees or charges for processing, reviewing or accepting an application, and any other fee before or at the start of a tenancy, unless a statute allows it. A landlord may charge for a background and credit check, but only the actual cost or $20, whichever is less. The landlord must waive it if the applicant gives a check done in the past 30 days. Late fees are capped at $50 or 5% of monthly rent, whichever is less, and only after rent is more than five days late.

**Federal rule.** The FTC's rule on unfair or deceptive fees, 16 CFR part 464, requires the total price up front. Its scope is live-event tickets and short-term lodging, including vacation rentals. It does not name long-term residential leases. If you manage short-term rentals, it applies to them.

Most states sit between these two. Before you add a resident fee, check:

- [ ] State law on application, screening and late fees
- [ ] City rules (some cities add their own caps)
- [ ] Rules for subsidized tenants, such as Housing Choice Voucher leases
- [ ] That the fee is in the lease and the listing before the tenant applies

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## What resident services can earn revenue?

Resident services earn revenue when they solve a real problem at a known moment, most of all at move-in and move-out. Buildium's 2026 post on moving services lists options such as a move-in concierge, utility setup and billing, and turn coordination. It suggests a setup fee or an ongoing fee for utility management.

Keep resident services optional where you can. An optional service with a clear price draws fewer complaints than a required package.

## How much can in-house maintenance add?

In-house maintenance can add the most revenue per door, but it also adds the most cost and risk. You earn the margin between what you bill and what labor, vehicles, tools and insurance cost you. The model below uses made-up inputs. Replace each with your own numbers.

**Example: 200 doors, in-house handyman**

| Line | Input | Annual amount |
|---|---|---|
| Work orders per door per year | 3 | 600 work orders |
| Share done in-house | 50% | 300 jobs |
| Average labor billed per job | $150 | $45,000 revenue |
| Technician pay and taxes | | −$42,000 |
| Vehicle, tools, fuel | | −$9,000 |
| Margin | | −$6,000 |

At 300 jobs, this example loses money. At 450 jobs a year with the same fixed costs, labor revenue rises to $67,500 and the margin turns positive. The lesson: in-house maintenance pays only above a job volume you can measure. Count your real work orders for 12 months before you hire.

Also tell owners how you price in-house work. If your agreement allows a markup on vendor bills, state the percent.

## How do I compare streams per door?

Compare every stream with one formula: annual revenue per door = price × uses per door per year × uptake rate. Here is a worked set of examples with made-up inputs:

| Stream | Price | Uses per door per year | Uptake | Revenue per door per year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lease renewal fee | $150 | 0.6 (renewals) | 100% | $90 |
| Mid-year inspection | $95 | 1 | 40% of owners | $38 |
| Utility setup at move-in | $25 | 0.4 (move-ins) | 60% of residents | $6 |
| Project management (10% of $4,000 turn) | $400 | 0.4 (turns) | 50% | $80 |

On 200 doors, these four streams add $214 per door, or $42,800 a year, before cost. Your numbers will differ. Model each stream before you launch it, and drop the ones that earn less than they cost to run.

## Which ancillary fees hurt reviews?

The fees that hurt reviews are the ones people did not expect. Watch for these patterns:

1. **Fees not in writing.** A charge that is not in the management agreement or lease reads as a trick.
2. **Fees with no service.** An "admin fee" with no named task invites the question "for what?"
3. **Hidden markups.** An owner who finds a 20% markup on a plumber's bill feels misled, even if the agreement allows it.
4. **Required bundles.** A required resident package with services nobody asked for draws complaints.
5. **Fees at the worst moment.** A large fee at move-out, on top of deposit deductions, often turns into a one-star review.

Owners now compare total cost, not the headline rate. Our [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) shows the full yearly cost an owner sees. Run your own fee list through it before you add a new line.
