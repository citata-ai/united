## What is a normal rental application fee?

A normal rental application fee is about $50. In a 2024 Zillow survey, 79% of recent renters said they paid an application fee, and the typical fee for their current rental was $50 (Zillow, 2025-10-28). Zillow itself sells a portable application for $40 that a renter can use at unlimited participating properties for 30 days.

The fee pays for screening. It is not rent and not a deposit. A good test for your fee: could you show an applicant a receipt for each dollar?

Each applicant pays a fee in most setups. Zillow notes that each adult whose name goes on the lease usually pays their own. So a couple who applies together pays twice. Keep that in mind when you set the amount.

## Do some states cap application fees?

Yes, many states cap or limit application fees, and each state writes the rule in a different way. Some set a dollar cap. Some tie the fee to your real cost. Some bar the fee outright. Here are 4 examples.

| State | Rule | Cap | Reuse of a recent report | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New York | Fee may cover only the background and credit check | $20 or actual cost, whichever is less | Fee waived if applicant gives a check from the past 30 days | Real Property Law 238-A |
| Wisconsin | Landlord may charge the actual cost of a credit report | $25 | No charge if applicant gives a report less than 30 days old | Stat. 704.085, ATCP 134.05 |
| California | Fee may cover actual costs plus reasonable time | $30 base, adjusted each year by CPI since 1998 | Not set in 1950.6 | Civil Code 1950.6 |
| Massachusetts | Only first month, last month, a security deposit and lock cost may be collected at or before move-in | No separate application fee in that list | Not applicable | G.L. c. 186, 15B(1)(b) |

Some details that trip landlords up:

- **New York receipts.** The landlord may not collect the fee unless it gives the applicant a copy of the check and the receipt or invoice from the company that ran it.
- **Wisconsin notice.** The landlord must tell the applicant about the charge before ordering the report, and must give the applicant a copy. A non-resident applicant can also be charged the actual cost of a background check, up to $25.
- **California receipt and copy.** The landlord must give an itemized receipt of out-of-pocket costs and time. The landlord must also send the applicant a copy of the credit report within 7 days of getting it.
- **Massachusetts.** The statute lists what a landlord may require "at or prior to the commencement of any tenancy." An application fee is not on the list.

Other states and some cities have their own rules. Check your state statute and your city code before you set a fee.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## What can the application fee pay for?

The application fee can pay for the cost of screening the applicant. California Civil Code 1950.6 is a good model. It allows the fee to cover "the actual out-of-pocket costs" of gathering information, such as credit reports and personal reference checks. It also allows "the reasonable value of time" spent by the landlord or an agent.

New York is stricter. Real Property Law 238-A lets the fee cover only the background check and credit check, at actual cost, with a $20 cap.

Here is a worked fee build for a state with no fixed cap. Use your own invoices.

1. **Credit report cost.** Take the price your screening company charges you per applicant.
2. **Background check cost.** Add it if it is a separate line on the invoice.
3. **Eviction search cost.** Add it if separate.
4. **Your time.** Only if your state allows it. Count real minutes for calls to employers and past landlords, times a fair hourly rate.
5. **Total.** That total is your fee ceiling. Round down, not up.

Things the fee should not pay for:

- Marketing the unit.
- Holding the unit off the market. That is a holding deposit, with its own rules.
- A profit margin.

If you reject an applicant or ask for a co-signer or a higher deposit because of a credit report, federal law adds a step. The FTC says you must send an adverse action notice. It names the credit bureau, says the bureau did not make the decision, and tells the applicant of the right to a free report within 60 days.

## Should you refund the fee if the unit is gone?

Yes, refund the fee if the unit is gone before you screen the applicant. California makes this a rule. Civil Code 1950.6(c) says a landlord may not charge a screening fee "when they know or should have known that no rental unit is available." If the landlord does not run the checks, it must return any part of the fee it did not use.

The fair practice in every state:

- [ ] Take applications only while the unit is open.
- [ ] Pause the listing the same day you approve an applicant.
- [ ] Screen in the order you receive complete applications, and write that policy down.
- [ ] Refund any applicant you did not screen, within 7 days.
- [ ] Keep each receipt in the applicant file.

A fee you refund on time builds trust. A fee you keep for a unit that was never open looks like a scam. The FTC warns renters about fake listings that ask for money before anyone sees the unit.

## How do you set your fee, step by step?

Set your fee from your state law first, then from your real cost. Use these steps:

1. **Read your state statute and city code.** Note any cap, any required receipt and any reuse rule.
2. **List your real screening costs** from your last invoice.
3. **Set the fee** at the lower of the cap and the real cost.
4. **Publish it** in the listing, with what it covers.
5. **Use the same fee for every applicant.** The Fair Housing Act bans different terms because of a protected class.
6. **Give a receipt** for every fee, even where your state does not require one.
