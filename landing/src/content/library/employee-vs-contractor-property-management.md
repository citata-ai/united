## What Tests Does the IRS Use?

The IRS reviews control and independence through behavioral, financial and relationship factors. A contractor label does not replace a review of those facts.

The IRS says no single factor decides the result. It also explains that working remotely does not, by itself, make someone an independent contractor. Read the actual relationship rather than choosing the cheapest payment method.

For your internal review, record who sets the work method and schedule. Note who supplies tools, pays business costs and can make a profit or loss. Describe whether the work continues and how closely it forms part of your normal service.

Give the facts to a tax professional before making a classification decision. Ask employment counsel to review other applicable tests separately. Do not assume that the IRS test settles wage, leave, unemployment or state-law questions.

Keep the review with the role file. If the arrangement changes, review the classification again. A vendor who once completed a separate project may later work under daily company direction. Your written agreement and tax setup should follow the real work, not an outdated description.

## Can Leasing Agents Be Independent Contractors?

A leasing agent needs a review of duties, pay terms and applicable rules before you choose contractor status. A real estate license alone does not settle the question.

The IRS describes special statutory nonemployee treatment for qualifying licensed real estate agents. Its guidance includes output-based pay and a written agreement about federal tax treatment. Ask a tax professional whether the person's actual services and terms qualify. Do not extend that treatment automatically to all leasing or administrative staff.

Separate the licensing question from tax classification. A person may need supervision for licensed work regardless of how the business pays them. Ask the supervising broker to approve the role, authority and reporting route.

Write the actual tasks in plain words. Showing a unit, negotiating lease terms, posting receipts and coordinating repairs are different acts. Review each task instead of assigning a broad property manager title.

Avoid copying a sales-agent contractor agreement for an office support role. The duties may differ in ways that affect the legal analysis. Build a fact sheet from the work you need, then obtain advice for that role and location.

## What Are the Risks of Misclassification?

Misclassification can create tax exposure and other employment-law issues. Get the correct advice before using contractor status as a cost-saving plan.

The IRS states that a business without a reasonable basis for contractor treatment may owe employment taxes. It offers Form SS-8 for a determination when status is unclear. Discuss that route and any correction steps with your adviser.

Use this review table to identify questions. The entries are prompts for advice, not a scoring test.

| Role fact | Question to document |
|---|---|
| Daily instructions | Who controls how work is done? |
| Fixed coverage hours | Who chooses when the person works? |
| Company systems | Are systems required for security, control, or both? |
| Personal business costs | Who carries the financial risk? |
| Other clients | Does the person operate a separate business? |
| Ongoing core duties | How does the work fit the company service? |
| Written terms | Do the terms match daily practice? |

Do not add up answers and call the total a legal result. Different tests can weigh facts differently. Give advisers the full picture, including work messages and supervision practices.

If you find a concern, preserve records and ask for a correction plan. Avoid asking the worker to backdate a contract or change earlier invoices. Fixing the current process needs accurate facts about the past.

## Which Property Management Roles Fit Contractors?

Project-based outside services may be candidates for contractor review, but no role title guarantees that result. Evaluate the actual arrangement each time.

An independent repair business can look different from a person assigned ongoing office duties under daily direction. A photographer hired for a defined job can look different from a full-time marketing role. These examples show facts to examine, not automatic classifications.

Describe the result you need from an outside service. State the approved scope, access limits, insurance expectations and evidence of completion. Avoid giving the provider more resident data than the job needs.

For internal roles, decide the supervision and coverage you need before discussing classification. If you need continuing direct control, tell your advisers plainly. Do not remove necessary supervision from the written description while keeping it in practice.

Review staffing cost on the proper legal basis. A contractor invoice may look simpler than payroll but still carry coordination and oversight costs. ## How Should You Review a New Hire or Vendor?

Use a classification check before the person begins work, then review material changes. Keep tax, employment and license questions visible as separate items.

- Write the tasks, expected results and supervision plan.
- Identify the work location and relevant jurisdictions.
- Record control, cost, tools and relationship facts.
- Ask the tax adviser to review federal tax treatment.
- Ask counsel to check other applicable worker tests.
- Ask the broker to approve licensed duties and supervision.
- Set up payment, access and records to match the reviewed role.

Give managers a clear route for later changes. Adding fixed shifts or new daily duties may require another review. Do not let a busy season quietly turn an outside project into an ongoing staff role.
