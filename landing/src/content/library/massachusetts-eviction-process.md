## Massachusetts eviction rules at a glance

A Massachusetts eviction is a court case called summary process (M.G.L. c. 239). You cannot skip a step. We read each rule below in the statute text on the Massachusetts Legislature site and in court rules on Mass.gov (2026).

| Rule | Limit or deadline | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Notice to quit, unpaid rent, written lease | 14 days | c. 186 §11 |
| Notice to quit, unpaid rent, tenant at will | 14 days; tenant can cure within 10 days if no such notice in the last 12 months | c. 186 §12 |
| State form with any nonpayment notice | Required; court will not accept the case without proof of delivery | c. 186 §31 |
| No-fault end of a tenancy at will | Rent interval or 30 days, whichever is longer | c. 186 §12, §13 |
| Filing fee, Housing Court | $135 ($120 + $15 surcharge) | Mass.gov fee page |
| First court event, District Court | 30 to 60 days after entry | Standing Order 10-20 |
| Trial, District Court | At least 2 weeks after that event | Standing Order 10-20 |
| Appeal window and wait for execution | 10 days after judgment | c. 239 §5(a) |
| Notice before removal | At least 48 hours, by the officer | c. 239 §3 |
| Removal hours | 9 a.m. to 5 p.m., not weekends or legal holidays | c. 239 §3 |
| No-fault stay | Up to 6 months; 12 months for disability or age 60+ | c. 239 §9 |
| Lockout or utility shutoff | $25 to $300 fine or up to 6 months in jail | c. 186 §14 |

## What notice must a Massachusetts landlord give for unpaid rent?

A Massachusetts landlord must give a 14-day written notice to quit for unpaid rent. That rule is in c. 186 §11 for a written lease and in §12 for a tenancy at will.

Under a written lease, the tenant can still stop the case. §11 lets the tenant pay all rent due, with interest and costs, on or before the day the answer is due.

Under a tenancy at will, §12 gives a 10-day cure. A tenant who got no similar notice in the past 12 months can pay all rent due within 10 days after receipt. The notice must quote the exact cure text from §12. If it does not, the tenant can cure until the answer date.

c. 186 §31 adds a form. Every nonpayment notice to quit must come with a state form that lists rental assistance and court rules. It must also show, in a prominent place: "THIS NOTICE TO QUIT IS NOT AN EVICTION." The court may not accept your summons and complaint without proof you delivered the form. District Court Standing Order 10-20 requires an "Affidavit of Compliance with G.L. c. 186 s. 31" with each residential nonpayment filing.

## How long does an eviction take in Massachusetts?

A Massachusetts eviction for unpaid rent takes at least 2 to 3 months in a District Court case where nothing goes wrong. Here is the minimum path, step by step, from the sources above:

1. **Serve the notice to quit:** 14 days.
2. **File the case** after the notice period ends.
3. **First court event:** the District Court clerk schedules a case management conference 30 to 60 days after entry (Standing Order 10-20). The tenant's answer is due 3 business days before it.
4. **Trial:** no sooner than 2 weeks after that conference, if the case does not settle.
5. **Wait for the execution:** no execution issues until 10 days after judgment (c. 239 §5(a)).
6. **Removal:** the officer gives at least 48 hours' written notice (c. 239 §3).

Sum of the minimums: 14 + 30 + 14 + 10 + 2 = 70 days, plus time for filing and service. Delays come from many places. Under c. 239 §15, a court must continue a nonpayment case while the tenant's application for emergency rental assistance is pending. An appeal adds its own bond steps (§5). The Housing Court runs its own standing orders, so check its schedule too.

## Where does a Massachusetts landlord file, and what does it cost?

A Massachusetts landlord files in the court for the place where the property sits. c. 239 §2 allows the District Court for that judicial district, or the Superior Court in some money cases. The Housing Court also hears summary process cases. Its fee page lists "summary process" fees.

The Housing Court charges $135 to enter an action: $120 plus a $15 surcharge (Mass.gov, Housing Court filing fees). You also pay a sheriff or constable to serve papers. Under Standing Order 10-20, the District Court also requires service of the conference notice by constable or sheriff at least 14 days before the event.

## Can a Massachusetts landlord evict for a lease violation or with no cause?

Yes. c. 239 §1 lets a landlord recover possession when a tenant holds on after the lease ends by its own terms, by notice to quit "or otherwise". The statutes we read set no fixed notice length for a lease violation. Your lease and your notice decide it, so have a local attorney draft it.

For no cause, the tenancy must be at will. §12 and §13 require written notice equal to the rent interval or 30 days, whichever is longer. Then the tenant has extra protections:

- **Stay of up to 6 months:** in a no-fault case, the court may delay judgment and execution (c. 239 §9).
- **Stay of up to 12 months:** if the household includes a person with a disability or a person aged 60 or older.
- **Counterclaims:** in a nonpayment or no-fault case, the tenant may raise code violations and other claims (c. 239 §8A).
- **Reprisal presumption:** a notice within 6 months of a code complaint is presumed retaliatory (c. 186 §18).

## Who removes the tenant after a judgment in Massachusetts?

An officer removes the tenant, not the landlord. c. 239 §3 requires the officer to give the tenant written notice at least 48 hours before the move-out. The notice names the date and time, the court and the docket number, and the licensed warehouse for any property left behind.

The officer may not carry out the execution before 9 a.m. or after 5 p.m., or on a Saturday, Sunday or legal holiday. If the tenant pays the full money judgment and the use and occupancy since judgment, you may not levy the execution. The tenant then counts as a lawful tenant (§3). You do not have to accept that payment. If you refuse it, you can still enforce the judgment in any lawful way.

## Is a self-help eviction legal in Massachusetts?

No. M.G.L. c. 186 §14 makes it a crime to regain possession "by force without benefit of judicial process". The same section covers a willful failure to supply required heat, hot water, water, light, power or gas. It also covers any interference with the tenant's quiet enjoyment.

The penalty is a fine of $25 to $300 or up to 6 months in jail. The tenant can also recover actual and consequential damages or 3 months' rent, whichever is greater, plus costs and attorney's fees. Lease waivers of §14 are void.
