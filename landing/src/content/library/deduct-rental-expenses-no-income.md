## When is a home held for rent?

A home stays held for rent when you continue a real effort to offer it to tenants. IRS Publication 527 allows eligible vacancy expenses for property held for rental purposes. An empty building alone does not prove that purpose.

Keep the listing, rental terms, inquiry log, and showing notes. Save each version when you change the price. These records explain what you offered and when someone could move in. A written timeline helps your tax preparer separate rental use from personal use.

Do not confuse an empty rental with a second home. Family visits, personal storage, and blocked dates can change the facts. Tell your preparer about those uses rather than hiding them in a vacancy category.

Use a reasonable market rent estimate and keep the homes you used for comparison. A price set so high that nobody could rent deserves a closer review. You need evidence of an actual rental plan, not just a label in your spreadsheet.

The practical test is simple: could an approved tenant have rented the home on the terms you advertised? Explain any period when the answer was no.

## What happens before the first tenant?

The first tenant does not set every tax start date. IRS Publication 527 ties depreciation to when a rental becomes ready and available for rent. The date you buy a house can differ from that date.

Separate the purchase, repair period, listing date, and move-in date. Save dated photos when the home becomes ready. Keep proof that utilities, access, and essential systems worked. Give your preparer the full timeline before you classify the costs.

A contractor invoice needs more detail than a broad description such as work on house. Ask what the contractor repaired or replaced. Separate work that keeps an existing item working from work that changes the property.

Do not assume every pre-tenant payment belongs in the current expense column. Purchase costs, improvements, financing costs, and regular operating costs need separate review. A payment date alone cannot settle the category.

Use a folder for each project. Include the quote, approval, invoice, proof of payment, and completion photos. This takes less effort than rebuilding the facts when the tax return is due.

## How long can a home sit empty?

Vacancy length alone does not decide whether a rental expense qualifies. IRS Publication 527 focuses on the property's rental purpose and availability. It does not give an unlimited permission to deduct all costs of any empty house.

Review a long vacancy as an operating problem as well as a tax question. Check the asking rent, property condition, access, and response time. Record what you changed and why. Keep a note when major work prevents occupancy.

If you offer the home for sale, tell your preparer whether it also remains available for rent. The IRS distinguishes those situations in Publication 527. Do not carry a rental label forward after your plan changes.

Also keep lost rent separate from actual bills. A month without a tenant creates a cash shortfall. It does not create a contractor invoice or a rent receipt. The IRS does not allow a vacancy deduction for rent you never received.

Build a cash budget that works before any tax benefit. A potential deduction cannot pay the mortgage today. Use the [management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) when outside help forms part of your vacancy plan.

## Where does the IRS explain the rules?

IRS Publication 527 explains rental use, vacancy costs, depreciation, and the difference between repairs and improvements. IRS Publication 925 explains passive activity and at-risk limits. Read those rules together when the property shows a loss.

An expense can qualify as a rental cost while a loss limit delays the tax benefit. Do not promise yourself a refund based only on the expense total. Ask your preparer to show any amount carried into a later year.

Use this review sheet before the appointment:

| Record | Question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| Listing history | Was the home offered for rent? |
| Ready-date photos | When could a tenant move in? |
| Repair invoices | What work did you pay for? |
| Personal-use log | Did you or family use the house? |
| Depreciation schedule | What costs remain to recover? |
| Prior tax returns | Are there suspended rental losses? |

Give your preparer the facts in order. Flag periods when you paused advertising or used the house yourself. Ask for a clear explanation of the treatment instead of choosing a category to reach a desired result.

## What should you check before filing?

Check that the books describe actual cash payments and actual property use. Match each payment to its invoice. Remove duplicate charges and separate owner transfers from income.

Then review the rental timeline against your calendar. A listing screenshot helps, but it does not explain a long repair closure. Add a short note for each gap. Keep the note with the bills so the explanation stays attached to the evidence.

Ask the preparer which costs enter the current return, which enter basis, and which require more facts. Ask how a later return to personal use would change the records. Save the answer with the final return.

Choose a record system you can maintain during the year. A plain folder and clear ledger can work. The goal is a reliable record of what happened, why you paid, and when the property could earn rent.
