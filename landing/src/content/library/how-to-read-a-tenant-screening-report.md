## What is in a tenant screening report?

A tenant screening report is a consumer report about one applicant. It usually has five parts. Read them in the order of the table, because the first step can make the rest useless.

| Section | What it shows | Weigh it |
|---|---|---|
| Identity match | Name, date of birth, past addresses, SSN check | First. A weak match can attach another person's records |
| Rent-related debt | Money owed to past landlords, often in collections | High. It relates directly to rent |
| Eviction records | Filings, judgments, dismissals | High, if a judgment and recent |
| Credit file | Open accounts, late payments, collections, charge-offs, bankruptcies | Medium. Look at patterns and dates |
| Criminal records | Convictions and sometimes charges | Only as local law allows |
| Score | A tenant score or a credit score | A summary, not a reason by itself |

The Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) sets the rules for the company that makes the report. 15 U.S.C. 1681e(b) says the company must "follow reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy." That is a duty on the agency. It does not mean every line is right.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## How do I check the identity match first?

Check that each record belongs to your applicant before you read anything else. Compare the full name, the date of birth and past addresses on the report with the application and the ID.

Watch for these signs of a mismatch:

- **Name only.** A record matched on name, with no date of birth or a different one.
- **Wrong place.** A record in a state where the applicant never lived.
- **Odd dates.** A record from a year when the applicant was a child.

The CFPB issued an advisory opinion on "name-only matching" in 2021. It withdrew that opinion, along with a 2024 opinion on background screening, in a Federal Register notice on 2025-05-12. The accuracy duty in the statute still applies. If a record looks wrong, ask the applicant and the screening company before you decide.

## What is in a credit report for renters?

A credit report for renters shows accounts, payment history and negative items. Focus on items that predict rent payment.

Use these questions as you read:

1. **Is there debt to a landlord or utility?** That is the strongest warning.
2. **Are late payments recent or old?** One bad year 5 years ago is different from late payments last month.
3. **Is the debt growing or shrinking?** Paid collections show effort.
4. **Is there a recent bankruptcy?** Note the date and the type.

The FCRA limits how old items can be. Under 15 U.S.C. 1681c(a), an agency generally may not report:

| Item | Maximum age on the report |
|---|---|
| Bankruptcy cases | 10 years |
| Civil suits, civil judgments, arrest records | 7 years, or the statute of limitations if longer |
| Paid tax liens | 7 years from payment |
| Collections and charge-offs | 7 years |
| Other adverse items, except convictions | 7 years |

Criminal convictions are not under the 7-year limit. Some exemptions in 1681c(b) apply to large credit, insurance and high-salary jobs, not to a normal rental.

## What do collections and charge-offs tell me?

A collection means the original creditor sent the debt to a collector. A charge-off means the creditor wrote the debt off as a loss. Both show a debt that was not paid as agreed.

Read three facts for each one: the creditor type, the amount and the date. A $90 medical bill from 6 years ago tells you little about rent. A $2,400 debt to a past landlord from last year tells you a lot. Write in your criteria which collections count, so you treat every applicant the same.

## How do I read an eviction record?

Read the outcome, not just the filing. An eviction filing only means a landlord started a case.

| Record shows | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Judgment for landlord | The court ruled for the landlord | Weigh it; check the date and the amount |
| Dismissed | The case ended without a ruling for the landlord | Usually do not count it |
| Settled or "stipulation" | The parties agreed | Ask for details |
| Filing only, no outcome | Unknown | Ask the applicant; do not assume |

Some states limit the use of these records. The New York Attorney General says a landlord cannot deny an applicant because of a past legal conflict with a landlord. If you reject an applicant after a report that shows prior landlord-tenant cases, the law assumes the cases were the reason.

## What is a resident score?

A resident score is a number a screening company makes for rentals. TransUnion's ResidentScore runs from 350 to 850, with 850 the best. RentRedi's help page lists these bands:

| ResidentScore | Band | Meaning (per RentRedi) |
|---|---|---|
| No score | N/A | Not enough data |
| 350–523 | Decline | High risk of missed rent or eviction |
| 524–537 | Conditional | Moderate to high risk |
| 538–559 | Low Accept | Moderate risk |
| 560–850 | Accept | Low risk |

A score is a summary. If you deny or set a condition because of it, the FCRA adverse action rules in 15 U.S.C. 1681m apply. You must disclose the score in writing or electronically.

## Which items should not count against an applicant?

Some items should not count, by law or by good practice. Leave these out of the decision:

- [ ] Records that may belong to another person.
- [ ] Arrests without conviction. Cook County bars their use.
- [ ] Items older than the FCRA limits above.
- [ ] Past landlord-tenant cases where state law protects them, as in New York.
- [ ] Credit history for a voucher holder in California who offers other proof of ability to pay, under Government Code 12955(o).
- [ ] Dismissed eviction cases.
