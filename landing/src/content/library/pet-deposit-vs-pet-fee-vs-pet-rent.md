## What is the difference between a pet deposit, a pet fee and pet rent?

A pet deposit, a pet fee and pet rent differ in 2 ways: whether the money comes back, and when the tenant pays it. Here is the comparison.

| | Pet deposit | Pet fee | Pet rent |
|---|---|---|---|
| When paid | Once, at move-in | Once, at move-in | Every month |
| Refundable | Yes, less lawful deductions | No | No |
| What it covers | Pet damage beyond wear and tear | Your cost to approve and prepare for a pet | Ongoing wear from the pet |
| Counts toward a deposit cap | Yes, in the capped states below | Depends on the state | No, it is a monthly charge, not a deposit |
| 2025 US average (RentCafe) | Slightly over $300 | $315 | $35.65 per month |
| Lease must say | Amount, that it is refundable | Amount and purpose, that it is nonrefundable | Amount and due date |

The averages come from RentCafe's 2025 analysis of listings on RentCafe.com, last updated October 2025. RentCafe also says about 80% of the properties listed for rent on its site allow at least one pet.

## How much is a typical pet deposit, fee and rent?

The typical amounts in 2025 were about $36 a month in pet rent and about $300 in a one-time charge. RentCafe reports average pet rent of $35.65, with an average pet fee of $315 and an average refundable pet deposit of "slightly over $300".

These are listing averages, not a rule. Your local market, the pet's size and your own costs should set your price. Over a 12-month lease, the 3 charges add up to very different totals for the tenant:

| Option | Math | Tenant pays in year 1 | Comes back to tenant |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deposit only | about $300 | about $300 | Yes, less damage |
| Fee only | $315 | $315 | No |
| Pet rent only | 12 × $35.65 | $427.80 | No |
| Deposit + pet rent | $300 + $427.80 | about $727.80 | $300 part, less damage |

Pet rent earns the most over time. A deposit protects you the most against one large damage bill, because you hold the cash at move-out.

## Which ones are refundable?

Only the pet deposit is refundable. A pet fee and pet rent are not, but some states require you to say so in writing.

Arizona is a clear example. A.R.S. 33-1321(B) says the landlord must state the purpose of all nonrefundable fees or deposits in writing. "Any fee or deposit not designated as nonrefundable is refundable." So in Arizona, a "pet fee" that the lease does not mark as nonrefundable must come back to the tenant.

The pet deposit follows your state's deposit return rules:

- **Arizona:** itemized list of deductions within 14 days, excluding weekends and holidays (A.R.S. 33-1321(D)).
- **Florida:** return within 15 days, or send written notice of a claim within 30 days (Fla. Stat. 83.49(3)(a)).
- **New York:** itemized statement and return within 14 days after the tenant leaves (GOL 7-108).

## Can I charge all three?

You can charge all three only where state law allows each one. Two kinds of rules get in the way: deposit caps and limits on upfront fees.

| State | Deposit cap | Upfront fee rule | Effect on pet charges |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arizona | 1.5 months' rent, "however denominated" | Nonrefundable fees must be named in writing | Pet deposit counts toward the 1.5-month cap |
| New York | One month's rent for most units | Most fees at the start of a tenancy are barred; screening fee up to $20 | A pet deposit fits inside the 1-month cap; a nonrefundable pet fee is a legal risk |
| Florida | No cap in 83.49 | No fee rule in 83.49 | Deposit rules in 83.49 apply to the pet deposit |

Worked check for Arizona: on $1,600 rent, the cap is 1.5 × $1,600 = $2,400. If you take a $2,000 security deposit, the most you can add as a pet deposit is $400.

Cities and counties can add their own rules. Check local law before you set your pet charges.

## What about service animals and emotional support animals?

An assistance animal is not a pet, so pet deposits, pet fees and pet rent do not apply to it. HUD's Fair Housing rule at 24 CFR 100.204 requires reasonable accommodations in rules such as a no-pets policy.

The HUD and Justice Department joint statement on reasonable accommodations is direct. Housing providers "may not require persons with disabilities to pay extra fees or deposits as a condition of receiving a reasonable accommodation." Its example: a provider may not charge a fee or security deposit to allow an assistance animal.

The same statement allows one charge. If the animal causes damage, you may charge the repair cost, or deduct it from the standard deposit all tenants pay. That holds only if you charge all tenants for damage they cause.

## Which option should you pick?

Pick the mix that matches your real risk and your state law. Here is a simple guide:

1. **Check the cap first.** Add the pet deposit to the security deposit and compare to your state cap.
2. **Use a deposit for damage risk.** It is the only charge you hold back at move-out.
3. **Use pet rent for wear over time.** Yards, carpets and floors wear faster with pets.
4. **Use a fee only where allowed.** Name its purpose in writing and mark it nonrefundable.
5. **Write one pet policy and apply it to everyone.** Exclude assistance animals from it.
