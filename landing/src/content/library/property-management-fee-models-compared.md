## How does a percentage fee compare to a flat monthly fee at different rents?

A percentage fee earns more than a flat fee on high-rent doors and less on low-rent doors. The cross-over point depends on your two numbers. With the market averages, it sits at about $1,190 of monthly rent.

The averages come from iPropertyManagement. In its sample of 722 branches (updated 2026-09-23), the average percent fee was 8.49% of collected rent. The average flat fee was $101.04 per unit per month. The flat fees ran from $49 to $250.

Here is what one occupied door pays you each month under each model:

| Monthly rent | 8.49% of rent | $101.04 flat | Hybrid: $50 + 4% | Which pays you more |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $900 | $76.41 | $101.04 | $86.00 | Flat |
| $1,190 | $101.03 | $101.04 | $97.60 | Same (percent vs flat) |
| $1,500 | $127.35 | $101.04 | $110.00 | Percent |
| $2,000 | $169.80 | $101.04 | $130.00 | Percent |
| $3,000 | $254.70 | $101.04 | $170.00 | Percent |

The hybrid column is an example we built, not a market average. Put your own rent and fees into the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to see the owner side.

**Watch for:** the percent base. BiggerPockets notes the gap between a fee on "rent due" and on "rent collected". A fee on rent collected pays you nothing on a month with no payment. A fee on rent due pays you even when the unit is vacant.

## What is a hybrid fee model?

A hybrid fee model charges a fixed base per door plus a smaller percent of collected rent. The base pays for work that does not change with rent: owner statements, inspections, tenant calls and accounting. The percent ties part of your pay to rent you collect.

Hybrid models solve one problem of each pure model:

- **Low-rent doors.** A pure percent fee on a $900 door pays $76.41 at the average rate. The work on that door is close to the work on a $2,000 door. A base amount keeps a floor.
- **High-rent doors.** A pure flat fee on a $3,000 door pays $101.04, while the average percent fee pays $254.70. A small percent keeps some of that upside.

A tiered model is a different idea. Tiers sell 2 or 3 service packages at different prices, for example basic, standard and full. Each tier can use a percent, flat or hybrid fee inside it. Tiers change what the owner buys. The fee model changes how you count the price.

## Which fee model do owners prefer?

We found no reliable public survey of owner preference between fee models. So we do not give a number. What the data shows is what managers offer, and that is a weak signal of what owners accept.

In the iPropertyManagement sample, 75.3% of companies charged a share of unit rent. That makes percent fees the default that owners see when they compare quotes. BiggerPockets says flat fees are often preferred by owners with higher rents or many properties, because the cost stays the same each month. That matches the math in the table above.

Our view: owners compare the headline number first. A flat fee is easy to compare. A percent fee needs the rent to make sense. If you sell to owners of high-rent single-family homes, a flat or hybrid fee can win the quote. If you sell to owners of lower-rent doors, a percent fee often looks cheaper to them.

## Which fee model gives the most predictable revenue?

A flat fee per door gives the most predictable revenue. Your monthly revenue is door count times the flat fee. A rent cut, a slow market or a concession does not change it.

| Model | Revenue moves with | Predictability | Risk to you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Percent of collected rent | Rent level and collections | Low to medium | Late payers and vacancies cut pay |
| Percent of rent due | Rent level | Medium | Owner pushback on vacant-month fees |
| Flat per door | Door count | High | Rent growth does not raise pay |
| Hybrid (base + percent) | Door count, then rent | Medium to high | More complex to explain |
| Tiered packages | Package mix | Medium | Owners pick the cheapest tier |

Flat fees have one cost: rent growth does not reach you. If rents in your market rise 4% a year, a percent fee rises with them. A flat fee needs a new agreement or a fee clause to rise. Put a yearly review clause in a flat-fee agreement.

## Do a la carte fees hide the real cost?

A la carte fees can hide the real cost when the headline rate is low. An owner who compares only the monthly rate will miss the leasing, renewal, setup and inspection fees.

The iPropertyManagement averages show how much these add:

- **Tenant placement** with management: 70.6% of one month's rent on average.
- **Lease renewal**, among flat-fee chargers: $231.77 on average.
- **Setup**: $185.24 per contract on average.
- **Inspection**: $106.72 each on average.

All Property Management lists more fees: eviction fees of about $200 to $500 plus court costs, and a share of tenant late fees.

Worked example. An owner pays 8% on a $1,500 rent ($120 a month, $1,440 a year). Add one new tenant at 70.6% of rent ($1,059) and one inspection ($106.72). The year costs $2,605.72, or 14.5% of the $18,000 rent. The headline said 8%.

**What to do:** show the effective rate, not only the headline rate. Put a full fee table on your site and in the agreement. An honest total cost makes your quote easier to trust when an owner compares 3 managers.

## How to pick a fee model for your company

Use this checklist to pick a model:

1. **List your doors by rent.** Find the median rent of your portfolio.
2. **Compute your cross-over rent.** Flat fee divided by percent rate.
3. **Compare the median to the cross-over.** Median above it: a flat or hybrid fee can win quotes. Median below it: a percent fee often looks cheaper to owners.
4. **Price your cost per door first.** A fee that does not cover the work on a low-rent door loses money in any model.
5. **Write the base in the agreement.** State "collected rent" or "rent due", and list which charges count.
6. **Review once a year.** Check the fee against rent growth and your costs.
