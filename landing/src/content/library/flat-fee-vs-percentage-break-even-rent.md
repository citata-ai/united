## How do I calculate the break-even rent?

Break-even rent for equal fee revenue is the flat monthly fee divided by the percentage rate. Use the percentage as a decimal. Compare the same billable rent base and service period.

For an invented example, use a $150 flat fee and an 8% rate. The calculation is $150 divided by 0.08, or $1,875. These are planning inputs, not published market prices.

| Monthly billable rent, example | Flat fee, example | Percentage fee at 8%, example | Higher manager revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1,000 | $150 | $80 | Flat fee |
| $1,500 | $150 | $120 | Flat fee |
| $1,875 | $150 | $150 | Equal |
| $2,500 | $150 | $200 | Percentage |
| $3,000 | $150 | $240 | Percentage |

The table holds all other terms constant. It excludes leasing fees, renewal charges, minimum fees and extra services. A real contract can change the answer.

Buildium's owner distribution guide explains that management fees can use a percentage of collected rent or a flat monthly amount. Read the agreement to identify the actual base. A rate on collected rent differs from a rate on scheduled rent.

Do not call this the company's profit break-even point. Equal fee revenue says nothing about the cost of providing service. Use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to inspect your inputs, then add the direct cost of each service plan.

## Which owners might prefer a flat fee?

A flat fee may appeal to owners who want a predictable management charge. That is a possible preference to test, not a claim that all owners choose the same model.

Show the total expected service cost rather than the monthly headline alone. A low flat fee can sit beside separate charges for work another plan includes. Owners need the scope before they can compare value.

Ask owners how they want to budget for vacant periods, lease renewals and repair coordination. Explain whether the fee continues when no rent arrives. Do not let the word flat imply that every charge is fixed.

Use a comparison sheet with the same services in each column. Include recurring management, leasing, renewals, inspection work and approved extras. Mark services that require a separate quote instead of filling them with assumed prices.

Check which owner questions recur during sales calls. Some owners may value a predictable charge, while others may prefer a fee tied to collected rent. Record the stated reason instead of assigning a preference based on rent alone.

Avoid a promise that flat fees always save money. The result depends on rent, included work and contract terms. Give the owner a calculation based on the actual property and proposed agreement.

## How does rent growth change flat fee revenue?

A fixed flat fee stays unchanged when rent rises unless the agreement allows a fee change. A percentage fee rises with its defined rent base, provided the rate and other terms stay constant.

For an invented example, rent rises from $2,000 to $2,100. At 8%, the fee rises from $160 to $168. A $150 flat fee remains $150 unless an agreed adjustment applies.

That difference is revenue, not added profit. Service costs can also change. Staff pay, vendor coordination and software expenses may rise without any change in rent.

Model the fee over the intended contract term. State the rent growth assumption and test a case with no growth. Do not treat a forecast as a promised increase in rent or income.

If you propose an annual flat fee review, explain the process before signing. State how the review works and how the owner receives notice of an agreed change. Have counsel check the contract terms for your state.

Consider what happens when collections fall. A percentage of collected rent can decline even if scheduled rent stays the same. A flat fee may remain due under its terms, but collecting that fee can still require work. Revenue in a model does not guarantee cash in the bank.

## Can I mix flat and percentage fees?

You can plan a mixed portfolio, but each account needs clear terms and correct billing controls. Have advisers check the agreements and any local restrictions.

Store the fee model, rate, minimum, rent base and effective date in the account record. Do not rely on staff memory. Keep the signed schedule close to the billing setup so a reviewer can check both.

Test billing for a normal month, a vacant month and a month with a returned payment. Include a rent change and an owner exit. These cases reveal differences that a full-collection example hides.

Review the margin of each model separately. A blended average may make a loss-making group look acceptable. Split the review by service scope as well as fee type.

Create a clear change process for owners moving between plans. Record the effective date and approvals. Prevent both plans from billing the same period or leaving a gap.

This is general information, not legal advice. Check the current law or ask a local attorney.

## How should I choose a model for new business?

Choose a fee model after you estimate the work and test the total price with real owner questions. The lowest headline fee may not fund the service you promise.

List the tasks each plan includes and estimate their direct cost from your own records. Include staff time spent explaining statements, handling exceptions and following up on funding. Keep setup work separate from recurring work.

Run a low-rent and high-rent example using the same scope. Then test vacancy and late collection. Mark every assumed input so the reader can change it.

Buildium's distribution guidance also highlights reserves and unpaid bills when calculating owner payouts. Explain that these amounts affect cash to the owner without becoming your management fee.
