New York sets notice rules by how long the tenant has lived in the unit. The key law is Real Property Law (RPL) 226-c. Here are the rules from the statute text on nysenate.gov, read on 2026-10-19.

| Rule | Limit or deadline | Statute |
|---|---|---|
| Notice of increase of 5% or more, or non-renewal: under 1 year | 30 days | RPL 226-c(2) |
| Same notice: 1 to 2 years | 60 days | RPL 226-c(2) |
| Same notice: 2 years or more | 90 days | RPL 226-c(2) |
| Late notice | Old terms continue until the period ends | RPL 226-c |
| Good Cause notice with the 226-c notice | Attach the RPL 231-c form | RPL 226-c(1)(a) |
| End a month-to-month tenancy in New York City | RPL 226-c notice period | RPL 232-a |
| Good Cause local rent standard | Lower of 10% or 5% + CPI change | RPL 211 |
| Notice for reasonable lease changes at renewal (Good Cause) | 30 to 90 days, written | RPL 216(1)(j) |
| Late fee | Lower of $50 or 5% of monthly rent, after 5 days | RPL 238-a |
| Background and credit check fee | Lower of actual cost or $20 | RPL 238-a |

## Does New York have a cap on rent increases or rent control?

New York has no single rent cap for every unit. Three sets of rules can limit an increase.

1. **Good Cause Eviction Law.** RPL Article 6-A covers many market-rate units in New York City. In a covered unit, an increase in a calendar year above the "local rent standard" is presumed unreasonable (RPL 216). RPL 211 sets that standard as "the inflation index or ten percent, whichever is lower." The inflation index is 5% plus the yearly change in the consumer price index (CPI). The landlord can rebut the presumption, for example with cost or tax increases.
2. **Rent stabilization.** The New York City rent stabilization law of 1969 and the Emergency Tenant Protection Act of 1974 (ETPA) set their own rent rules. GOL 7-107 refers to both laws.
3. **Rent control.** Some units fall under the city rent and rehabilitation law or the emergency housing rent control law.

Good Cause has 15 exemptions in RPL 214. One is a small landlord with no more than 10 units in the state. Another is an owner-occupied building with 10 or fewer units. A third is rent above 245% of fair market rent. If your unit is exempt and not rent-regulated, the statutes we read set no percentage cap. The notice rules below still apply.

Here is a worked example of the local rent standard. Say the CPI change is 3.5%. The inflation index is 5% + 3.5% = 8.5%. That is lower than 10%, so the standard is 8.5%. On a $2,000 rent, an increase above $170 is presumed unreasonable. This CPI figure is a sample, not the real number. Check the current CPI change before you set an increase.

## How many days notice does a New York landlord need to raise the rent?

A New York landlord needs 30, 60 or 90 days' written notice for an increase of 5% or more. RPL 226-c ties the days to the tenant's time in the unit:

| Tenant's occupancy or lease term | Notice |
|---|---|
| Less than 1 year, and no lease of at least 1 year | 30 days |
| More than 1 year but less than 2 years, or a lease of 1 to 2 years | 60 days |
| More than 2 years, or a lease of at least 2 years | 90 days |

The longer of total occupancy or lease term decides the notice. Since 2024-08-18, the notice must also include or attach the RPL 231-c Good Cause form. That form says if the unit is covered, which exemption applies, and why an increase is above the standard.

If you give late notice, the tenancy continues under the old terms until the full notice period runs out. So late notice delays the new rent. An increase below 5% is outside the RPL 226-c notice rule. In a Good Cause unit, RPL 216(1)(j) still expects written notice 30 to 90 days before the lease ends.

## Can a New York landlord raise the rent during a fixed-term lease?

A New York landlord's notice rules apply at renewal, not in the middle of a lease. RPL 226-c speaks of a landlord who "intends to renew" with an increase, or who does not intend to renew. The statutes we read give a landlord no right to change the rent mid-term. The lease sets the rent until it ends. Read your lease for any rent change clause, and ask an attorney before you use one.

## How much notice is needed to end a month-to-month tenancy in New York?

In New York City, a landlord gives the RPL 226-c notice to end a month-to-month tenancy. RPL 232-a says a monthly tenant cannot be removed for holding over unless the landlord serves notice "pursuant to the notice period required by subdivision two of section two hundred twenty-six-c." So the notice is 30, 60 or 90 days, by length of tenancy.

The notice must say that the landlord elects to end the tenancy. It must also say that the landlord will start summary proceedings if the tenant does not leave. You serve it the same way as a notice of petition.

Outside New York City, RPL 232-b covers notice by the tenant, or by a landlord in a non-residential tenancy. The tenant gives at least 1 month's notice. A residential landlord there uses the RPL 226-c non-renewal notice.

In a Good Cause unit, notice alone is not enough. The landlord also needs a good cause ground from RPL 216.

## Do cities in New York add their own rent or notice rules?

Yes, cities, towns and villages in New York can add their own rules in two ways.

- **Good Cause opt-in.** Outside New York City, Good Cause applies only where a town, village or city adopts it by local law (RPL 213). The local law may set its own small-landlord unit count. It may also set its own fair market rent percentage for the high-rent exemption. The Division of Housing and Community Renewal (DHCR) lists the places that opt in.
- **ETPA.** ETPA section 3 lets a city, town or village declare a housing emergency when the vacancy rate is "not in excess of five percent." Rent stabilization can then apply to a class of housing there.

Before you send a notice, check three things: Is the unit rent-regulated? Is it in a Good Cause place? Is it exempt? Then pick the 30, 60 or 90-day notice and attach the RPL 231-c form.
