## What lines appear on an owner statement?

An owner statement usually shows the property's cash activity and the amount available for payment to you. Formats differ, so ask the manager to define each heading. Start with the reporting period and the property name.

Read opening cash, receipts, expenses, owner payments, and closing cash in that order. Then find amounts held for reserves or pending bills. Ask whether tenant deposits appear in a separate report. Do not add them to money you expect to receive.

This is an assumed cash example, not a manager's statement:

| Cash movement | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Opening owner cash | $500 |
| Rent received | $2,000 |
| Repair payment | -$300 |
| Management fee | -$200 |
| Owner payout | -$1,500 |
| Closing owner cash | $500 |

The arithmetic closes because receipts less payments leave the stated balance. It does not prove that every charge is valid. You still need the lease, agreement, invoice, and payment record.

Ask whether the report shows cash paid or costs charged during the period. A bill can appear before payment in some systems. Write down the report basis so later comparisons use the same terms.

## How do you check fees against the contract?

A fee is correct only when its calculation matches the signed service terms. Find the fee clause before you compare the amount. Check the rate, charge base, timing, and any minimum.

A percentage of rent collected differs from a percentage of rent due. Ask how refunds, concessions, late payments, and tenant charges affect the calculation. Do not assume every receipt uses the same rate.

Separate recurring management from leasing, renewal, inspection, and repair coordination fees. Match a one-time charge to the event that triggers it. Ask for the relevant contract clause when a label is unclear.

For repairs, match the owner charge to the vendor bill. Ask whether a markup sits within the charge or on a separate line. Check that a later refund or warranty credit returns to the property ledger.

Use the [management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator) to test the fee structure outside the statement. Enter assumptions that match the contract. A useful comparison includes one-time events as well as the ordinary month. It should help you understand costs rather than replace an invoice review.

## How does an owner statement differ from a P&L?

An owner statement follows cash held and transferred, while a profit-and-loss report groups income and expenses. Ask which accounting basis each report uses. The labels can look similar while the totals answer different questions.

A payment to you reduces cash held by the manager. It does not create a new property operating cost. An owner contribution increases available cash but does not represent tenant rent.

Debt also needs care. A full mortgage payment affects your cash budget, but principal and interest need separate treatment in your books. The IRS's rental income guidance lists interest among common rental expenses. Do not classify the full payment as interest.

Your manager may not pay every property bill. Insurance, taxes, loan payments, or projects paid directly by you may sit outside the statement. Add those records before you assess the property's result.

Compare the statement to a complete property ledger. Mark which accounts the manager controls and which you control. This avoids calling a large payout profit when other bills remain unpaid. It also avoids counting the same bill twice when both you and the manager record it.

## Which numbers do you need at tax time?

Tax preparation needs the full-year income and cost records, not only the total owner payouts. The IRS identifies Schedule E as a form used for rental real estate income and loss. Your preparer must decide what belongs on your return.

Request the annual ledger, income summary, vendor details, and copies of relevant information returns. Add costs you paid outside the manager's system. Include major projects and the prior depreciation schedule.

IRS Topic 414 distinguishes current repairs from improvements that generally require recovery through depreciation. Give the preparer the work scope. A short ledger label such as maintenance cannot make that decision.

Match annual totals to monthly reports and explain corrections. Keep a separate note for transfers, refunds, and charges posted to a later period. A corrected statement should identify what changed rather than overwrite the history without explanation.

Do not ask the manager to make the ledger equal a desired tax result. Ask for accurate transaction records. Then let your tax professional apply the rules to those facts and reconcile the return to the books.

## What should you ask when totals do not match?

Ask a focused question that names the property, period, line, and missing document. Attach the statement page and state the total you expected. Clear questions reduce repeated messages.

Check timing before you call the amount an error. Rent can arrive near the period cutoff. A vendor credit can post after the original bill. Ask the manager to show the transaction trail rather than provide only a verbal answer.

Keep a log of open items and the date each closes. Ask for a corrected statement when a real posting error changes your records. Save both versions with the explanation.

California DRE's hiring guide stresses reading the management agreement and checking the manager. Use that same care after hiring. Download reports while portal access works. Keep your own copy so a later change of manager does not break your financial history.

This is general information, not tax advice. Check the current law or ask a tax professional.
