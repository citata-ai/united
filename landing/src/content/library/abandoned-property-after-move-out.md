## What are the rules for a tenant's left-behind belongings?

The rules for a tenant's left-behind belongings come from state law, and they are strict. The 4 states below share one pattern. Decide the unit is abandoned, send a written notice, and store the items for a set period. Then sell or dispose of them. The numbers in each part differ by state.

Here are 4 states side by side:

| State | Days to claim after notice | Low-value rule | Statute |
|-------|----------------------------|----------------|---------|
| California | At least 15 days after personal delivery, 18 days after mailing | Under $700 total: landlord may keep or dispose | Civil Code 1983, 1988 |
| Florida | At least 10 days after personal delivery, 15 days after mailing | Under $500 total: landlord may keep or dispose | Fla. Stat. 715.104, 715.109 |
| Oregon | At least 5 days after personal delivery, 8 days after mailing (45 days for manufactured homes) | $1,000 or less for most items: landlord may throw away or give away, not keep | ORS 90.425 |
| Washington | Sale after 45 days from notice | $250 or less total: sale after 7 days, except personal papers, family pictures and keepsakes | RCW 59.18.310 |

Read your own state's statute before you touch anything. Some states also have city rules on top.

## How long must I store items?

Store the items at least until the claim date in your notice passes. In California, that date is at least 15 days after you hand over the notice. If you mail it, the minimum is 18 days (Civil Code 1983). Florida's minimum is 10 days after personal delivery or 15 days after mailing (Fla. Stat. 715.104).

Oregon adds a second clock. A tenant has 5 days after personal delivery or 8 days after mailing to contact you. After that contact, the tenant has 15 days to remove the items (ORS 90.425). For manufactured homes and floating homes, the periods are longer.

Washington sets a longer wait for most property: 45 days from mailing or delivery of the notice before a sale (RCW 59.18.310). Personal papers, family pictures and keepsakes always get the full 45 days.

Store the items in a safe place. You may charge reasonable storage costs where the statute allows it.

## What notice must I send?

Send a written notice that tells the former tenant what you hold, where it is, and the last day to claim it. Each state lists the content. Florida, for example, says to deliver the notice in person or by first-class mail to the last known address. If you have reason to think that address will not reach the tenant, also send it to any other known address (Fla. Stat. 715.104).

Washington's notice must include your name and address, where the property is stored, and the sale or disposal date. It must also state the tenant's right to get the property back (RCW 59.18.310).

A safe notice checklist, built from the 4 statutes above:

- [ ] Your name, address and phone
- [ ] A description of the items, clear enough for the owner to know them
- [ ] Where the items are stored
- [ ] The last date to claim, set by your state's minimum
- [ ] Storage costs the tenant must pay to claim, if allowed
- [ ] What happens after the date: sale or disposal
- [ ] Delivery method and date, with proof (mail receipt or a photo of the posted notice)

If your state's statute includes sample notice wording, use it.

## Can I throw away trash right away?

You can usually throw away true trash right away, but be careful about what counts as trash. In Oregon, the statute lets a landlord promptly dispose of rotting food. An Oregon court also held that abandoned property does not include trash or items with no value (ORS 90.425, case note Eddy v. Anderson).

Follow 3 rules on day 1:

1. **Photograph every room before you remove anything.** Wide shots, then close-ups.
2. **Remove only clear trash and spoiled food.** Old food, empty boxes, broken items with no resale value.
3. **Hold anything a person might want back.** Papers, photos, IDs, medicine, electronics, tools, furniture in usable shape.

When in doubt, store it. A bag of papers costs little to keep. A lawsuit over a thrown-out family album costs much more.

## What happens to items worth very little?

Items worth very little often follow a faster path. In California, unclaimed property worth less than $700 in total may be kept or disposed of in any way (Civil Code 1988). In Florida, the limit is $500 (Fla. Stat. 715.109).

Oregon lets a landlord destroy or give away most property worth $1,000 or less. The same applies when storage and sale costs would likely exceed the sale price. But the landlord may not keep it for personal use (ORS 90.425).

Above those limits, the items go to a public sale. In California and Florida, the sale must be advertised in a local newspaper. After you subtract storage, ad and sale costs, the rest goes to the county treasury within 30 days of the sale. The former tenant can claim it for 1 year.

Worked example (California): a tenant leaves a couch, a TV and 4 boxes. You estimate a resale value of $450. That is under $700, so after the claim date passes, you may keep or dispose of the items. If the estimate were $1,200, you would need a public sale with a published notice.

## What about vehicles?

Vehicles often follow separate rules, so check before you call a tow truck. Oregon includes vehicles and recreational vehicles in its abandoned property statute. It also lets a landlord use the towing process in ORS 98.830 for an abandoned motor vehicle instead (ORS 90.425). For recreational vehicles, Oregon requires copies of the notice to any lienholder (a lender with a claim on the vehicle).

In other states, a car in a tenant's parking spot may fall under private property towing law, not landlord-tenant law. Ask your local police or a tow company which rules apply.

## A 7-step process to follow

1. **Confirm the tenancy ended.** Keys returned, lease ended, or a court order executed.
2. **Photograph everything** before you move a single item.
3. **Remove only trash and spoiled food.**
4. **Inventory the rest** on a written list.
5. **Send the notice** your state requires, with proof of delivery.
6. **Store the items** until the claim date passes.
7. **Sell or dispose** under your state's value rule, and keep receipts.
