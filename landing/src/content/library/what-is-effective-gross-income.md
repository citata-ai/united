## What is effective gross income?

Effective gross income (EGI) is the income a rental property actually earns before operating expenses. It is the step between the rent a property could earn and the profit it does earn. Some sources call it gross effective income; the measure is the same.

Buildium's dictionary describes it as "the total income a property generates, adjusted for factors that can reduce the ideal income." It writes the formula as:

**Gross effective income = potential rent − vacancy loss − concessions − other adjustments**

Most investor and lender models also add other income, such as laundry or parking. The full version is in the next section.

EGI matters because owners, lenders and buyers value a property on what it earns, not on what the rent roll promises.

## How do you calculate EGI?

You calculate EGI in 4 steps:

1. **Start with gross potential rent (GPR).** Every unit at market rent for 12 months.
2. **Subtract vacancy loss.** Rent lost on empty units.
3. **Subtract concessions and credit loss.** Free rent, discounts and unpaid rent you wrote off.
4. **Add other income.** Money from the property that is not base rent.

**EGI = GPR − vacancy loss − concessions − credit loss + other income**

A worked example for a 12-unit building. Market rent is $1,500 per unit per month.

| Line | How | Annual amount |
|---|---|---|
| Gross potential rent | 12 units × $1,500 × 12 months | $216,000 |
| Vacancy loss | 5% of GPR | − $10,800 |
| Concessions | 4 new leases, $500 off each | − $2,000 |
| Credit loss (bad debt) | Unpaid rent written off | − $3,200 |
| Other income | Laundry, parking, pet rent, late fees | + $6,600 |
| Effective gross income | | $206,600 |

The math: $216,000 − $10,800 − $2,000 − $3,200 + $6,600 = $206,600.

The 5% vacancy loss is a sample input. For context, the U.S. Census Bureau rental vacancy rate was 7.3% in Q2 2026 (FRED series RRVRUSQ156N). Your local market and your building may differ a lot from the national number. Use your own 12-month history if you have it.

## What other income goes into EGI?

Other income is any regular income from the property that is not base rent. It is often small per unit, but it adds up across a building.

| Other income type | Example | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Laundry | Coin or card machines | Net of the vendor's share |
| Parking | Garage or reserved spaces | Monthly or per space |
| Pet rent | Monthly charge per pet | From the pet addendum |
| Storage | Lockers or storage units | Monthly |
| Late fees | Charged under the lease | Only fees actually collected |
| Application fees | Charged at application | Check local limits |
| Utility reimbursements | Tenant share of water, trash | Only if billed apart from rent |
| Kept deposits | Deposit amounts kept for damage | Only the part kept |

Kept deposits need care. IRS Publication 527 says not to include a security deposit in income when you receive it if you plan to return it. It becomes income only if you keep it. So count only kept amounts in other income.

Count other income the way you collect it. A late fee you billed but never collected is not income. Put it in credit loss instead.

## Gross potential rent vs EGI: what is the difference?

Gross potential rent is the most a property could earn from rent. EGI is what it really earns. The gap shows how well the property is leased and collected.

| Measure | What it assumes | What it includes |
|---|---|---|
| Gross potential rent | Every unit leased at market, every tenant pays | Base rent only |
| Gross scheduled rent | Every unit at its actual lease rent | Base rent only, at contract rates |
| Effective gross income | Real results | Rent collected, plus other income |

In the example, GPR is $216,000 and EGI is $206,600. EGI is 95.6% of GPR. If a manager cuts vacancy loss from 5% to 3%, vacancy loss drops by 2% × $216,000 = $4,320. EGI rises to $210,920.

That is why vacancy and collections matter more than small fee changes. A 2-point vacancy gain in this example is worth about 65% of all the other income ($4,320 ÷ $6,600).

## How does EGI lead to NOI?

EGI leads to NOI by subtracting operating expenses. NOI (net operating income) is the property's income after the costs to run it, but before debt and capital spending.

**NOI = EGI − operating expenses**

Continuing the 12-unit example:

| Line | Annual amount |
|---|---|
| Effective gross income | $206,600 |
| Property taxes | − $24,000 |
| Insurance | − $9,600 |
| Repairs and maintenance | − $14,400 |
| Utilities (owner-paid) | − $10,800 |
| Management fee (8% of collected income) | − $16,528 |
| Other operating expenses | − $4,200 |
| Total operating expenses | − $79,528 |
| Net operating income | $127,072 |

The math for the fee: $206,600 × 8% = $16,528. Total expenses: $24,000 + $9,600 + $14,400 + $10,800 + $16,528 + $4,200 = $79,528. NOI: $206,600 − $79,528 = $127,072.

Mortgage payments and capital expenditures, such as a new roof, are not operating expenses. They come after NOI.

Note how the fee works. Many management fees are a percent of collected income, so they rise and fall with EGI. To test a fee on your own numbers, use the [property management fee calculator](/tools/property-management-fee-calculator).

## Where does EGI appear in an owner report?

EGI appears near the top of an annual owner report or operating statement, after the income lines and before expenses. A clear report shows each step from potential rent to EGI, so the owner sees where income was lost.

Ask for these lines on each annual report:

- [ ] Gross potential rent
- [ ] Vacancy loss
- [ ] Concessions
- [ ] Credit loss (bad debt)
- [ ] Other income, by type
- [ ] Effective gross income
- [ ] Operating expenses, by category
- [ ] Net operating income

Many monthly owner statements show only rent collected and bills paid. That is a cash view. An annual operating statement with EGI and NOI is a performance view. Owners need both, most of all when they plan a sale or a refinance.
